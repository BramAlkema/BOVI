# The Quarrel Beneath Money

Dear Guy,

There is a strange similarity between the grand theories of money.

They disagree about almost everything. One says money is gold. Another says it is state authority. Another says it is debt. Another says it is code, or scarcity, or trust, or a technology for solving exchange problems. Some theories try to explain where money came from. Some try to explain what money is. Others want to redesign it.

Yet almost all of them share one conviction: the others have mistaken the real thing.

That conviction is not merely intellectual arrogance. It reveals something important about money itself.

Money theories repeatedly turn a partial truth into an essence. They take one layer of money—the material, the legal, the relational, the computational, the political, or the psychological—and treat it as the underlying object. The result is a kind of totalising partiality: each theory sees something real, but then mistakes its view of money for money itself.

Your argument in _Out of Thin Air_ offers a powerful way through this quarrel. Money is not fundamentally the thing made of gold, paper, shells, or code. It is a coordination equilibrium in shared expectations. I accept this unit because I expect you to accept it, and you accept it because you expect someone else to accept it. The material object is an interface to that equilibrium.

This does not make money imaginary. Expectations determine which monetary system wins; they do not determine the entire value of what that system enables. The worth of money lies in the cooperation, specialisation, settlement, and exchange that become possible once people can rely on a common medium. Expectation selects the system. It does not magically manufacture the fruits of coordination.

This distinction matters because it exposes a peculiar inconsistency in conventional thinking. Economics is perfectly comfortable with intersubjective explanations elsewhere. Preferences are relational. Expectations matter in bank runs and currency crises. Coordination games depend on what people believe other people believe. Financial markets are analysed as equilibria of expectations.

But when economics reaches money, it often switches vocabulary. Suddenly value is said to reside in an object. Gold “backs” money. A commodity possesses intrinsic monetary value. A token is valuable because a state declares it so. A fixed supply is assumed to create credibility. The discipline moves from social ontology to object-talk without explaining why.

The mistake is not simply that one side prefers gold and another prefers paper. The deeper mistake is that both sides are looking for the wrong kind of thing. They ask which substance money really is, when the more useful question is what social equilibrium the substance makes visible, transferable, enforceable, or legible.

This is why the distinction between commodity and token money is less fundamental than it first appears. Gold, silver, cowries, tally sticks, banknotes, and digital entries may have very different physical properties, but they can perform the same social role. They can serve as interfaces to a shared agreement about what settles a claim.

The historical evidence becomes clearer when viewed this way. A cowrie does not become a fundamentally different kind of money when a state begins taxing in cowries. The shell remains the same. What changes is the institutional environment around it. A state with sufficient capacity can read the monetary agreement, impose a unit against which obligations must be cleared, and redirect existing expectations toward fiscal purposes.

This suggests that state capacity—not the material substrate—is the crucial variable in the history of taxation. The state does not necessarily seize the stock of money. It changes what people expect everyone else to accept. That is a redirection of an equilibrium rather than a confiscation of a substance.

The same insight helps explain why some historical cases trouble simple origin stories. A commodity can circulate in a relational economy. A token can become the basis of bureaucratic extraction. Grain can be used as a provisioning good while silver serves as the unit of account. Cowries can circulate socially before becoming instruments of taxation. The substrate does not determine the entire monetary order.

What matters instead is how an economy makes claims common, readable, enforceable, and transferable.

This also explains why the rival theories find one another so frustrating. They are often not answering the same question.

The commodity theorist asks: what gives a monetary object durability, scarcity, or credibility?

The chartalist asks: who has the authority to define the unit and demand it in payment?

The credit theorist asks: what relation between people does the monetary entry record?

The historian asks: under what conditions did particular monetary systems emerge?

The designer asks: what should a monetary system make possible, and who should control it?

The coordination theorist asks: why does one medium become accepted rather than another?

Each question is legitimate. The problem begins when an answer to one question is presented as the answer to all of them.

A state can select a currency without creating the social value that makes a ledger useful. A commodity can be scarce without functioning as money. A debt can be socially meaningful without being denominated in a transferable monetary unit. A currency can be accepted without being fair. A monetary system can be technically efficient while being politically extractive.

The theories therefore disagree because money is not one problem. It is a bundle of problems that happen to be carried by the same institutions and often by the same instrument.

This is where money theory becomes philosophical. Money is an essentially contested social kind. It is not a neutral object waiting to be correctly named. How we define it determines which evidence counts, which historical events become central, and which reforms appear possible.

A theory of money is therefore never entirely outside money. It can justify institutions, organise expectations, legitimise authority, and shape the very system it claims merely to describe. Saying that money is a commodity supports one kind of monetary politics. Saying that it is state debt supports another. Saying that it is code, or a ledger, or a coordination equilibrium opens different possibilities.

The theory participates in the object.

That is why the recurring conviction that “the others do not understand money” is both understandable and dangerous. It is understandable because each theory really does expose a blind spot in the others. It is dangerous because the theorist can mistake the discovery of a blind spot for the discovery of the whole.

Your own argument has a particularly valuable safeguard against this danger. If money is a distributed expectation, then its nature is contingent by construction. It depends on what a population can commonly know, expect, enforce, and reproduce at a given time. This prevents the slide from “this monetary form existed” to “this monetary form was natural,” and from “this arrangement persisted” to “this arrangement was right.”

But the same caution should apply to the coordination-equilibrium account itself. Saying that money is expectation risks becoming another reduction if it is treated as the entire explanation. Expectations select and sustain a monetary system, but they operate through ledgers, institutions, technologies, legal rules, enforcement mechanisms, and unequal positions of power.

A fuller synthesis might therefore say:

> Money is a socially selected ledger, sustained by shared expectations, implemented through a material or digital interface, and made politically consequential by institutions that can read, enforce, and redirect it.

That formulation does not dissolve the differences between theories. It puts them in their proper places.

The commodity theorist studies the interface. The credit theorist studies the relation. The chartalist studies the authority. The historian studies the sequence. The coordination theorist studies the equilibrium. The political economist studies who controls the system and who bears its costs.

None of these is money alone.

The deepest lesson may be that money is not a thing that people discovered. It is an agreement that people continually reproduce—and an agreement that institutions continually try to capture.

That is why money remains so poorly understood. Its physical interfaces invite object-thinking. Its legal forms invite state-thinking. Its debts invite relation-thinking. Its prices invite market-thinking. Its history invites origin stories. But underneath all of these is a social achievement: the coordination of expectations across people who do not know one another and across transactions that do not happen at the same time.

The quarrel between monetary theories is therefore not a failure of intelligence. It is the predictable result of trying to describe a multi-layered social technology with a single essence.

The task is not to decide which theory has finally found the real money.

It is to ask, of each theory:

What does it see? What does it exclude? Which layer does it mistake for the whole? And whose interests are served when that partial view becomes the definition of reality?
