# The Theory of Money — a Recension

*A rewriting of Menger's money chapter on his own bones: keep the skeleton, strike one line.*

## Editorial principle

Almost everything in the 1871 chapter was already a **pull** account, and we keep it: the organic, unintended origin; differing saleability as the motor; the refusal to call money a measure of value; marginal utility throughout. Menger flinched **once** — he made the most saleable good a *commodity with prior consumption value*, a seed of objective worth beneath the subjective valuation. We strike that one line and let his own engine finish the run.

- **Keep:** emergent origin; saleability as the engine; money is *not* a measure of value; marginal utility everywhere.
- **Strike:** that the terminating use must be a *material commodity*. Replace it with: the terminating use is any **direct service subjectively valued for its own sake** — and the medium has one (it beats memory and settles conflict), so it needs no commodity beneath it.

---

## §1. The Nature and Origin of Money

Money was nowhere the invention of a ruler or the fruit of a compact. It is an institution no one designed and everyone built — the unintended deposit of innumerable men each pursuing his own advantage. So far the original account stands, and stands firmly.

But we must be exact about *what* advantage each man pursued, for the older statement reached for the wrong one. It is commonly said that some commodity, already valued for its consumption, came by degrees to be taken not for use but for re-trade, and so hardened into money. The premise smuggled in is that the medium must descend from a thing already eaten, worn, or wielded — that value, here as everywhere, must be **pushed up from below** by a substance.

It need not. What each man seeks in accepting the medium is not a substance he might one day consume, but the **reduction of his own future cost of transacting** — the sparing of the search for a double coincidence, of the fractional and the perishable trade, of the burden of remembering who owes whom. The value is not pushed up from the token's body; it is **pulled forward from the trades the token will let him make**. The medium is a thing whose usefulness lies ahead of it.

## §1a. The Answer to the Regression

Here is the objection the recension must answer head-on, for it is the strongest in the literature. Mises (1912) pressed it thus: money yields no direct satisfaction; its only utility is its exchange value; therefore the marginal utility of money cannot be grounded in any present use, and to escape a vicious circle one must regress backward through time to a moment when the money-good was valued as an ordinary **commodity**. The commodity origin, he held, is not a historical accident but a *logical necessity*.

The answer begins as a charge of inconsistency. It defeats the *letter* of the 1912 claim, though not — as §1b will concede — its strongest form.

**Watch where Mises lets the regress stop.** Gold's monetary value, he says, regresses to gold's *ornament* value. But he does **not** demand that the ornament value regress any further — to some deeper commodity beneath the adornment. He is perfectly content for the value of an ornament, or of a diamond, to be **groundless in the foundationalist sense**: suspended from present subjective marginal utility, anchored in nothing below it. Indeed the whole diamond–water paradox is solved precisely by *refusing* the regress — the diamond's worth is set at the margin, now, by direct subjective valuation, and no one asks what commodity lies beneath the diamond.

So the regress, even in Mises, terminates in **"a direct use, subjectively valued for its own sake."** That is the floor he everywhere accepts.

Then comes the quiet substitution. For money — and money alone — he narrows "direct use" to "**material commodity**." Why must the terminating use be a thing one can eat, wear, or hang upon a wall, rather than any service valued directly for itself? He gives no reason, and can give none, for his own diamond shows the terminus needs only to be *valued directly*, not *made of stuff*.

**The medium supplies exactly that terminus — without stuff.** The error in the original objection is a single false premise: that the medium has *no* direct use. It has two, and both are real in a world with no prices whatever:

1. **It beats memory.** A conserved, divisible, tamper-evident count offloads what the brain forgets and what the brain distorts in its own favour. Relief from the failures of memory — fading, dispute, partiality, the cap at Dunbar's number — is a direct good, valued for itself as surely as warmth or light. A man alone values a reliable tally of his stores.

2. **It settles conflict.** A record all parties trust ends the disputes that the elementary forms of fairness forever generate — who contributed, whose turn it is, what is owed, what is just. Settled conflict is a direct good, valued for itself as surely as peace. A people that never trades still forgets, and still quarrels over shares; the device that ends the quarrel is worth having before a single thing is priced.

Neither service is an *exchange* use; therefore neither is circular. They are direct uses, subjectively valued — exactly the terminus Mises permits for the diamond — carried on a substance worth nothing. The marginal utility of the medium is grounded on day one, in the present tense, by the friction its better-than-memory and conflict-settling service removes; the exchange value then accretes *on top of* that grounded direct value, precisely as gold's monetary value accreted on top of its ornament value. The circle never forms, because there is a non-exchange floor available from the first day, and it is not a commodity.

**The rope is the controlled experiment.** Gold deceived the whole tradition because it carried *both* values at once — ornament and ledger-service — so no one could tell which was load-bearing. Looking at gold, Mises saw the ornament and concluded the *substance* was the anchor. The tally rope **zeroes the substance**: its commodity worth is nil, only the service remains. If money still emerges on the rope — and it does — then the service was the active ingredient all along and the commodity was a confound, never a cause. Strip the substance and the value does not vanish; therefore the substance was never holding it up.

This is why the framework is **more Mengerian than Menger**: it does not abolish the regress, it relocates its terminus from a priced commodity to a directly-valued service, and so applies Menger's own diamond–water solution to the one good he mistook for special. But the relocation must survive a sterner form of the objection, to which we now turn.

## §1b. What this settles, and what it does not

Honesty about the strength of the foregoing. It defeats the *letter* of the 1912 claim: Mises wrote "commodity," and the diamond shows he cannot, *in principle*, object to a regress that halts at a directly-valued use rather than at a substance. The burden shifts — he must say why the terminating use must be *material*, and his own diamond denies him the answer.

But the strongest form of the objection, urged in the modern debates, does not say "commodity." It says **prior price**. What is to be explained is purchasing power, itself an exchange ratio; and (the objection runs) an exchange ratio can be anchored only by a *prior* exchange ratio. On this telling the terminus must be a good that already commanded a **price** in trade, whatever its substance. The diamond meets this test, because adornment makes men give goods to obtain a diamond — adornment generates a price. Does the medium's service do the same? Private relief of one's own memory plainly does not: that I value my own tally says nothing of what a tally fetches in trade. Here the simple diamond analogy breaks, and must be repaired.

Two routes answer the sterner objection.

**The first keeps the regress and meets the price test.** The conflict-settling service is not private; it is one a group gives **real goods** to secure — as men pay an arbitrator, a witness, or a trusted measurer. A device that ends the quarrel over shares commands payment in the very goods whose division it governs, and that payment is a genuine prior price — anchored in the *consumption* value of the goods given, terminating cleanly, with the device's own substance still worth nothing. The medium thus enters trade already bearing a ratio against goods, earned by a **service** and not by a substance; its superior saleability then makes that ratio focal and sticky until it is the numéraire. This satisfies the real requirement of the regression theorem — a prior, non-monetary price — while voiding its metallist conclusion. It is the conservative win: *more Mengerian than Menger*, not *anti-Menger*. (Its own soft spot, to be defended rather than assumed: that the conserved unit commands a price for the *service* and not merely the near-free substrate — the scarcity that bears the price is in the conserved count, not in the cost of weaving the rope.)

**The second rejects the requirement.** It denies that an exchange ratio needs a prior exchange ratio at all: value may bootstrap from common knowledge alone, a focal loop closing on itself — turtles all the way up, no terminus. The evidence is real (a token bootstrapped from negligible prior trade value, sustained by expectation of acceptance alone). But its honest status is **premise-rejection defended by evidence, not an internal knockout** — and it still owes the argument that *ordinal, relative* salience can seed the first ratio where a bare number could not. The likely discharge: a good with a direct use can be **bartered** like any good, acquiring relative ratios in the ordinary way, so the dimension to be focal on is supplied by barter and not conjured from nothing — at which point the second route quietly leans on the first.

The framework's settled position is the **first** route, with the second held as the deeper and more contested claim. Neither is a checkmate against Mises. Both retire the commodity — which is all the recension needs.

## §2. Saleability, Reconsidered

The engine of the original account — that goods differ in their degree of saleability (*Absatzfähigkeit*), and that the most saleable outruns the rest until it is taken by all — is retained without amendment. We correct only the reading of *what saleability is*.

The saleability of a good is the smallness of the loss a man suffers in turning it back into other goods at need. Stated so, saleability is simply **transaction cost avoided, measured from the holder's side**. The most saleable good is therefore not the one with the richest prior consumption, but the one that **dissolves the most friction** for the most people — and friction-dissolving feeds upon itself, for each new acceptor deepens the network that makes the next acceptance cheaper. Hence the pull toward a single medium is steepest where the friction relieved is greatest; one money tends to crowd out its rivals, the lesser media settling beneath it in a graduated order of saleability. There is no rung on this ladder at which a commodity must be standing — only the friction, and the common knowledge that others will relieve it too.

## §3. The Kinds of Money Appropriate to Particular Peoples and Periods

No one substance is money by nature, and different peoples in different ages have settled upon different media — cattle, metals, weighed silver, tallies, paper, entries in a book. The original account ascribed this variety to the differing commodities each people happened to value. We ascribe it to the differing **technologies of the reliable record** available to each: a people fixes upon whatever substrate most cheaply furnishes a *known and conserved supply, divisible and hard to counterfeit* — the conditions under which expectation can become common knowledge. Where that substrate is a metal, the older eye sees a commodity and is satisfied; where it is a knotted rope or a line in a register, the same eye grows uneasy and hunts for the hidden commodity beneath. There is none, and the rope is the more instructive case precisely because it offers nothing to consume.

## §4. That Money is Not a Measure of Value

Here the original chapter was ahead of its successors, and we only strengthen it. Money is no measure of value; there is no substance of value in the good for an instrument to gauge. A price is not a reading taken off a fixed scale but the **revealed margin** — the rate at which, here and now, men will give the one good for the other. Money does not measure this rate from outside; it is the good in which the rate is most conveniently expressed, being the most saleable. To ask what *backs* the measure is to mistake a running tally for a stored quantity. The measure stores nothing. It reports.

## §5. The Storing of Wealth, and the Carry-Cost Margin

A man holds the medium rather than goods or yielding claims because it spares him future friction; he holds it only up to the point where the cost of holding — the yield foregone, the slow erosion of its purchasing power, the charge of keeping it safe — just equals the friction the next unit held would spare him. Beyond that margin he would sooner hold the fruitful asset and bear the friction; short of it he would sooner hold the medium. The purchasing power of money is the variable that brings the whole stock to rest at this margin, given a known supply.

From this the old paradox dissolves a second time, and in the same manner Menger dissolved it for water and diamonds. The *aggregate* friction money relieves is an ocean — the whole multitude of trades that occur only because it exists; yet the *marginal* unit is bid down to the waterline where its convenience just covers its carry. Total worth immense, marginal worth at the margin. The medium pays its holder not in interest but in **friction removed**, and the inflation that erodes it is nothing but the issuer clipping that silent yield — and able to clip only so much as the friction the holder would otherwise suffer.

## §6. The Margin Made Visible — a Caution

It is tempting to offer the following as a crucial experiment against the commodity theory: alter the money supply surreptitiously and unequally, and observe that prices move and trades clear that would not have cleared before, the gain passing to those the new money reaches first. This *is* instructive — it shows value living at the margin rather than in any backing, and it shows the surreptitious, uneven injection to be the very channel of extraction (Cantillon's point).

But it must **not** be advanced as a refutation of the regression theorem, and honesty requires the caution. The non-neutrality of money under uneven injection is *common ground* — it is itself Austrian doctrine. Both the commodity theory and this one predict that prices move when the stock changes, for both hold that marginal utility against the existing stock sets purchasing power **once purchasing power exists**. The experiment operates entirely *downstream* of the disputed question, which is the **origin** of exchange value; it presupposes an already-valued money to perturb. It discriminates nothing about genesis.

The genuine crucial experiment lies at the origin, and it is the **rope** (§1a): the specimen in which commodity worth is zeroed and money emerges anyway.

## §7. Metallism's Real Claim: Governance, Not Value

The foregoing refutes the metallist's *value* theory and yet will not move a single metallist, and we must be honest about why. Metallism is a theory of **credible supply governance** wearing the costume of a theory of **value**. It fuses two questions that have nothing to do with each other:

- **Q1 — what gives money its value?** Forward acceptance under known supply (§1–§5). *Anchor type is irrelevant here.*
- **Q2 — what makes a supply rule un-betrayable?** A question of governance — and here **anchor type matters greatly.**

The commodity never did any real work on Q1. Everything the metallist actually cares about lives in **Q2**: not the worth of the stuff, but the refusal to trust a human being with the dial. *That concern is legitimate*, and the recension concedes it outright — **discretion is the betrayal vector**, exactly as §6 showed. We have been refuting an ontology while sharing the politics. To engage the metallist one must answer Q2, not Q1.

**"Trustless" is a myth; trust is relocated, not abolished.** Fiat's supply runs on **trust in politicians**; gold's on **trust in extraction rates**. Gold is not trustless — it merely moves the trust from a body that can dilute you *tonight, on purpose* to a process (geology, mining engineers) that **no single actor controls**. That is a genuine gain in *betrayability*, and it is the only thing gold ever bought — not value, but betrayal-resistance.

Graded on the two properties the framework actually requires, gold is **mediocre**:

| Anchor | Impersonal (no one can debase at will) | Symmetrically **known** (supply path forecastable) | **Elastic** (supply can answer a demand shock) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Legal** (fiat) | ✗ — a person holds the dial | ~ — only as good as the institution's credible commitment | ✓ — the dial is also a shock absorber |
| **Natural** (gold) | ✓ — geology, not a person | **✗** — extraction is a technology shock waiting to happen | ✗ — rigid; it transmitted the Great Depression |
| **Mathematical** (Bitcoin) | ✓ | ✓ — schedule public and fixed | **✗✗** — fixed by design; a deflation engine |

Gold passes "impersonal" and **fails "known."** Its supply is a noisy, technology-dependent process — the New World silver flood after Potosí and the price revolution; the California, Australian, and Witwatersrand rushes; the 1887 cyanide process that suddenly drew gold from low-grade ore. The metallist treats extraction as a constant of nature; history treats it as an innovation surface. Gold is *apolitical but unpredictable* — half of what "symmetrically known supply" demands.

**Bitcoin is what the metallist was reaching for.** It delivers the *impersonal* governance gold gave **and** the *knownness* gold never could — a mathematical anchor in place of a geological one. The metallist is a rules-man who **mistook the rock for the rule**; Bitcoin is the rule without the rock. (Scope it honestly: this concerns the *supply-governance* axis alone. On volatility-as-unit-of-account, or bearer-asset-without-counterparty, gold and Bitcoin trade different risks. But on the one axis metallism is actually about, Bitcoin dominates gold.)

**And the legal anchor can be bound too.** The enemy is *discretion*, not *the state*, and **any substrate can host either a rule or a discretion.** Fiat can be bound with a credible commitment until it behaves like a natural anchor — an independent central bank, a constitutional limit, an NGDP or *k*-percent rule, a currency board (Hong Kong importing the dollar's discipline wholesale). These are the rule-binding instruments (Friedman) and the exit-and-competition instruments (Hayek, *Denationalisation of Money*). The metallist sits between them — wanting a rule *and* distrusting the state monopoly — and reaches for gold as the apolitical escape. The framework offers both wings **without the metal**.

**But the dial is also insurance — the third axis the metallist, and an early draft of this table, leaves out: elasticity.** A supply that cannot answer a demand shock is not safety, it is fragility. Gold's rigidity *transmitted the Great Depression*; Bitcoin makes the rigidity a sacrament and so builds a deflation engine. The very discretion the metallist fears as a *betrayal vector* (Q2) is, on this axis, a *stabilisation tool* (call it Q2′) — the babysitting co-op did not need un-betrayable scrip, it needed *more* scrip when hoarding hit. The two concerns pull opposite ways, and the optimum **trades them off**: enough rule to resist deliberate dilution, enough discretion to absorb shocks. That trade-off has a name in practice — flexible inflation targeting — which is why neither pure rule nor pure discretion is the answer. (The omission was caught by a Krugman-style critique: the original table sided quietly with the goldbugs against the one axis macroeconomics cares about most.)

So the honest line is not *metal-good / fiat-bad*, nor even a clean *discretion-bad / rule-good*, but **substrate-agnostic across three axes at once — impersonal, known, and elastic — and no anchor maxes all three.** Gold buys impersonality and pays in rigidity; Bitcoin buys impersonality-plus-knownness and pays double in rigidity; bound fiat buys elasticity and pays in the credibility it must continually earn. This is still why the framework can be *more* sympathetic to the metallist than the chartalists or MMT, who tell him his fear is confused (the sovereign cannot run out — relax). His fear was never about *running out*; it is about being *diluted on purpose* — a real Q2 concern. The framework answers it honestly: **you are right that discretionary supply is a betrayal vector; you are wrong that the cure must be a commodity, and wrong that betrayal-resistance is the only thing to optimise. The rule you want can be mathematical or institutional rather than geological — but it must stay elastic enough to absorb a demand shock, which is exactly what gold and Bitcoin cannot do.**

## Historical note: the poles this dissolves

Three answers framed the field, and the recension dissolves all of them by refusing the question they share — *what pushes value into the money?*

- **Mises** (*Theory of Money and Credit*, 1912) pushed from **behind**: purchasing power regresses through time to a commodity origin. His truth, de-anchored: value really is marginal utility against known supply — keep that entirely — but the terminus need be no commodity, only a directly-valued use, which the medium's better-than-memory and conflict-settling service supplies (§1a).

- **Knapp** (*State Theory of Money*, 1905) pushed from **above**: money is a creature of law; the sovereign defines the unit and demands it back in taxes; the material is irrelevant. He deliberately bracketed purchasing power. His truth, de-anchored: the state is an enormous engine of acceptance — a guaranteed sink and the largest node in the network — but one input to the acceptance loop, not its foundation, and not necessary (Bitcoin has no sovereign; a dollar holds worth where its issuer is adversarial). *Credibility attaches to the rule, not the ruler.*

- **The debt / credit theory** (Mitchell-Innes 1913–14; Macleod; Graeber 2011; MMT) is the deepest of the three and the most nearly right. It saw, correctly, that the substrate was never metal but a **ledger of obligation** — credit precedes coin by four millennia. Keep that entirely. Its error is twofold. At the value layer it is the *chartalist push from above* in another dress: it grounds the token's worth in the **obligation behind it** (redeemability, the tax-drive) rather than in forward acceptance — and so fails exactly where Mises fails, on irredeemable fiat, on Bitcoin (nobody's liability), on the dollar held where its issuer owes the holder nothing. And at the level of essence it reads the **residue for the function**: unsettled positions on a ledger *look like* debt, so it concludes money *is* debt. The rope shows the reverse — money is what lets you *not carry* the debt. *"Debt is what happens when settlement cannot be made thin enough, public enough, or timely enough."* Money is debt's **antidote**, not its embodiment; debt is the failure case the medium exists to dissolve. In this project's terms, the debt theory is the **Balanced-mode origin story** (best supported anthropologically, but one of four) hardened into an essence.

**The unifying diagnosis.** Each push theory mistakes a *visible by-product* for the *invisible function*:

- **commodity theory** mistakes the **substrate** (metal) for the essence (ledger);
- **debt theory** mistakes the **residue** (unsettled positions) for the essence (settlement / acceptance).

In the shorthand of this project: Mises is *turtles sideways* through time and stops at the metal; Knapp is *turtles up to the throne* and stops; the debt theorist points at the residue the ledger leaves and calls it the ground. The recension is **turtles all the way up** — the loop closes on itself in expectation, the sovereign merely a very large turtle, the commodity merely an optional first one, and the debt merely the residue of a settlement not yet made thin enough.
