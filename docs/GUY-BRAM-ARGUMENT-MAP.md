# Guy versus Bram: Argument Map

This map compares Guy’s *Out of Thin Air* position with Bram’s BOVI position. It keeps the important qualifications: Guy allows private money things; Bram allows state bootstrapping; taxation is neither the sole source of monetary value nor irrelevant.

| Issue | G — Guy / OOTA | B — Bram / BOVI | Actual difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is money? | A social institution: a unit of account plus recognised claims and “money things.” | A sufficiently reliable ledger of who has given and not yet received. | G stresses institution and authority; B stresses ledger function. |
| Where is value? | Value is social and relational; accepted claims command real resources. | Value is the marginal surplus enabled by accepted monetary cooperation. | Broad agreement; B gives the sharper price theory. |
| What is intersubjectivity? | People accept a claim because they expect others to accept it. | Acceptance is recursively expected, but acceptance selects money; it does not itself constitute value. | B separates selection from value more sharply. |
| What makes a claim monetary? | Recognition, enforceability, and expected settlement. | Acceptance, availability, authentication, conservation, and clearing. | G foregrounds standing; B adds operational integrity conditions. |
| What comes before price? | The claim must first have social standing: people must recognise it and expect settlement. | Once standing exists, price reflects expected marginal service minus costs, risk, and extraction. | G explains the precondition; B explains the price afterward. |
| Who can create money? | Anyone can create a “money thing,” but sovereign money is created by the state and private money is denominated in it. | Private, community, platform, commodity, or protocol networks can become money if they satisfy the required conditions. | G treats private money as hierarchical; B allows more autonomous monetary systems. |
| What is private bank money? | Bank deposits are private liabilities created endogenously by banks, but they clear through public money. | Bank deposits are one type of ledger; their monetary status comes from acceptance and settlement, not merely their issuer. | G stresses public settlement hierarchy; B stresses general monetary function. |
| What starts a monetary network? | State authority, taxation, public obligation, or an existing institution can establish or redirect it. | State coercion is one bootstrap engine among several: state, speculation, industrial use, and platform leverage. | G foregrounds public authority; B generalises the cold-start problem. |
| Is tax the source of value? | No. Tax creates demand for sovereign money but does not explain all its uses or worth. | No. Tax is an acceptance and bootstrap mechanism, not the value-source. | Agreement. |
| Is tax a cold-start argument? | Partly. Tax can generate compulsory demand, but G also says states can redirect an existing equilibrium. | Yes, explicitly: tax can start or reinforce the network, but continuation value comes from use. | B makes the bootstrap/continuation distinction more explicit. |
| What if taxation stops? | The sovereign currency may remain valuable if its wider institutional and settlement network remains intact. | It can retain value if trade, contracts, banks, and expectations continue; it may collapse if the whole support network disappears. | Agreement, with different emphasis. |
| Why do foreigners accept dollars? | They participate in a dollar network anchored by U.S. authority, institutions, trade, and settlement. | They receive liquidity, settlement, trade, and option value without owing U.S. tax. | G explains the anchor; B explains the foreign holder’s marginal benefit. |
| What does the state do? | It defines the sovereign unit, imposes obligations, issues public liabilities, and settles claims. | It is one anchor, bootstrap, enforcer, and possible extractor among several. | State foundation for sovereign money versus institutional layer in money generally. |
| Does the state create all money? | No: private banks create money things; but modern sovereign money is state money. | No: states can create or co-opt money, but money can also arise through non-state networks. | This is the central scope distinction. |
| What about Bitcoin? | Bitcoin challenges the necessity of a central authority for a payment network, but does not replace the state hierarchy of bank money. | Bitcoin demonstrates that a non-state protocol can support a monetary network without taxation or a central issuer. | Bitcoin refutes universal state necessity, not the existence of public monetary hierarchies. |
| What is commodity money? | A physical interface carrying socially recognised claims. | Ledger money carried by a commodity substrate—“ledger money in costume.” | Same anti-essentialist conclusion. |
| Why did grain matter? | It was legible, seizable, calendrical, and directly useful for provisioning. | Those are administrative and practical advantages, not an intrinsic monetary floor. | G explains state usefulness; B blocks the leap to essence. |
| Why did cowries matter? | Existing cowrie networks could later be conscripted by states; state capacity changed while the substrate stayed constant. | Their value came from acceptance and network use, not shell substance. | G’s historical case supports B’s anti-essentialism. |
| What makes taxation possible? | Legibility and seizability: can the state read the claim, and is there a fiscal choke point? | Known supply, authentication, enforcement, availability, and credible acceptance. | G adds fiscal-administrative variables; B adds monetary-integrity variables. |
| What is the price of money? | A price is possible once a claim has standing and expected settlement. | The price is the present value of expected marginal monetary services minus costs, risk, and extraction. | G supplies the institutional precondition; B supplies the pricing model. |
| Is price the sum of beliefs? | No. Expectations stabilise a claim but do not abolish material constraints. | No. Price is a relational equilibrium constrained by production, scarcity, time, law, and technology. | Agreement. |
| Can ecological damage simply be priced? | Not before it becomes a recognised claim with a clearing obligation. | Public harms may remain outside private price even after a claim exists; price is not total social value. | G says constitute the claim first; B distinguishes price from welfare afterward. |
| What does the monetary ledger miss? | Physical depletion, ecological damage, unpaid care, and unavailable resources. | Public externalities may not enter price unless they feed back into acceptance, availability, credibility, or marginal benefit. | G stresses incompleteness; B stresses capitalisation and incidence. |
| What is extraction? | Redirecting expectations may not seize a physical stock, but it changes who must clear what. | Extraction is incidence: who receives new money, bears dilution, pays fees, loses privacy, or lacks recourse. | B adds that non-seizure can still impose real losses. |
| What does MMT explain? | Issuance, taxation, public liabilities, fiscal sovereignty, and real-resource constraints. | The state-side monetary machine, but not the whole theory of value, emergence, or distribution. | G makes MMT central; B treats it as one layer. |
| What does BOVI explain? | The bottom-up side: acceptance, ledger function, usefulness, and coordination. | How accepted monetary relations generate surplus and how authority, networks, and extraction interact. | B expands the bottom-up and distributional analysis. |
| What is the historical direction? | In the state-money/MMT strand: authority and obligation are foundational to modern money. | Social ledgers and networks can precede, coexist with, or later be captured by states. | G starts with sovereign money; B starts with money in general. |
| What is the main danger? | Treating money as a physical object and ignoring political authority. | Confusing acceptance with value, price with welfare, and issuance with fairness. | G guards against materialism; B guards against analytical conflation. |
| What is the empirical test? | Hold state capacity constant and test whether substrate determines taxability. | Compare acceptance, availability, marginal service, issuance, and incidence across systems. | G tests state conscription; B tests monetary value and distribution. |
| Does either theory imply policy? | No. Monetary ontology does not automatically dictate policy. | No. BOVI is diagnostic and politically non-prescriptive. | Agreement. |

## Bottom line

> **G explains sovereign money: how authority creates a public unit and organises a hierarchy of private claims.**
>
> **B explains money in general: how accepted ledgers become useful and valuable, whether or not a state is involved.**

G’s MMT source explicitly allows private bank money while placing it under sovereign money’s settlement hierarchy: [Wray, *Understanding Modern Money*](https://www.levyinstitute.org/pubs/Wray_Understanding_Modern.pdf).

Bitcoin supports B’s narrower claim that a monetary network can operate without a central authority or bank, but it does not prove that private bank deposits can operate without public settlement infrastructure: [Nakamoto, *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*](https://bitcoin.org/en/bitcoin-paper.html).
