# Who Owns the Door

*A companion note to "Money with a Return Date": what the future-obligation family means at the beginning of a money's life, and who collects at its terminus.*

---

## I. Where the last note stopped

The previous note in this series ended at a door. Modern money, the Bank of England's own account says, is lent into existence and destroyed on repayment — made with a return date. The two-century literature behind that fact, from Fullarton's law of reflux to the fiscal theory of the price level, explains the acceptance of a valueless token by pointing at a **future obligation denominated in it**: a tax bill, a loan due, a surplus promised. And read carefully, that family delivers exactly two services — it *selects* the monetary equilibrium, and it *terminates* the unraveling that otherwise kills token money in any world with a last period — while the third thing popularly claimed for it, being the *source of the value*, is the one register the theorems do not deliver. The value is the trade the token unlocks; the obligation is the guarantee that "tomorrow, someone must still be standing at the door."

That sentence left two questions standing. What happens at the *beginning* of a money's life, before any door exists? And — since a guaranteed door is never free — who owns the door, and what do they charge?

## II. The dividend and the hazard

Both questions price more easily with the microeconomics on the table, so fix the accounting first.

A token everyone accepts pays no coupon in goods. What it pays, each period, is a **service dividend**: the option to complete trades that would otherwise die. A stranger will not take your IOU — no commitment; will not barter — no double coincidence; so without the token, the surplus of that trade never comes into existence at all. Holding the token is holding an *option on otherwise-dead trades*. Call the expected value of that option per period **ℓ**: the probability of meeting such a trade, times the surplus at stake, times your bargained share. That is "the marginal trade the money unlocks," priced — real cargo, produced by acceptance rather than prior to it. No floor; a real dividend. (And it is derived, not assumed: the search-theoretic literature builds ℓ from the frictions themselves — Lagos and Wright is the modern benchmark; Patinkin stated the margin half a century earlier.)

Now the relay. You hold the token one period, harvest one ℓ, and spend it — which is *selling the remaining stream to the next holder* at their discounted valuation of the tail. Iterate the standard asset recursion and the token's worth is the present value of the entire chain of trades it will unlock across all of its future holders: a hot potato with a dividend at every hop. In steady state the chain collapses to a perpetuity:

> **V = ℓ / (r + δ)**
>
> the value of money = the flow of unlocked marginal surplus, divided by the time discount plus the hazard that the acceptance network dies.

Every comparative static in these notes falls out of the fraction. A thicker market raises ℓ — and raises it for everyone at once, since each holder's acceptance is a component of every other holder's ℓ: the self-reinforcing pull. More tokens set the marginal token against an ever-more-marginal trade, so ℓ falls at the margin: quantity discipline, derived rather than assumed. A trust shock raises δ and cuts V without touching the dividend at all.

Two consequences carry the rest of this note. First, the margin is *observable*: holders add real balances until the marginal ℓ equals the foregone nominal interest rate, so the market quotes the unlock-service's price daily — it is the convenience yield, the zero return people accept on checking balances. Second — the hinge — the obligation family enters the fraction in exactly one place, and it is not ℓ. A tax discharges at face value whatever prices are; it adds no dividend. What a legally pre-committed acceptor does is **truncate δ**: it makes the unraveling branch unreachable. Like a guarantee on a bond — it never touches the coupon; it changes the survival probability. *The trades are the dividend; the sink is the default protection.* The three registers of the last note, in one formula: **ℓ is the value-source; δ is where selection and termination live.**

And the fiscal theory? Its valuation equation is the *same present-value machine* pointed at a different stream: it discounts the state's primary surpluses where this fraction discounts the traders' trade surpluses. The century's quarrel over what backs the token is, in the end, a dispute over **whose surplus stream is being discounted** — and the registers resolve it: the traders' stream is the value; the state's stream is the guarantee on δ. Two dividend-discount models, one door.

## III. Termini are launch scaffolds

Start with a symmetry the quarrel has hidden for a century. The classical, backward-looking answer to money's value — Mises's regression theorem, tracing today's acceptance through yesterday's prices to a use valued before money — is, read at its strongest, a theory of how the expectation chain can *begin*. Its terminus does its work once, at the origin, and then retires; on Mises's own account, fiat survives the severed link to gold. The tax-driven answer is the same shape pointed forward: Smith's colonial paper, the greenback-era Demand Notes, the installation of a unit by receivability — accounts of how a money gets *launched*. Both schools, in other words, are **cold-start theories that got promoted into value theories**. The demotion can be performed symmetrically and without loss: keep both engines, refuse both essences.

Seen that way, the two termini — the commodity floor behind, the obligation sink ahead — are **launch scaffolds**. Monies historically begin life with one attached, and mature networks can shed them: the world left gold, and the value stayed; Iraq's Swiss dinar lost every obligation its issuer owed and circulated for a decade on expectation alone. In the fraction's terms: a scaffold is a δ-device for the launch window, deployed exactly when the hazard is at its lifetime maximum. Floors and sinks are edge devices. The interior of a money's life runs on the acceptance loop.

Three cases calibrate the claim.

**Bank money has no cold-start problem — by construction.** A loan-issued deposit is *born pre-sold*: created in the same act as the dated obligation that will buy it back. The borrower is its guaranteed first and last acceptor. Tooke's old discriminator — paper that is "returnable to the issuer" versus paper that is not — turns out to be a cold-start taxonomy: only non-returnable money must go out and find believers. Bank money is born with its believer signed up. *Only orphan money needs converts.*

**Small-scale money barely faces the problem either.** At the scale of a village tally — the mutual ledgers described in *The Four Modes* — reputation already does what statutes do later: repeated dealing makes commitments enforceable without any formal machinery, which is why a record of who-owes-whom suffices there and why the deep proof that "money is memory" needs no state anywhere in it. The cold-start problem is a *stranger-scale* problem. It bites exactly where reputational enforcement runs out — and that is precisely where the formal termini, tax and contract, do their heaviest lifting.

**And the exception prices the rule.** Bitcoin is the one large money that cold-started with no terminus at all — no floor of prior use, no obligation anywhere in its network — on the strength of speculation organized around an ideological focal point, the ingredient Hayek's otherwise identical proposal lacked. It worked, once, and the bill is permanent: the volatility, and a worthless equilibrium that never leaves the menu. Launching without a scaffold is possible. It is just not free.

The corollary reaches into policy. The great stabilizations are, on this reading, exercises in **terminus engineering**. Sargent's classic study of the interwar hyperinflations documents that in Austria, Hungary, Poland, and Germany alike, inflation "stopped abruptly rather than gradually" — and not by monetary tightening: note circulation *kept growing rapidly* after each stabilization. What ended the inflations was a credible "change in the policy regime": a newly independent central bank "legally committed to refuse the government's demand for additional unsecured credit," together with a simultaneous fiscal alteration binding the government to back its debt with "sufficiently large prospective taxes relative to public expenditures." Read that against the previous note's two-factor result and it is the ratio theorem operating in the wild: the stock grew, the sink's real capacity was rebuilt, and the level stabilized — because the level was never a function of the stock alone. Germany's Rentenmark of 1923 is the textbook piece, and its details are better than the legend. The famous "backing" — a mortgage on the nation's land and industry — was, as historians from Bresciani-Turroni onward have put it, a fiction: never foreclosable, redeemable only into another gold-indexed paper claim, and in practice almost never redeemed (about 1,600 Rentenmarks had been presented by January 1924). Meanwhile the note was **not even legal tender**: no law compelled any private party to take it. The only legal compulsion anywhere in its design was that *public treasuries were obliged to accept it* — Knapp's pay-offices, operating naked — alongside a hard cap on issuance and on credit to the state that ended deficit monetization, and a budget swung from deep deficit to surplus within two months. The notional floor was a common-knowledge prop; every working part was a capacity-limited sink and a bound issuer. What stopped the inflation was not the story told about the land. It was the re-attachment of a terminus. (A minority reading — Franco, Merkin — credits the exchange-rate peg as the operative mechanism; the fictional-backing finding, by contrast, is near-universal.)

## IV. The terminus is a toll booth

Now the second question — because everything in Section III describes a service, and the service has an owner.

A guaranteed acceptor is, by construction, a counterparty you cannot refuse to deal with. Innes located the micro event a century ago: the taxpayer "has to acquire his portion of the debt from some holder" — a *forced trade with a deadline*. Forced trades on known calendars have prices that someone else gets to set. The very property that makes the sink a perfect terminator — refusal is impossible — makes it a perfect toll booth. If the skims of a monetary system live in its gaps, the deepest gap has an address: **the skim lives at the terminus.**

Trace the circuit and the toll has two ends. Tokens go out at a source and come home at a sink, and each end has its own incidence. The source side is the famous one: Cantillon's observation that whoever stands near the spigot spends new money at old prices. The sink side has a twin that almost nobody traces: whoever stands *far* from the tokens as the obliged date approaches must scramble for them against a deadline — distress sales, tax-date liquidity squeezes, the seasonal stringencies of every agrarian tax calendar.

And the sink-side pump has been operated deliberately, in the open. The colonial record — assembled in Forstater's study of colonial Africa — shows administrations imposing direct money taxes, hut, poll, head, and wife taxes, payable only in the colonial currency, designed to force subsistence populations into wage labor and cash cropping. The design was explicit about which register it was using: "the colonial government did not need the colonial currency held by Africans. What they needed was for the African population to *need* the currency." Some taxes were discharged by proof of having worked for European employers — a "labor tax," in the period's own vocabulary, whose purpose was visibly not revenue. And the intent was stated by the administrators themselves: "We consider that taxation is the only possible method of compelling the native to leave his reserve for the purpose of seeking work," Governor Sir Percy Girouard of British East Africa is reported to have said. The sink as a labor pump, on the record. It belongs in this family's ledger next to its successes: the same device that stabilizes a currency also herds people into it.

## V. The weapon clause

The herding has a formal version. In the search-theoretic models, a government committed to *accepting* an object selects it as money once the government's share of activity crosses a threshold — and the theorem runs in reverse: a sufficiently large government, by *refusing* an object, can eliminate it from circulation. Acceptance policy is a two-edged instrument, and history has swung both edges. The United States in 1865 laid a ten-percent tax on state bank notes with the express purpose of taxing them out of existence, and the Supreme Court, upholding it in *Veazie Bank v. Fenno*, was frank that Congress may "restrain, by suitable enactments, the circulation as money of any notes not issued under its own authority." Wörgl's celebrated depression scrip was shut by litigation over the note monopoly; the WIR's book-money cooperative, offering nothing bearer-shaped to seize, survived from the same era to the present — the discriminator, as *The Four Modes* argues, being bearer-versus-book, not the economics.

Once herded into a unit, holders are exposed to the unit's supply governance — and an elastic supply *redistributes on its way through*, normally, lawfully, and mostly invisibly. That exposure is the standing rent of the arrangement.

## VI. The legitimation subsidy

Here is why the register-sorting of the previous note is not pedantry but political economy. The popular compression — *the tax gives money its value* — is not merely a theoretical overclaim. It is a **legitimation subsidy**. If the state is the source of the value, then whatever flows to the terminus reads as a creator's royalty. Demote the claim to what the theorems actually support — the state supplies *selection* (the best common-knowledge machine ever built) and *termination* (the node that blocks the unravel) — and the invoice changes character: these are magnificent, genuinely valuable *coordination services*, and they are services, priced and paid for like any other. The metallist error underprices them at zero; the chartalist error rounds them up to everything. The honest position pays for the door and asks for a receipt.

A receipt would itemize the **terminus rent**: seigniorage at the source; the dilution skim on holdings herded into the unit; the deadline squeezes at the sink; and, on the private row of the family, the spread charged where the loan-sink's owner meets a borrower with nowhere else to stand. None of these is theft; every one of them is a price set at a node with structural bargaining power, contestable only where it is visible. In §II's terms, the legitimation subsidy is an accounting fraud between the fraction's two slots: **it bills ℓ's stream — the traders' dividend — to the owner of the δ-machine.**

## VII. Who owns the door

So the family of obligation-stabilized money arrives with its politics attached, and the live monetary alternatives sort neatly as answers to one question — who owns the terminus, and what do they charge?

The **state answer**: a tax-owned door — maximally elastic, credible at scale, and rent-collecting by design; you buy the world's best stabilizer and pay the terminus rent. The **protocol answer**: no door at all — no owner, no rent, and the permanent volatility bill of a money that is all selection and no termination. The **mutual answer**: a door owned by its users — reflux without a sovereign, rent negotiated among members, capacity limited by the community's own scale. The Swiss dinar, one last time, is the found experiment at the edge: a network that *evicted* its toll collector and kept the coordination — a decade with no seigniorage flowing to anyone — while forgoing everything an owned door provides: elasticity, a lender of last resort, an anchor better than the odds of future re-adoption.

Nothing in this note says which answer to choose; that depends on what a community needs its door to do, and the framework behind these notes is deliberately neutral on the response. It is partisan only on the visibility: whatever door you stand behind, the toll should be legible — traced at *both* ends of the circuit, the Cantillon end and the deadline end, and billed as what it is, the price of selection and termination, not the source of the value.

The last note ended: money is made with a return date, and the return date is the guarantee that someone is standing at the door. Now the addendum it owed you. The door is real, the guarantee is real, and the service is worth paying for. But the door has an owner, the owner sets the toll, and the first demand of anyone who lives behind it is not a different door. It is an itemized bill.
