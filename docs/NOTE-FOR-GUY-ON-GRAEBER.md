# What to Make of Graeber

Dear Guy,

You asked what to make of Graeber. The short answer is that his argument is better than his reputation, and his evidence is thinner than his prose. Those two facts pull in opposite directions, and almost every mistake people make with *Debt* comes from noticing only one of them.

Start with what is secure, because it is most of what you will want from him and none of it is seriously contested. Accounting and credit records precede coinage by millennia: the Mesopotamian tablets are administrative, and the first coins arrive some two and a half thousand years later. Nobody has produced an attested case of a barter-only economy that then went on to invent money. And obligation is the older and deeper monetary substrate; the commodity is a latecomer wearing the costume. That result holds. It is worth saying that it is anthropology's result rather than his — he is the synthesist, not the discoverer — but it holds.

Now the correction that matters most, because it runs in his favour and almost nobody knows it.

**He never claimed that barter does not exist.** The chapter is called "The Myth of Barter" and the title has done him enormous damage, because the argument underneath it is careful and the title is not. What he actually says, in plain words, is that barter is almost never the way neighbours deal with one another — it happens between strangers, and between enemies. That is the claim. Barter is real, it is well attested, and it turns up reliably where trust is absent or has broken down. His point is only that it is not the seedbed from which money grew.

Hold onto that distinction, because it is the difference between a defensible position and an indefensible one. The economists who have gone after him — Murphy, Selgin, Hummel — are mostly attacking a universal negative that the book does not assert. When you cite him, cite the argument and not the chapter title. Anyone who writes "barter is a myth" has handed a critic a free win that Graeber himself never had to concede.

There is one place where his handling of a source will not survive a hostile reader, and you should know it before a reviewer finds it in your footnotes. His authority for the barter claim is Caroline Humphrey's 1985 paper, and he calls it the definitive anthropological work on barter. He quotes her line that no example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described — and he stops there. Her sentence does not stop there. It continues, immediately, with a *but*: there are economies today that are nevertheless dominated by barter, and that is in fact what her paper is about. It is a study of trade partnerships in north-east Nepal, arguing that barter is largely a *post*-monetary phenomenon that intensifies as an economy disintegrates. Elsewhere she writes that barter was "very rare" as a dominant system — not that it never occurred. None of this overturns his claim, and her post-monetary finding actually supports another argument of his. But a twenty-four-page article on Nepalese trade is not the definitive work on barter, and the truncation is the kind of thing that gets quoted back at you. Go to Humphrey directly and you get a better claim than the one he built on her.

The item I would most want you to know about, given what your book is doing, is the one buried in his endnotes.

The idea that coinage was invented to pay soldiers — the military-coinage complex, which he takes from Geoffrey Ingham and extends into a military-coinage-slavery complex — is a *minority* position in numismatics. It peaked in the late 1950s and has been losing ground ever since to Kraay, Price, Wallace and Schaps, partly on the objection that you cannot pay soldiers in coin unless there are already people willing to take coin, and partly on denomination: the early issues are too large and too few to have functioned as everyday currency. Graeber knows all of this. He cites the literature accurately in a note, calls the first objection weak, and overrides it. That is a legitimate move and he made it in the open. But the main text sails on as though the matter were settled, and a reader who takes the narrative at face value will not learn that the specialists are mostly on the other side. If soldiers-and-coins is going to carry weight for you, take the weight off him and put it on the numismatists — or say plainly that you are backing a minority reading, as he should have.

Two more things, then the part that actually constrains you.

The typology — his three principles of communism, exchange and hierarchy — has been contested in print, and the contest is more interesting than the scheme. Bill Maurer, reviewing the book at length in *Anthropological Forum*, points out that the single "exchange" category has to hold both commercial exchange and Mauss's gift, which most of the discipline is at pains to keep apart. Maurer's sharper objection borrows Jane Collier's ideal types and observes that they sit at right angles to Graeber's, because Graeber derives relations from principles while Collier reads the principles off the distribution of rewards — the people with the goods being the ones who get to claim their closeness to a universal principle put them there. John Holbo has made the mirror-image complaint that the boundary between communism and exchange will not hold either. The scheme is generative. It is not settled, and it should not be adopted as furniture.

Maurer also lands the heaviest scholarly blow in the literature, and it is worth stating precisely because it is so easy to state loosely: Graeber attacks the primordial-debt theorists and then reproduces their structure, conflating an origin in the sense of a metaphysical starting point with an origin in the sense of a historical moment. He does it, Maurer notes, while remaining silent on Mauss — the actual ancestor of the theory he is attributing to three French economists.

And the constraint. **There is no theory of monetary value in this book.** He gives you money's origin, its social form, and its moral history, and then he stops. He does not ask what makes a unit *worth* anything, and he does not pretend to; Mike Beggs called it an economics-sized gap that Graeber cannot fill, and the charge was never rebutted. That is a gap rather than an error — a book is allowed to not be about something. But your book cannot inherit the gap. Whatever account you give of why a sovereign unit has the value it has, you will be building it yourself, and *Debt* will not hold the other end of it.

There is also some ordinary carelessness. A footnote about the founding of Apple is wrong in every particular; a passage on the Federal Reserve overstates its distance from the government; the chapter on 1971 and American debt-imperialism is the weakest in the book, asserted rather than evidenced, and it requires you to believe that US Treasuries are a promise everyone knows will not be kept, when they are the most sought-after asset on earth. None of that touches the anthropology. It does tell you what happens when he moves outside his field.

So: use him. He is the best readable synthesis of a real and hard-won result, and the result is one your book depends on. But cite the careful Graeber rather than the quotable one, and put the primary sources underneath anything that has to bear weight — Humphrey for barter, the assyriologists for Mesopotamia, the numismatists for coin. Do that and a successful attack on *Debt*, when it comes, costs you a footnote instead of a chapter.

## Where to read these

**The book itself** — [*Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, Melville House first printing, 2011](https://archive.org/details/graeber-debt_the_first_5000_years), full text.

**Graeber answering his critics** — ["On the Invention of Money"](https://libcom.org/article/invention-money-notes-sex-adventure-monomaniacal-sociopathy-and-true-function-economics), his 2011 reply to the economists, and where the flat "0%" formulation appears. His [reply to the Crooked Timber seminar](https://crookedtimber.org/2012/04/02/seminar-on-debt-the-first-5000-years-reply/), and his [reply to Brad DeLong](https://davidgraeber.org/articles/brad-delong-reply/) on the Apple and Federal Reserve errors. The [full seminar](https://crookedtimber.org/category/david-graeber-debt-seminar/) is the single best entry point to the whole argument.

**The barter claim** — Caroline Humphrey, "Barter and Economic Disintegration," *Man* 20(1), 1985: [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2802221), or [this open PDF](https://voidnetwork.gr/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Barter-and-economic-disintegration-by-Caroline-Humphrey.pdf) if you want to read the truncated sentence in context — it is on the first page.

**The economists** — Robert Murphy, ["Have Anthropologists Overturned Menger?"](https://mises.org/mises-daily/have-anthropologists-overturned-menger); George Selgin, "The Myth of the Myth of Barter" ([Cato original](https://www.cato.org/blog/myth-myth-barter), which currently blocks non-browser requests — the [FEE reprint](https://fee.org/articles/is-barter-a-myth/) is the same piece); Jeffrey Rogers Hummel, [on Graeber at Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2012/07/hummel_on_graeb.html), the most structurally careful of the three.

**The missing value theory** — Mike Beggs, ["Debt: The First 500 Pages"](https://jacobin.com/2012/08/debt-the-first-500-pages), which is where the "economics-sized gap" line comes from. J.W. Mason's defence, ["In Defense of David Graeber's *Debt*"](https://jacobin.com/2012/09/in-defense-of-david-graebers-debt/), concedes more than it means to.

**The scholarly reviews** — Bill Maurer, "David Graeber's *Wunderkammer*," *Anthropological Forum* 23(1), 2013: [open PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2n76r50s/qt2n76r50s_noSplash_8f94cd6b7f32ca8af7ff69aa18fce96a.pdf) (the [publisher's version](https://doi.org/10.1080/00664677.2012.729492) is paywalled). This is the one to read if you read only one. John Holbo, ["Reciprocity vs. Baseline Communism"](https://crookedtimber.org/2015/02/19/reciprocity-vs-baseline-communism/), on the typology's other seam.

**Antiquity and the coinage question** — John Weisweiler (ed.), *Debt in the Ancient Mediterranean and Near East* (Oxford, 2022), whose opening chapter is on Graeber's currency-slavery-warfare complex; OUP blocks scripted requests, so the [Bryn Mawr review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2023/2023.09.30/) is the accessible way in. Neville Morley, a Roman historian, weighs the ancient history [here](https://crookedtimber.org/2012/02/22/the-return-of-grand-narrative-in-the-human-sciences/).

**The 1971 chapter** — Henry Farrell, ["The world economy is not a tribute system"](https://crookedtimber.org/2012/02/22/the-world-economy-is-not-a-tribute-system/), and his [retrospective a decade on](https://crookedtimber.org/2023/07/08/debt-4102-days-later/).

**The factual errors** — Brad DeLong has pursued these for years; [this post](https://braddelong.substack.com/p/on-april-fools-day-we-remember-david) collects the Apple paragraph.

**Worth reading even though it isn't a criticism you need** — Chris Knight, ["Did Communism Make Us Human?"](https://libcom.org/article/did-communism-make-us-human-anthropology-david-graeber-chris-knight), attacks baseline communism from the left, and is the most interesting disagreement in the pile.
