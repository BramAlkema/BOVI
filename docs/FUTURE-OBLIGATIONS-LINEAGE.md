# Money Pulled From the Future — the future-obligation lineage

*A research deep-dive on the shoulders that explain the micro phenomenon from the macro side: future taxes, future loan repayments, future debt service, future surpluses.*

---

## The question

The framework is micro-first. Its load-bearing scene is two people and a tally: a valueless rope, marked and accepted, unlocking a trade that would not otherwise have cleared. The value is *floorless but fruitful* — nothing prior underneath it, real cargo inside it — and the reason anyone takes the tally is **forward acceptance**: they expect the next person to take it too.

There is a large, serious literature that explains the same acceptance from the other end of the telescope. It says the tally is taken because **someone, at a dated future moment, is obliged to hand it back**: a taxpayer to a treasury, a borrower to a bank, a government to its bondholders. These are aggregate-scale accounts. The question this document answers is whether they explain the micro phenomenon — and if so, by what mechanism, and at what cost to the framework's own claims.

**They do. And the answer is more interesting than either "yes, chartalism was right" or "no, Quirk 3 stands."**

---

## The finding in one line

> **A future obligation denominated in the unit is a guaranteed future acceptor. It terminates the forward-acceptance regress at a node that is legally pre-committed rather than merely expected. It is not a floor under the value; it is a sink ahead of it.**

And one clause of precision that the formal record forces: **what travels backward from the sink is acceptance, not a level.** The sink's *existence* buys selection; only its *capacity relative to the stock* buys anything quantitative (Smith's ratio, and Starr's theorems below).

The whole family — Smith's colonial tax receivability, Knapp's pay-offices, Innes's redemption-by-taxation, Fullarton's reflux, Schumpeter's credit against unbuilt output, the fiscal theory's discounted surpluses — is **one device in four substrates**. And the device is not a value-source. It is a **common-knowledge generator**: it makes forward acceptance public, dated, and enforceable, which is precisely the selection mechanism the framework already runs on.

This is the framework's own push/pull pattern turning up inside the macro literature, where nobody calls it that. **Mises terminates his regress backward — through remembered, realized prices to a prior price, a use valued before exchange. Cochrane terminates his forward — through forecast surpluses to the real resources taxation will extract. A floor is behind you; a sink is ahead of you.** (Mises wrote "commodity"; his own stated requirement is only "exchange value on account of other employments" — a prior price, the standard the recension's §1b already holds him to.) The two regresses are not one regress run in two directions. Mises's chain is memory: it answers how a valuation of pure exchange value can ever *form*; it is walked one step per day — today leans on yesterday — and reaches its terminus once, at the origin, after which the terminus does no further work; fiat survives the cut link on his own account. Cochrane's chain is forecast: it answers what pins the level *today*, and is walked in full at every date — zero the expected surpluses and the value goes now. What the two moves share is exact and smaller: **each stops a chain of exchange-value-explained-by-exchange-value at a node valued outside the acceptance loop.** Mises even keeps a forward-facing slot of his own — *credit money*, "a claim falling due in the future that is used as a general medium of exchange" — where a Misesian will file Cochrane's dollar before demanding the unit's backward regression all the same; §5 answers him: the claim is denominated in the very unit it is supposed to back. Only one of the two directions is compatible with a rope.

**Credit for the halves, before the join.** The directional contrast is not this document's: David Glasner stated it in 2011 — *"The Regression Theorem, as its name attests, is backward-looking. But economic problems, as Austrian economics to its credit usually recognizes, are forward-looking"* — and, following Earl Thompson and Wicksteed, named tax-acceptability as *"all that is necessary to avoid the backward induction argument"* ("The Paradox of Fiat Money", *Uneasy Money*, 25 July 2011). The sink is MMT house doctrine under two of its own names: Mosler's *drain* — *"government spending, which adds reserves to the banking system, and government taxation and borrowing, which drain reserves"* (*Soft Currency Economics*; his drain is operational plumbing for interest-rate maintenance, not a value claim, but the hydraulic image is his) — and Wray's *redemption* — "taxes drive money", and the tax payment *"simultaneously destroys currency as well as the taxpayer's liability to government"* (1998). Tymoigne makes redemption a valuation formula: a currency is a financial instrument priced by its reflux channels, and *"by not creating any means for their monetary instruments to reflux, some governments had effectively imposed an infinite term to maturity so the fair value was zero"* (Levy WP 799, 2014) — that is the sink, stated as finance. What this document adds is only the identification: that the floor and the sink are the *same* termination move executed in opposite temporal directions. That sentence appears in none of these sources; Glasner comes within a clause of it and should be read first.

## §1. One device, four substrates

| | who is obliged | to whom | when | what enforces it | the sink's capacity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Future tax** | every taxpayer | the treasury | the tax date | law, courts, prison | total tax take per period |
| **Future loan repayment** | every borrower | the lending bank | the maturity date | contract, collateral, foreclosure | outstanding loan book |
| **Future primary surplus** | the state | its bondholders | the whole future path | the state's ability to tax and abstain from spending | present value of surpluses |
| **Future taxes, capitalised now** | households, anticipating | themselves | now, by foresight | rational expectations | — |

Read across: the same object each time. **A dated, enforceable, unit-denominated claim on a named party.** Every row creates someone who *must* acquire the unit before a deadline. That person is a buyer of last resort — not for the token's substance, but for the discharge it performs.

The rows differ in exactly one respect that matters for the framework: **rows 2 and 4 need no sovereign.** The reflux sink is private, decentralised, and dated — and it has its own formal literature: Shubik & Wilson (1977) give fiat money terminal value through a default penalty, and Dubey & Geanakoplos (1992) prove positive value in a finite horizon *because agents must repay bank loans* — row 2, built formally, twenty years before this document. This is why the loan side of the family fits the framework better than the tax side — see §5.

## §2. The shoulders, in their own words

> **Verification legend.** ✅ = quoted from primary full text retrieved and read for this document. ⚠️ = quotation taken from secondary reporting; the wording should be checked against the original before it goes into anything published.

### Adam Smith, 1776 — the tax sink, and its capacity ✅

The lineage does not begin with Knapp. It begins with the person the metallists claim. Smith, on colonial paper money in *Wealth of Nations* Book II, Ch. II:

> "The paper of each colony being received in the payment of the provincial taxes, for the full value for which it had been issued, it necessarily **derived from this use some additional value**, over and above what it would have had, from the real or supposed distance of the term of its final discharge and redemption. **This additional value was greater or less, according as the quantity of paper issued was more or less above what could be employed in the payment of the taxes** of the particular colony which issued it."

> "**A prince, who should enact that a certain proportion of his taxes should be paid in a paper money of a certain kind, might thereby give a certain value to this paper money, even though the term of its final discharge and redemption should depend altogether upon the will of the prince.**"

Two things are already complete in 1776, and both are load-bearing.

First, **value without a redemption date.** Smith explicitly detaches the tax-value from any promise to convert: the prince may never redeem, may redeem at whim, and the paper still holds value. This is a forward sink with no floor beneath it — Smith, of all people, describing floorless value and not flinching.

Second, and more useful, **the sink has a capacity.** Value falls as issuance rises *relative to the tax take*. The anchor is not the existence of a tax but the **ratio of the stock to the sink**. This is a quantity theory bounded by an obligation, and it is the piece popular chartalism drops.

*(Cochrane quotes this passage as his own ancestor. His rendering trims Smith's "should be paid" to "be paid" — immaterial to the argument, but the framework's habit is to quote from the source.)*

### Georg Friedrich Knapp, 1905 / 1924 trans. — the unit is a legal creature ✅

The book opens with the sentence everyone cites:

> "**Money is a creature of law. A theory of money must therefore deal with legal history.**"

And on what "chartal" means — which is *not* what the popular version says:

> "**Chartality rests on a certain relation to the laws.** It is, therefore, impossible to tell from the pieces themselves whether they are Chartal or not."

Knapp's actual claim is narrower and better than the slogan. He is defining the **unit of account** as a legal artefact, not deriving purchasing power from taxation. He says so: the validity of the pieces "is fixed by law", but the *quotation* — what they exchange for — he deliberately brackets. The State pay-offices decide what counts as a valid discharge; they do not thereby decide what it buys.

**This is the single most misread source in the family.** Knapp is a theory of the **unit**, and the framework can take it whole. It is not a theory of the **price level**, and reading it as one is what the recension's Quirk 3 fires at.

### A. Mitchell-Innes, 1913 & 1914 — the sink stated as a law ✅

The 1914 essay is the sharpest statement in the whole literature of the future-obligation mechanism:

> "The holder of a coin or certificate has the absolute right to pay any debt due to the government by tendering that coin or certificate, and **it is this right and nothing else which gives them their value.**"

> "With every coin issued a burden or charge or obligation or debt is laid on the community in favor of certain individuals, and **it can only be wiped out by taxation**."

> "**Whenever a tax is imposed, each taxpayer becomes responsible for the redemption of a small part of the debt which the government has contracted by its issues of money** … He has to **acquire his portion of the debt from some holder** of a coin or certificate or other form of government money, and present it to the Treasury in liquidation of his legal debt."

> "**This, then — the redemption of government debt by taxation — is the basic law of coinage** and of any issue of government 'money' in whatever form."

And the general principle, stated as commerce's own grammar:

> "**A credit cancels a debt; this is the primitive law of commerce.** By sale a credit is acquired, by purchase a debt is created. Purchases, therefore, are paid for by sales."

> "A banker is one who **centralises the debts of mankind and cancels them against one another**. Banks are the clearing houses of commerce."

The third quotation is the one that does the macro→micro work, and it is usually skipped. **"He has to acquire his portion of the debt from some holder."** The taxpayer must *go and get* the unit from someone who has it. That trade is a micro trade. The macro obligation reaches the individual only by propagating backward through the trading network — which is §4's mechanism, named in 1914.

*Note on a famous line:* the "the eye has never seen, nor the hand touched, a dollar" phrasing widely attributed to Innes was **not found** in the 1913 text retrieved here. What he does argue, verifiably, is that "there never was a monetary unit which depended on the value of coin or on a weight of metal." Use that.

### P. H. Wicksteed, 1910 — the sink with its capacity, in prose ⚠️

*The Common Sense of Political Economy*, Book II Ch. VII (quoted here via Forstater, "Tax-Driven Money", CFEPS WP 35, 2004; check against the 1910 edition before print):

> "as these demands of the Government are recurrent, **there will always be a set of persons to whom the Government paper** stamped with a unit weight of gold **is actually equivalent to that weight of gold itself**"

— acceptance spreads because others are "willing to enter into fresh bargains … as long as it is easy to find persons who, being themselves under obligation to the Government, actually find the Government promise to relinquish their claim … as valuable as the gold itself" — and the bound:

> "a very large number of easily accessible persons actually value the paper as much as the gold **up to a certain determined point, the point, to wit, of their obligations to the Government. Thus it is that a limited demand for paper**, at its face value in gold, **constitutes a permanent market**."

Guaranteed acceptor, network propagation, and Smith's capacity ratio — all three, 1910, in prose. Glasner's and Starr's lineages both run through this passage.

### Abba Lerner, 1947 — the relay, terminated ✅

*Money as a Creature of the State*, AER 37(2), p. 313 (verified by OCR of the JSTOR scan of the original — the scan is at `sources/lerner-1947.pdf`, OCR pages alongside it):

> "**The modern state can make anything it chooses generally acceptable as money** and thus establish its value quite apart from any connection, even of the most formal kind, with gold or with backing of any kind. It is true that a simple declaration that such and such is money will not do, even if backed by the most convincing constitutional evidence of the state's absolute sovereignty. **But if the state is willing to accept the proposed money in payment of taxes and other obligations to itself the trick is done. Everyone who has obligations to the state will be willing to accept the pieces of paper with which he can settle the obligations, and all other people will be willing to accept these pieces of paper because they know that the taxpayers, etc., will accept them in turn.**"

> "…at the present time, in a normally well-working economy, **money is a creature of the state. Its general acceptability, which is its all-important attribute, stands or falls by its acceptability by the state.**"

Read the first passage closely: the final sentence is §4.2's mechanism — forward acceptance propagating outward from the legally-obliged node — **stated in 1947**. The fusion this document performs in §4.2 was performed there first; what Lerner adds beyond Innes is the explicit expectations relay. He also reads historical gold-backing as nothing more than "historical accounts of how acceptability came to be established in certain cases" — acceptability transferred from a thing that already had it, i.e. a bootstrap engine, named as such in 1947.

Where Lerner overreaches is the second passage: *stands or falls*. That is the constitution claim — state acceptance not as the best acceptance-engine but as the thing acceptability consists in — and the specimen that refutes it is not Buenos Aires (see §5) but the sink-zeroed case: the Iraqi Swiss dinar, holding value for a decade with no state willing to accept it anywhere.

### Tooke and Fullarton, 1844/45 — the reflux, i.e. the private sink ✅

The Banking School's answer to the Currency School: a note issued through a loan cannot be over-issued, because it **returns to the issuer as the loan is repaid**. Fullarton, *On the Regulation of Currencies* (2nd ed. 1845), Ch. III, pp. 64–65:

> "Bank-notes … are never issued but on loan, and **an equal amount of notes must be returned into the bank whenever the loan becomes due**. Bank-notes never, therefore, can clog the market by their redundance … The banker has only to take care that they are lent on sufficient security, and **the reflux and the issue will, in the long run, always balance each other.**"

And the mechanism ranked above convertibility itself (pp. 67–68):

> "But it is not so much by convertibility into gold, as by the regularity of the reflux, that in the ordinary course of things any redundance of the bank-note issues is rendered impossible. … **It is the reflux, that is the great regulating principle of the internal currency.**"

> "So long as the Bank has a sovereign out on terminable securities at short dates, the channel of reflux must remain open, and **the Bank is utterly impotent to add a superfluous note to the currency.**" (p. 79)

Tooke's parallel, *An Inquiry into the Currency Principle* (1844), p. 69 — on why *government* paper is different:

> "It is quite clear that paper created and so paid away by the Government, **not being returnable to the issuer**, will constitute a fresh source of demand, and must be forced into and permeate all the channels of circulation."

The discriminator inside the Banking School is thus *returnability* — exactly the sink. Two honest notes from the full text: the tidy "three reflux channels" list (repayment / deposit / conversion) that the secondary literature recites appears nowhere as a list in Fullarton — the routes are real but scattered (pp. 64, 67, 80, 96–97); and Fullarton states the law's own boundary condition at p. 80 — cut off the discount channel and fresh issues *"would then be so much permanently added to the circulation."* The law of reflux is conditional on short-dated lending, by its author's own hand.

### Bank of England, 2014 — the reflux, confirmed by the issuer ✅

*Money creation in the modern economy* (McLeay, Radia, Thomas), Quarterly Bulletin 2014 Q1:

> "**Just as taking out a new loan creates money, the repayment of bank loans destroys money.**"

> "For example, suppose a consumer has spent money in the supermarket throughout the month by using a credit card … if the consumer were to pay their credit card bill in full at the end of the month, its bank would reduce the amount of deposits in the consumer's account by the value of the credit card bill, **thus destroying all of the newly created money**."

> "Banks making loans and consumers repaying them are the most significant ways in which bank deposits are created and destroyed in the modern economy."

The central bank stating Fullarton's law as operational fact, 170 years later. **Most of the money in a modern economy is a matched pair: a deposit, and a dated obligation to extinguish it.** That is a net-zero ledger with a clearing schedule. (What that grant does and does not concede: §5.)

### Wicksell 1898 ⚠️, Schumpeter 1911/1934 ✅ — credit against output that does not exist yet

Wicksell's pure-credit economy removes the metallic base entirely and lets the banking system's rate govern the price level (cited from the secondary literature; not retrieved here).

Schumpeter is now verified against the Opie translation (*The Theory of Economic Development*, Harvard 1934) — and the famous passages sit in **Chapter II, pp. 73–74**, not Chapter III as commonly cited:

> "It is always a question, not of transforming purchasing power which already exists in someone's possession, but of **the creation of new purchasing power out of nothing** — out of nothing even if the credit contract by which the new purchasing power is created is supported by securities which are not themselves circulating media — which is added to the existing circulation." (p. 73)

> "The banker, therefore, is not so much primarily a middleman in the commodity 'purchasing power' as a **producer** of this commodity. … he has himself become **the capitalist par excellence**. … He is the ephor of the exchange economy." (p. 74)

> "Access to the national dividend is usually to be had only on condition of some productive service previously rendered or of some product previously sold. This condition is, in this case, not yet fulfilled. **It will be fulfilled only after the successful completion of the new combinations.**" (p. 74)

And in Chapter III (pp. 101–102), the forward-facing instrument named exactly:

> "That kind of credit, which is designated by traditional opinion as abnormal, also creates claims to the social product, which, however, in the absence of past productive services could only be described as **certificates of future services or of goods yet to be produced**."

This is the cleanest **pull** statement in the entire literature. The credit is issued against a *future* that the credit itself brings into being — and Schumpeter closes the loop on the reflux side too: the entrepreneur "must not only legally repay money to his banker, but he must also economically repay commodities to the reservoir of goods" (p. 110), so the "credit inflation" is "more than eliminated" on completion. Nothing prior backs it; the loop produces its own cargo, then clears. Floorless-but-fruitful, in a 1911 Austrian's handwriting. *(Attribution note: Opie's phrase is "certificates of future services," not the "claim tickets" of popular quotation.)*

### Sargent & Wallace 1981 → Leeper 1991 → Sims & Woodford 1994 — the arithmetic gets unpleasant

*Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic* is the hinge: if fiscal policy moves first, then today's monetary tightness only buys **more** future money creation, and the price level today depends on the *whole expected future path* of surpluses. Leeper's active/passive taxonomy formalised which authority moves first; Sims and Woodford turned the government's intertemporal budget constraint into an equilibrium condition that prices the debt.

*(The Minneapolis Fed scan of Sargent–Wallace is image-only; no verbatim quotation is offered here rather than one reconstructed from memory.)*

### John Cochrane, 2023 — the family names itself ✅

The book draft is the most useful source in the file, because Cochrane states the position with no diplomatic padding.

The core:

> "The price level P₀ adjusts so that the real value of nominal debt equals the present value of real primary surpluses. I refer to (1.5) as the '**government debt valuation equation**.' **It works like stock**, in which the stock price adjusts so that the value of a given number of shares equals the present value of dividends."

> "Nominal government debt acts as a **residual claim to primary surpluses**. The price level is like a stock price."

The self-classification — and this is the sentence the framework must reckon with:

> "**We may view the fiscal theory as a backing theory of money. Dollars are valuable because they are backed by the government's fiscal surpluses.** … Dollars say 'This note is legal tender for all debts, public and private,' so you have the right to pay your taxes with dollars. **That right constitutes a backing.** Dollars backed by gold can be soaked up by giving people the gold in return for dollars. **Our dollars can be soaked up by taxes.**"

His own ancestry claim:

> "My story that money is valued because the government accepts its money in payment of taxes **goes back to Adam Smith himself**."

And the passage that decides how far the framework can go with him — quoted here in full, because an earlier draft's ellipsis dropped the sentence that carries the load:

> "**That we pay taxes in dollars is not essential.** The government could accept goods or foreign currency for tax payments and then sell those to soak up dollars. **What matters to price level determination is that the government uses tax revenues in excess of spending to soak up any excess dollars at the end of the day, and thereby maintain their value.** While not necessary, offering the right to pay taxes with money, or requiring such payment, is a useful way of **communicating and pre-committing** to fiscal backing."

Read precisely: what Cochrane declares inessential is tax-*payability* — the legal right to discharge tax in dollars. The *soak itself* — real resources withdrawn against the stock — he holds essential. The replaceable part is the communication device; the irreplaceable part is the backing. §5 takes him at exactly that word.

The frictionless claim, which is the live disagreement:

> "**Frictionless valuation is a property of a backing theory of money.** … Money may gain an additional value if it is specially liquid and limited in supply … But in a backing theory, a fundamental value remains when the liquidity value or supply limits disappear. **Entirely fiat money loses all value in that circumstance.**"

On the private-sink row of §1's table, he is equally direct:

> "Real bills' force for price stability is strong, because real bills are **salable assets**. If people don't want the money any more, they can have the real bills back. The government need not tax or borrow against future surpluses to soak up extra money. … Real bills insulate the backing of money for liquidity provision from government finances."

### Ross Starr, 1974 — the formal micro bridge ✅

*The Price of Money in a Pure Exchange Monetary Economy with Taxation* (Econometrica 42(1), 45–54; retrieved from Starr's own UCSD page and read in full). The unraveling, which he attributes to **Patinkin (Econometrica 1949)**, not to himself:

> "It is distressingly easy to find economies in which zero is the only price of money consistent with equilibrium. Consider an economy over time with a finite horizon. Near the terminal period the economy will be imbued with a *Weltuntergangsstimmung* … This argument can regress indefinitely so that the price of money is zero in all periods."

> "If we say that money is accepted because it is accepted, then we must agree that if money were not accepted then it would not be accepted because it would not be accepted."

The fix:

> "**Taxes can be used to create a demand for money independent of its usefulness as a medium of exchange, thereby ensuring that its price will not fall to zero.**"

— for which he quotes **Kaulla** (*Grundlagen des Geldwerts*, 1920, via Ellis): *"the note debt of the state stands against a corresponding quantity of demands by the state which can be unconditionally satisfied by the notes"* — a 1920 statement of the pre-committed node — and block-quotes Lerner 1947 at length. The formal lineage is thus **Patinkin 1949 (problem) → Kaulla/Lerner (verbal fix) → Starr 1974 (proof)**.

What the theorems actually say, and the fine print matters more than the folklore:

- **Theorem 1**: if taxes are small enough, the zero price of money is always a self-fulfilling equilibrium — the worthless outcome never leaves the menu on its own.
- **Theorem 2**: if the aggregate tax take falls short of the money stock *at every price*, zero is the **only** equilibrium price. An undersized sink supports nothing at all.
- **Theorem 3**: if the take strictly exceeds the stock (over the relevant range), an equilibrium exists and every equilibrium has money's price above the bound — and the existence proof constructs it exactly where **the nominal take crosses the stock**. Smith's ratio, proved.
- **Theorem 4**: the positive-price equilibria are Pareto efficient; the zero-price ones are not.

Two technical facts the synthesis must respect. First, the taxes are price-contingent lump sums payable only in money — and a tax fixed *purely in nominal terms* cannot satisfy Theorem 3's hypotheses; the leading case is a tax fixed in real terms, whose nominal magnitude blows up as money's price falls. The sink pins a level only when its real bite does not collapse with the price of money. Second, this is a model result, conditional on money being obtainable only by trade and the tax penalty binding — a theorem, not a law of nature.

An earlier draft called this paper "largely ignored." That is false in its own field: Goldberg's "tax-foundation theory of fiat money" (*Economic Theory* 2012 — the term is Goldberg's coinage, not a 19th-century doctrine) extends it dynamically; Shubik & Wilson (1977) and Dubey & Geanakoplos (1992) built the private-sink variants; Andolfatto teaches it. It is ignored by the *popular* chartalist literature, which is a different complaint.

## §3. What others said about them

**On the fiscal theory — the constraint-versus-equilibrium fight.** Willem Buiter, *The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level: A Critique* (Economic Journal 112, 2002), argues the FTPL "has feet of clay" because it confuses two different things: **budget constraints, which must hold identically**, and **equilibrium conditions, which hold only in equilibrium**. If the government's intertemporal budget constraint is a genuine constraint, it cannot also be the equation that determines the price level.

Marco Bassetto's *A Game-Theoretic View of the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level* (Econometrica 70(6), 2002) is the more constructive version: model the market structure explicitly, specify what happens **off** the equilibrium path, and you find that fiscal price determination *can* be recovered — but only under government strategies "more complex than the simple budgetary rules usually associated with the fiscal theory," and "the government budget constraint cannot be merely viewed as an equilibrium condition."

Kocherlakota & Phelan (1999) and Niepelt (2004) are usually filed as variants of the same complaint, but Kocherlakota–Phelan's is a different animal — see §3a, because their reading is not an objection to this document; it is prior art for it. The dispute has never fully closed; it is a live methodological fight about what a budget constraint *is*.

**On chartalism — the objections, graded.** The **size problem** is real and now has a theorem: taxes are a modest fraction of output while the currency circulates in vastly greater volume — and Starr's Theorem 2 is that problem stated as an impossibility result (an undersized sink supports *nothing*, in the finite-horizon model). The **extraterritorial objection** — dollars valued where no US tax is owed — is *weaker than this document first supposed*: §4.2's own bridge answers it, since the foreign holder is downstream of the sink through the trading network, not free of it. What actually refutes the constitution claim is the **sink-zeroed specimen** — the Iraqi Swiss dinar (§5), value with the obligation machinery entirely absent.

**On the Austrian reception.** No dedicated Misesian refutation of the fiscal theory surfaced in this pass. The school's engagement is indirect and revealing: Hummel (Liberty Matters, 2014) classifies FTPL as treating fiat money as Misesian *credit money*; Hülsmann, in the same exchange, absorbs the tax sink into the regression framework — all media of exchange need a nonmonetary value component, and fiat's is *"the threat of violence"* via tax enforcement and legal tender (⚠️ paraphrase per the fetched summary; verify wording). Note what that concedes: the school's own writers relocate the sink into the floor slot — **§5's diagnosis (police power in the place of the metal), made by the defendants themselves.**

**On the Banking School.** The reflux argument lost the legislative fight to the Currency School in 1844 and was treated as defeated for a century — then quietly vindicated on the operational question by the endogenous-money literature (Kaldor; Moore's *Horizontalists and Verticalists*, 1988) and finally by the Bank of England's own 2014 statement. **Losing the policy argument and winning the mechanism argument are different events, a century apart.** Cochrane's verdict inverts the classical one: "Classic doctrine: A real bills policy leads to an uncontrolled price level. Fiscal theory doctrine: A real bills policy is consistent with a determinate price level."

**On the natural experiments — three, running in three directions.** *Add the sink:* during the Civil War the US issued two papers side by side — Demand Notes, receivable for customs duties, and greenbacks, which were not. The Demand Notes held within ~10¢ of gold par while greenbacks fell to a 40¢ discount by March 1863 (Mitchell, *A History of the Greenbacks*, 1903; Koning, *Moneyness*, 2017). The sink itself priced, in the wild. *Zero the sink:* the Iraqi Swiss dinar (§5) — value for a decade with the obligation machinery entirely absent. *Decree a sink too small for the stock:* Japanese colonial Taiwan, c. 1895 — taxes payable only in Japanese money, intended to install the currency; people deferred their taxes, revenue collapsed, the policy was abandoned (reported in Li & Wright 1998). Priced when added, survived when deleted, impotent when undersized: that triangulation is the document's whole grading of the sink — a stabiliser with a capacity condition, not a source. (Friedman & Schwartz supply a fourth data point of the same shape: the US West Coast simply stayed on specie through the greenback suspension — cited by Aiyagari & Wallace as their size-threshold failure case.)

**On Barro.** *Are Government Bonds Net Wealth?* (JPE 1974) makes the future-tax channel explicit in households' heads: bonds are not net wealth because rational households capitalise the future taxes that will service them. The empirical literature has been unkind to strict Ricardian equivalence, but the mechanism survives as the reason a *future* obligation shows up in a *present* price — which is the entire trick the whole family performs.

## §3a. The mechanism school meets the sink

The framework's mechanism lineage — Ostroy, Townsend, Kocherlakota — says what money *is*: a record. The obligation family says what stabilizes it: a pre-committed future entry. Retrieving the primary texts shows the two literatures already met, twice, and both meetings must be credited.

### Kocherlakota 1998 — memory has no taxes, and no commitment ✅

*Money Is Memory* (JET 81, 232–251; the freer-spoken Staff Report 218 version alongside). The theorem, in his words:

> "The main proposition is that any allocation that is feasible in an environment with money is also feasible in the same environment with memory. … **Hence, from a technological point of view, money is equivalent to a primitive form of memory.**"

> "In an environment with memory, an **imaginary balance sheet** is kept for each agent. … In the monetary environment, **money is merely a physical way of maintaining this balance sheet.**"

Three facts about this paper matter here, and the third is the one nobody quotes.

First, its **scope**: the theorem is about *feasible sets* — what allocations a society *can* reach — not about selection or value. Kocherlakota says so: the equivalence holds "from a technological point of view"; the equilibrium descriptions are "misleading when taken as descriptions of the technological function of money"; and "this message is clearly not the last word." Nothing in the paper picks the monetary equilibrium over autarky. (There is even a calibration drift on record: the Staff Report claimed "a definitive and general response to the old question of *Why does money exist?*"; the JET version softens to "an advance over the usual justifications." The published self-assessment is the right one.)

Second, its **statelessness**, verified by grep over both full texts: the word *tax* appears **zero times** in either version; *government* appears zero times in the JET text and exactly once in the Staff Report (a seigniorage aside, cut from the journal). The framework's minimal ledger needs no sovereign, on the authority of its own source.

Third — the sentence that fuses the two literatures, from the Staff Report's Section V (cut from the JET version):

> "This threat of external force is not present in monetary transactions. **Money only provides memory, but bonds provide both memory and commitment.**"

There it is. Money works precisely where enforcement is *absent* — "societies that do not have any technology of enforcement" is his own framing of the friction. A future obligation is, in Kocherlakota's own taxonomy, **not more money — it is a bond**: a ledger entry *plus* the threat of external force. So the obligation family is not a rival description of the ledger. It is **the ledger plus the one ingredient the ledger deliberately lacks**, and that ingredient — commitment binding off the equilibrium path — is exactly what lets a sink terminate the unraveling (§4.3) that pure memory-money cannot survive in finite horizon (Starr). The tax sink is a bond wired into the police power; the rope is money in Kocherlakota's strict sense, memory with no bond anywhere. The framework's two central objects sit on the two sides of his cut section.

### Kocherlakota & Phelan 1999 — the selection reading, from inside the Fed ✅

The author of *Money Is Memory* co-wrote one of the canonical FTPL papers the following year (*Explaining the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level*, Minneapolis Fed Quarterly Review 23(4)), and its reading is — verbatim — the reconciliation this document arrived at independently in §4.4:

> "According to the fiscal theory of the price level, fiscal policy can be used to **select** which of these many paths actually occur. … the theory is **equivalent to giving the government an ability to choose among equilibria.**"

> "Thus, **at its very core, a non-Ricardian policy is an equilibrium rejection device.**"

And the value question, handled in a footnote with the quotation marks doing the philosophy:

> "Perhaps we should particularly note that this non-Ricardian policy **eliminates the worthless-money equilibrium** … This implies that a non-Ricardian policy can **"cause" money to have value.**"

Scare quotes theirs. Selection and termination granted; value-source held at arm's length by punctuation. **The three registers of §5, written inside the Federal Reserve in 1999** — this document's reconciliation is borrowed shoulder-height like everything else in it, and the credit goes here.

Their skepticism is aimed at something else entirely, and it lands on this document too, so state it: whether a government's policy is non-Ricardian is a claim about its behavior at **unobserved, off-equilibrium price paths**, so "the fiscal theory of the price level is not falsifiable," and whether governments *can* make such commitments is "a **religious, not a scientific, question**." Their conclusion is an honest credence, not a theorem: "One cannot 'believe in' the fiscal theory device and the monetarist device simultaneously. **We choose to believe in the latter.**" The bite for §5: the sink's *termination* service rests on commitment about paths that are never observed — the pre-committed/merely-expected asymmetry this document leans on is real in the model and, by Kocherlakota–Phelan's argument, undecidable in equilibrium data. That caveat now lives in §6, where it belongs.

## §4. Why the macro account explains the micro phenomenon

Four bridges. The first two are the ones usually named; the last two are the load-bearing ones.

**1. The aggregate is a sum of dated micro obligations, and it is delivered individually.** There is no such thing as the economy paying tax. There are individuals receiving dated bills. The macro identity is an accounting summary of a very large number of micro coercions, each of which arrives at one person with a date on it. Nothing has to travel from the aggregate down; the aggregate was assembled from the bottom in the first place.

**2. Backward propagation through the trading network.** This is the answer to "but *I* owe no tax." You do not need to be the obliged party. You need to be able to *reach* one by trade — and, in a connected market, everyone can. Innes says it exactly: the taxpayer "has to acquire his portion of the debt from some holder." That acquisition is a bid for the unit, and it propagates outward from every sink to every holder. **The obligation is enforced on one node; the demand it creates is felt at every node connected to it.**

And here is the part that matters: *that propagation is forward acceptance.* You take the tally because you expect someone to take it from you; the tax merely guarantees that at least one such someone exists and is legally unable to refuse. The macro obligation account and the micro acceptance account are not two theories. **They are the same loop, described from the terminus and from the interior.** (Lerner said it more sharply in 1947 — see his entry above; this bridge is borrowed, not built here.)

**3. The sink repairs the bubble's fragility to a last period — and what it transmits backward is acceptance, not a level.** The framework's own value lineage marks this as the unfinished business of the Samuelson/Tirole result: the pure-bubble equilibrium "leans on an infinite horizon to avoid backward-induction unraveling." A dated obligation is the device that blocks that unraveling — stated exactly. What is opinion-independent at the tax date is *acceptance*: the treasury is legally bound to take the tally at face value. What the discharge is *worth* still depends on the price level, which is everyone else's opinion. So the induction that runs back from the tax date carries acceptance, and only acceptance.

Starr (1974) prices both halves of this. In a finite-horizon economy, money with no sink unravels from the last period — his word is *Weltuntergangsstimmung*. Taxes payable in the token block the unraveling under a condition that cannot be dropped: **the take must cover the stock**. If aggregate tax liabilities fall short of the money stock at every price, zero is the *only* equilibrium price of money (Theorem 2); a positive price exists, and is pinned, exactly where the nominal take crosses the stock (Theorem 3). That is Smith's ratio, proved: the sink's *existence* buys acceptance; only its *capacity relative to the stock* buys a level. And because taxes recur, every holder is a finite chain of trades from a legally pre-committed acceptor — the infinite relay of merely-expected strangers becomes finite chains to bound nodes. The sink does not make the horizon finite; it makes the equilibrium robust to the horizon being finite, which the pure bubble is not. In the infinite-horizon formalism the terminal condition is a transversality condition, not a backward induction — Cochrane says so in as many words — and every formal result that pins the price *level* needs a nominal rigidity the obligation itself does not supply: a predetermined nominal stock confronting a sink whose real bite does not scale with prices (Cochrane's real surpluses against fixed nominal debt — his own example: a proportional income tax yields the real surplus s = τy, invariant to P; Starr's token-take against the token-stock; Ljungqvist–Sargent's real taxes payable in money, which with no initial nominal stock pin nothing). An obligation that scales one-for-one with the price level guarantees an acceptor at every date and anchors no level at all. **The obligation family supplies the terminal-shaped node the framework's forward relay lacks — as a selection device.** Which is bridge 4, with the theorems now attached.

**4. The obligation is a common-knowledge device, not a value source.** This is the reconciliation, and Cochrane hands it over himself. Tax-payability is "**not essential**" — the state could accept goods or foreign currency and sell them to soak up dollars. What the tax right *does* is serve as "a useful way of **communicating and pre-committing** to fiscal backing." Communicating and pre-committing. A tax law is public, dated, uniform, and enforced: it is about the most efficient generator of *common knowledge of acceptance* that a society has ever built. That is not a rival to the framework's selection mechanism. **It is the framework's selection mechanism, implemented in statute.** The search-theoretic school proved the micro version, and the proof carries its own correction to the tax story ✅ (both papers retrieved). Li & Wright (1998) put government agents inside a Kiyotaki–Wright economy as a fraction γ of the population committed to exogenous trading rules, and prove selection theorems of a single form: **if and only if γ exceeds a threshold**, a policy of always accepting the money eliminates the nonmonetary equilibrium, guarantees the universal-acceptance equilibrium uniquely — "even if the Π=1 equilibrium does not exist without government intervention" — and, run in reverse, a big government's *refusal* demonetizes what it refuses. Aiyagari & Wallace (1997) get existence from acceptance alone ("Given that government agents always accept fiat money … there always exists a monetary steady state") plus the same size logic, and note the policy's real *cost*: favoring money forgoes beneficial barter trades. Two footnotes to keep the attribution honest: the mechanism in both papers is guaranteed acceptance **in ordinary trade, not taxes** — Li & Wright say so ("Although we do not have taxation explicitly in our model") — and their historical case is a pure tax-decree that *failed*: Japanese colonial Taiwan, c. 1895, taxes payable only in Japanese money, "people deferred paying taxes, decreasing revenue by so much that the policy had to be abandoned!" The state was too small a participant in the local economy. Size is the threshold; the tax is one way for a state to be big at the point of acceptance, not a magic word.

(Two services, not one: the same statute is also the relay's terminus — bridge 3. Selection says why *this* money is held; termination says why the induction does not unravel. §5's refusal denies the sink only the third register: value-source.)

## §5. Where this lands against the framework

**Three grants.**

- **Knapp is right about the unit, and the framework should take him whole.** The unit of account is a legal artefact. He never claimed more, and the canon's Quirk 3 was never aimed at him.

- **The reflux row is the framework's own clearing discipline, at bank scale — Obligated, not Balanced.** Fullarton's law and the BoE's "repayment of bank loans destroys money" describe a net-zero ledger with a dated clearing schedule and no sovereign in it. By the canon's own frame the discipline is **Obligated** — *clears on schedule, with penalty* (contract, collateral, foreclosure — §1's own enforcement column) — set inside a Value-mode contract at formation (the interest is the price); nothing here nets over a window between peers. And say precisely what is being granted, because the recension refuted a credit theory and this row must not hand it back. The matched pair puts the debt on the **issue side, not in the essence**. The borrower's dated obligation is Q2 supply-governance — issuance disciplined by a scheduled un-issuance, self-scaling, decentralised, no dial-holder — and a private sink for selection (§1, row 2). The deposit-holder's value remains Q1 forward acceptance, and the pair demonstrably decouples: deposits stay whole when the matched loan sours — the depositor holds no claim on the borrower; the bank does — which is exactly what a value-layer credit theory forbids. Nor is the pair the recension's failure case. "Debt is what happens when settlement cannot be made thin enough, public enough, or timely enough" is scoped to **residual debt** — the unsettled residue of a trade that wanted to clear now, the thing the medium dissolves. A dated loan is **contractual credit** — a priced intertemporal trade, the deferred-payment function in its own instrument, a product the medium enables rather than a settlement it failed. Money remains debt's antidote in the first sense while most of its modern supply is issued through the second. The framework has been describing the mechanism without claiming the macro evidence that supports it.

- **The sink supplies a terminal condition the forward relay otherwise lacks — where a sink exists.** Per §4.3, and the claim is formal, not decorative: Starr (1974) proves it in finite-horizon general equilibrium, where taxes payable in the token are exactly what keep its price off the zero that end-of-horizon unraveling otherwise forces — a taxpayer short of the unit must bid real goods for it or bear the penalty, so a zero price cannot clear. State the terminus class honestly. Formal termini come in two families and only two: **floors** (backward: a direct use — Mises's route, and the industrial-use engine) and **sinks** (forward: a pre-committed acceptor — statute for the tax, contract for the loan repayment and for a platform's redemption commitment; private law is law too). **Speculation is neither.** Under common knowledge it cannot stop a finite backward induction — it is the thing that unravels — and it supports value only as *selection on the unterminated infinite-horizon branch*, where the worthless equilibrium stays available. So the gap the value lineage flags is closed for sink-equipped monies and stands open, deliberately and visibly, for sink-less ones: Bitcoin runs on selection without termination, and that is the formal name of its fragility — a prediction the framework should own out loud, not smooth over. This grades the engines without contradicting Quirk 3: anchor type still does not matter to the *value-source* (Q1 — floorless-but-fruitful in every case), but it does matter to the *robustness of the relay*. No single terminus is necessary; and yet a money whose network contains no member of {floor, sink} holds value only as an unselected bubble that its bootstrap engine must continuously re-select.

**One refusal, and it survives contact — restated so it cannot be turned back.** The value claim still fails, but not for the reasons the first draft gave. Not replaceability: a device replaceable only by other members of the same soak-class would leave the *class* as source — and Cochrane's concession is narrower than it reads, for what he calls inessential is tax-*payability*, while the soak itself he holds essential ("what matters to price level determination is that the government uses tax revenues in excess of spending to soak up any excess dollars at the end of the day, and thereby maintain their value"). And not Buenos Aires: §4.2 already answered it — the sink's bid propagates to every connected node, so the foreign holder is downstream of the sink, not free of it. The refusal rests where the framework's refusals always rest: on the specimen with the ingredient zeroed. The **Iraqi Swiss dinar** circulated for a decade (1993–2003) after its own issuer demonetized and repudiated it — "backed by no formal government, central bank, nor any law of legal tender" (King 2004) — receivable nowhere, redeemable by no one, its supply fixed by the accident of missing printing plates. Its late appreciation tracked the *odds* of a future re-monetization: a merely-expected terminus doing precisely the work the pre-committed one was said to monopolize. Bitcoin is the further case, with no sink anywhere in its network. Zero the sink and value survives; therefore the sink was never the source — the tax is to the relay what gold's ornament was to the rope: a confound, load-bearing-looking, removable. What the sink *is*, is the strongest selection-and-termination device ever built. Keep three registers separate and the whole document falls into place: **value-source** — floorless-but-fruitful, in every case; **selection** — the sink is the best device anyone has built, and not the only one; **termination** — the sink is the only *forward* terminus, and a money with no terminus at all rides the unterminated relay and inherits its fragility.

**One live disagreement, which should not be smoothed over.** Cochrane claims **frictionless valuation**: "a fundamental value remains when the liquidity value or supply limits disappear. Entirely fiat money loses all value in that circumstance." The framework says the opposite — that value *is* the frictions, the marginal trades unlocked, Simmel's distance overcome, Lagos–Wright's derived liquidity service. Cochrane's position is Mises's argument rotated to face forward — with the difference that Mises's anchor is genetic and dischargeable while Cochrane's binds at every date: pure fiat cannot hold value without an anchor, and the anchor is the surplus stream rather than the commodity.

The framework's answer has to be the same in both directions. **The backing is denominated in the thing it backs.** The primary surpluses are collected *in dollars*; the residual claim is a claim on dollars. Cochrane's escape — the state could collect goods instead and sell them — is real, but it relocates the anchor to the state's coercive capacity to extract *real resources*. At which point the fiscal theory has the metallist's exact structure with the sovereign's police power in the place of the metal: **an impersonal substance, prior to and independent of acceptance, into which the token can be cashed.** That is a floor. It is a well-defended floor with a large empirical literature behind it, and the framework denies it — but it should be denied out loud, as a disagreement with a serious rival, not absorbed as a friendly amendment. (The Austrian reception in §3 is the back-handed confirmation: Hülsmann relocates the same sink into the same floor slot from the other side.)

## §6. What is not settled

- **Whether the terminal condition is doing real work or is a modelling convenience.** Buiter's objection is unresolved: if the intertemporal budget constraint must hold identically, it cannot also determine the price level. Bassetto shows a version can be recovered but needs off-path government strategies far more elaborate than the story tells.
- **The size problem, quantitatively.** Smith framed it as a ratio; Starr's Theorem 2 states it as an impossibility — in the finite-horizon model, a sink smaller than the stock supports nothing at all. Nobody in the popular literature computes the ratio for an actual economy. The framework's B3 axis (symmetrically-known supply) is where that calculation belongs.
- **Whether the reflux sink alone is sufficient.** Rows 2 and 4 of §1 need no sovereign, and Shubik–Wilson 1977 / Dubey–Geanakoplos 1992 formalize the private terminus. If private dated obligations can carry the termination register by themselves, the state anchor becomes an empirical convenience rather than a structural necessity — a strong result for the framework, argued formally by others, not yet integrated here.
- **The termination register rests on off-equilibrium commitment, which is unobservable in equilibrium data.** Kocherlakota–Phelan's non-falsifiability argument (§3a) applies to this document's own pre-committed/merely-expected asymmetry: a statute's off-path bite is exactly the thing realized data can never exhibit. The asymmetry is real in the model; whether any actual sink carries it is, in their word, religious. Bassetto's off-path strategies are the constructive response; the question stays open.
- **The Swiss dinar is not a perfectly clean no-sink case.** King's own account has its late value tracking re-monetization odds — a *speculative future sink*. That fact is used above for the framework (a merely-expected terminus doing the pre-committed terminus's work), not hidden; but a hostile reader could run it the other way (the market pricing a sink-to-come), and the honest statement is that the specimen dissociates value from *present* obligation machinery, not from the *idea* of it.
