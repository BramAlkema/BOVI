# Argentina — Big Mac War & Dollarisation

## Why this is the framework's centrepiece case

Argentina is the most important worked example in the book because it shows **all four pieces** of the framework operating in the open:

1. **Adversarial mode forcing** by the state (suppressing measurements)
2. **Inflation as broken common knowledge** in real time
3. **Dollarisation as spontaneous unbundling** by ordinary people
4. **Personal indices defeating institutional manipulation**

It is also the home of **Doña Elena**, the framework's protagonist (see [unbundled personal stack](../practice/unbundled-personal-stack.md)).

## Part 1: The Big Mac War (2011)

The Argentine government under Cristina Fernández de Kirchner was facing inflation officially around 9% but actually 25%+. The Big Mac Index — *The Economist*'s long-running cross-country comparison — was making them look bad.

So **Guillermo Moreno**, the Secretary of Commerce, leaned on McDonald's: sell the Big Mac at a loss, half the price of every other burger on the menu, just to fake the index ranking.

McDonald's complied. They quietly took the Big Mac off menu boards and advertising so customers wouldn't notice the absurd price gap with the Quarter Pounder right next to it.

It worked for about a year.

In June 2012, *The New York Times* and *The Economist* exposed the suppression. Within days, the Big Mac price jumped **26% overnight** — a single-day "correction" as McDonald's gave up the charade. The whole thing collapsed.

### What the suppression reveals

The state cared so much about a stupid burger index that they ordered a multinational to *lose money on every sale* to fake the headline number. **Why?** Because they could not afford for people to be able to compare prices honestly. The index was dangerous to them precisely because it worked.

This is the framework's argument for personal indices in one episode: institutional measurements can be suppressed; personal ones cannot. The state can pressure McDonald's. It cannot pressure your notebook.

## Part 2: The Milei reversal (2026)

Under President Javier Milei's "crawling peg" policy (managing the official exchange rate), Argentina has flipped to the *opposite* extreme. The country is now the **second most expensive Big Mac in the world (~$7.37)**, behind only Switzerland.

This reflects the real appreciation of the peso under managed exchange — the currency is artificially strong against the dollar while local prices have remained high, inflating the dollar-denominated cost in international comparison.

Same currency, same country, opposite distortion. **Both directions are tells.**

### What both directions reveal

- **Cheap Big Mac (Kirchner)** = state hiding inflation
- **Expensive Big Mac (Milei)** = state propping up the currency

Either way, the burger talks. The framework's claim: *trust the burger, not the policy press release.*

## Part 3: Dollarisation as spontaneous unbundling

Argentina has been in an ongoing currency crisis for decades. Ordinary Argentinians have responded by spontaneously unbundling — exactly what the framework recommends, but driven by survival, not theory.

The Argentinian unbundled stack:

| Function | Instrument |
|---|---|
| Tax-currency floor | Pesos (held only as long as required) |
| Daily transactions | Pesos converted just-in-time |
| Store of value | USD cash (mattress), USDT on phones, real estate, Mercado Libre savings |
| Long-term contracting | Rent and big purchases denominated in dollars |
| Black-market currency exchange | The *cueva* — informal exchange shop above a clothing store, runs the "blue rate" |

This is the framework's recommended architecture, in widespread daily use, by people who have never read the framework. **They figured it out by necessity.**

## The cueva

A *cueva* (literally "cave") is an informal currency exchange. Nondescript door, back room, a man with a cash drawer. Everyone changes their dollars at the rate the state pretends doesn't exist.

The cueva is interesting because it inverts the standard "legal vs illegal" intuition. From a Western reader's perspective, the cueva looks illicit. But:

- The state's *official* market enforces an exchange rate nobody believes
- The cueva's rate is what the actual market clears at
- The cueva is a *less coerced* exchange than the official one

The state's "legality" and the cueva's "illegality" do not map onto consent. See [consent axis](../core/consent-axis.md).

## What this case teaches the framework

1. **Personal indices beat institutional ones** — the Big Mac War proves it empirically
2. **Suppression of measurement is itself the most reliable signal** — what the state hides is what it can't afford to admit
3. **Dollarisation is forced unbundling** — not ideology, survival
4. **The local currency reduces to its Obligated floor** when it fails — held only for tax
5. **Both inflation and artificial-strength are tells** of currency manipulation
6. **The framework was built by people like Doña Elena**, not by economists — and Argentinians are case-zero
