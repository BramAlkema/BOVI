# Special-Purpose Money

## Definition

**Money's functions are separable, and through most of history they were carried by different objects in the same society.** All-purpose money — one instrument doing exchange, account, payment and storage at once — is the unusual case, not the natural one.

This is the instrument-side pedigree for the framework's [unbundling thesis](../core/unbundling-thesis.md). It matters because unbundling is easily mistaken for a heterodox provocation. It is not: the separability claim appears in the founding text of the four-function list, and was the settled anthropological position for decades.

## Jevons said it first, in the text that established the list

W. S. Jevons, *Money and the Mechanism of Exchange* (1875), ch. III, having enumerated the four functions:

> "We might certainly employ one substance as a medium of exchange, a second as a measure of value, a third as a standard of value, and a fourth as a store of value."

His cases: in Elizabeth's reign silver was the common measure of value while gold served large payments, and 18 Eliz. c. VI (1576) required *corn* as the standard of value for college leases. He also described gold acquiring its functions in sequence rather than together.

So the framework's position is a **recovery**, not a heresy. The welding of the four functions into one instrument is a later teaching convention, not Jevons's claim.

## Polanyi's money-uses

Karl Polanyi ("The Semantics of Money-Uses," 1957) treated money as quantifiable objects put to distinct **uses** — *payment*, *standard* (accounting), and *exchange*, with hoarding subordinate — and argued these were **separately institutionalised**. One object discharged payments (bridewealth, wergeld, fines, tribute), another served as standard, a third circulated in exchange. All-purpose money fuses them; that fusion is historically exceptional.

George Dalton ("Primitive Money," *American Anthropologist* 1965) systematised the contrast and supplied the vocabulary still in use: **special-purpose** versus **general-purpose** money — objects performing monetary work only in circumscribed ways, restricted to particular people, occasions and relationships.

Paul Bohannan's Tiv material (1955, 1959) is the ethnographic exhibit: three ranked spheres — subsistence, wealth/prestige (brass rods, cattle, cloth), and rights in persons — with brass rods acting as a standard of value *inside their sphere only*. Exchange within a sphere was *conveyance*; across spheres, *conversion*.

## What was abandoned, and what survived

The framework should borrow this carefully, because parts of it were dismantled.

**Abandoned:**
- **The evolutionary story** — special-purpose money as a primitive stage superseded by all-purpose money.
- **The primitive/modern dichotomy.** Jacques Melitz ("The Polanyi School of Anthropology on Money," *American Anthropologist* 1970) attacked it by arguing that **modern money is special-purpose too**. Note the direction: this *reinforces* separability while destroying the dichotomy.
- **Strong sphere-boundedness.** Jane Guyer (*Marginal Gains*, 2004) showed Tiv prestige goods circulated as currency outside the community; the restriction was a means of retaining control, not an incommensurability barrier. Akin & Robbins (*Money and Modernity*, 1999) found Melanesians did not collapse into a single sphere on contact with general-purpose money — they built *new* spheres to channel it.

**Survived, and is now background rather than controversy:** the separability of the money-uses itself. Guyer's own later work proceeds from the premise that the classic four functions are disaggregated, in contemporary systems as much as historical ones.

**The modern successor** is Akinobu Kuroda ("What is the complementarity among monies?", *Financial History Review* 2008): most people through most of history dealt with **concurrent** currencies whose coexistence was functional rather than accidental — one money did what another could not. This is the separability claim without the evolutionary baggage, and it is the citation to prefer.

Independently, Philip Grierson (*The Origins of Money*, 1977) derives the money **of account** from wergeld compensation tariffs, arguing a thing can serve as standard of value without circulating — separability argued from numismatics and legal history rather than ethnography.

## What this licenses, and what it does not

**Licensed:** that the four functions are separable; that unbundled monetary arrangements are historically normal; that asking *which use does this instrument serve* is a sounder question than *is this really money*.

**Not licensed:** any claim that a given relational mode "owns" a given monetary function. Fiske's models and Polanyi's money-uses are two independent typologies — one partitions relationships, the other partitions uses of an instrument. A correspondence between them is available and interesting, but it is **the framework's own contribution**, not a finding in either literature. See [Fiske](fiske-relational-models.md) and `MODE-FUNCTION-MAPPING-PLAN.md`. The framework's candidate for that correspondence — required resolution per discipline, with pre-registered kill conditions — is `../../SCALE-LADDER.md`, currently under adversarial specimen test.

## The worked case

[Rai stone money of Yap](../cases/rai-stones-yap.md) is a specimen of exactly this structure: rai discharged prescribed obligations — war indemnities, ransom, compensation, tribute — while pearl-shells carried small change, and other valuables (*yar*, *gau*, *mbul*) served their own uses, with known exchange rates between them. One society, several monies, each fit to a discipline. Rai's indivisibility, which looks like a defect on a Value-mode rubric, is a qualification for the obligation-discharging job it actually did.
