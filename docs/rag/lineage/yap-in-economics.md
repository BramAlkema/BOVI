# Yap in Economics — how one ethnography became everyone's parable

## Definition

The reception history of the Rai stones: a single 1910 travel ethnography, excerpted and re-purposed for over a century, with each author's polemic rewriting the details. Documented case by case in Jo Lindsay Walton, "Bitcoin and stone money" (*Finance and Society* 8(1), 2022), the one systematic study of the chain. What the record actually shows about Yap itself is in [the case file](../cases/rai-stones-yap.md); this note tracks what happened to the story.

## The chain

**Furness (1910).** William Henry Furness III visited Yap in 1903 and published *The Island of Stone Money*. His own thesis, seldom carried forward, was a **labour theory of value** — the stones as "truly a representation of labour as the mined and minted coins of civilisation." The famous sunken stone is a single informant's tale, flagged as such in his first five words ("My faithful old friend, Fatumak, assured me"), about a stone nobody living had seen, lost "a few hundred feet" offshore two or three generations earlier. The monetary moral — the comparison to Treasury silver traded on printed certificates — is Furness's addition, not the Yapese's.

**Keynes (1915, 1930).** The *Economic Journal* excerpted three pages of Furness in 1915, dropping the other Yapese monies. In *A Treatise on Money* (1930) Keynes introduced the **earmarking** framing — and placed the story on the wrong island (Rossel), invented the "richest man in the island" detail, and converted Furness's explicit "without so much as a mark to indicate the exchange" into its opposite, transfer *by* marking.

**Friedman (1991).** "The Island of Stone Money" made the case canonical, pairing the German black-cross episode with the 1932–33 Bank of France gold earmarking at the New York Fed. His narrow thesis — the operative content of a monetary arrangement is shared convention, and the sophisticated case is no more rational than the "primitive" one — is defensible. His details drifted: the sunken stone moved from a few hundred feet offshore to "some 100 or so miles away," in rhetorical parallel with gold "3,000 miles away," and textual evidence suggests he worked from Keynes's excerpt while citing Furness's pages.

**The textbooks.** Walton documents the fabrications directly against their cited sources: Mankiw invented a phase in which stones "were conventionally moved around with each transaction"; Miles & Scott invented a paper currency backed by rai; Amacher generalised the one sunken stone to a standing practice; Ruffin & Gregory invented fractional shareholding in stones. None of these appears in any ethnography.

**The bitcoiners (2014–).** Rai as "the first blockchain" — a distributed, immutable, hard-capped ledger. The scholarly version (Fitzpatrick & McKeon 2020) is more guarded than its popularisers, itself cataloguing divisibility, uniformity, anonymity and distribution as points where the analogy fails; the popular version inherits every drifted detail above and adds a supply cap that Yap never had — the stock expanded roughly tenfold once Western ships relaxed the transport constraint.

## Two dating corrections

Popular accounts date rai to two millennia. The archaeology (Fitzpatrick 2002) supports intensive quarrying over the last few centuries, with a tentative origin around AD 1400; the older radiocarbon dates at the Palauan quarry sites are, in the excavator's own judgement, probably unrelated to stone money. And the O'Keefe discount — mass-produced stones supposedly marked down as inferior — is not supported: Furness cites O'Keefe's trade as proof that new stones were accepted on equal terms, and Kubary recorded prices holding; what the record does show is aggregate expansion and falling per-stone value.

## The pattern

Every link in the chain reads Yap as a curiosity that illuminates *our* money — fiat, earmarking, convention, blockchain — rather than as a monetary institution on its own terms. The framework's posture runs the other way ([the case file](../cases/rai-stones-yap.md)): the thing requiring explanation is the reader's assumption that money must travel, divide, and price.
