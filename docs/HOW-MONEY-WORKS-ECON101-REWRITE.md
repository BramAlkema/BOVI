# Chapter 24 — How Money Works

## A shared ledger for unfinished exchange

> **Adaptation note.** This is a critical rewrite of [Chapter 24, “The Nature and Creation of Money,” in *Economics Principles* v2.0](https://2012books.lardbucket.org/books/economics-principles-v2.0/s27-the-nature-and-creation-of-mon.html). Banking mechanics have been updated using central-bank sources listed under Further reading.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish subjective value, a market price, and a monetary unit.
2. Explain money as a sufficiently reliable ledger of unfinished exchange.
3. Explain why monetary coordination is intersubjective without turning private valuations into one shared value.
4. Identify the minimum technical requirements of a monetary ledger.
5. Compare gold, fiat currency, and Bitcoin by their supply-governance technologies rather than by “intrinsic value.”
6. Separate the four jobs conventionally bundled as the “functions of money.”
7. Classify cash, deposits, payment cards, credit, and financial assets.
8. Use balance sheets to explain how commercial-bank lending creates deposits and repayment destroys them.
9. Explain why monetary aggregates and the textbook money multiplier are models and policy conventions, not the essence of money.
10. Ask who governs a monetary system, who benefits from its rules, and where tax-like extraction may be hidden.

## Start up: a rope in the middle of the market

Imagine four villagers: Adam makes tools, Oz repairs them, Bea prepares hides, and Kai catches fish. They already exchange in several ways. They give to family, return favours, obey communal duties, bargain with strangers, and remember promises. We do not need to pretend that they first lived in a pure barter economy. The rope in this story is a teaching device, not a claim about the historical origin of money.

Place a rope in the middle of the market and give each person an arm of it. Everyone can inspect the marks. No one may alter a mark unseen.

When Adam gives Oz an axe and receives nothing immediately, the group records the agreed settlement price: Adam receives six marks and Oz gives up six. When Oz later repairs Bea’s spear, Oz receives four and Bea gives up four. The balances after four trades might look like this:

| Trade | Adam | Oz | Bea | Kai |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Adam gives Oz an axe for 6 | +6 | -6 | 0 | 0 |
| Oz repairs Bea’s spear for 4 | +6 | -2 | -4 | 0 |
| Bea gives Kai a hide for 5 | +6 | -2 | +1 | -5 |
| Kai gives Adam fish for 3 | +3 | -2 | +1 | -2 |

Eighteen marks of exchange have occurred, but the group need only carry four net positions. Adam has given three more marks’ worth than he has received. Bea has given one more. Oz and Kai have received more than they have given. The entries sum to zero.

The rope did not produce the axe, repair the spear, prepare the hide, or catch the fish. It did something else: it allowed each act of giving to be separated from the moment, person, and object through which the giver would later receive. It compressed several private obligations into one public settlement surface.

The rope is not the goods. It is not their value. It is shared memory of unfinished exchange.

That is our entry point:

> **Money is a sufficiently reliable ledger of who has given and not yet received.**

Sometimes that ledger literally contains names and balances. Sometimes a portable bearer object—a coin or note—embodies a transferable position. Sometimes the entries sit in a bank database or a distributed digital ledger. The substrate changes. The coordination problem does not.

The standard definition—“anything widely accepted as a medium of exchange”—remains a useful test for whether something is functioning as money. But it describes the visible outcome, not the mechanism underneath it. The ledger definition explains what the accepted medium does.

## 24.1 Value, price, and money are different things

Money is usually taught before these three concepts have been separated. The result is a hidden category error: a price is treated as if it were a measurement of value, and the money used to quote the price is treated as if it contained value.

Start with a simple sale.

Lena would pay at most €70 for a pair of shoes. Rafael, who owns the shoes, would accept any amount above €50. They settle at €60.

What has the market discovered?

It has not discovered that the shoes are “really worth” €60. Lena preferred the shoes to €60; Rafael preferred €60 to the shoes. Their valuations did not become equal, merge, or collapse into an objective value. If both parties valued the two sides identically, there would be no gain from exchanging them.

The €60 is a **price**: a public record of the exchange ratio at which these two parties, under these conditions, agreed to settle. A different buyer, seller, time, place, bargaining position, or set of alternatives could produce a different price.

This gives us three distinct concepts:

| Concept | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Value** | A person’s context-dependent ranking of one option against another | A substance inside an object |
| **Price** | A recorded settlement ratio agreed in a particular exchange | A measurement of everyone’s value |
| **Money** | The accepted ledger used to carry settlement positions between exchanges | The value being measured |

A monetary unit standardizes the language in which offers and accounts are expressed. It makes prices comparable as numbers. It does not turn the experiences, needs, constraints, or preferences behind those numbers into one common quantity.

### Heads up: public does not mean objective value

A posted price can be an objective fact in the modest sense that everyone can read the same number. The completed transaction can also be an objective event. Neither fact implies an objective value behind the price.

Markets discover **executable terms**. They do not discover what a thing is worth in itself.

## 24.2 Money is intersubjective

A valuation belongs to a subject. A monetary system exists between subjects.

You accept euros not because every euro user values euros equally, but because you expect enough other people and institutions to accept them later. They accept them partly because they expect the same of you and others. Each participant has a private reason; the network depends on mutually recognized expectations.

That is what **intersubjective** means here. It does not mean that society possesses one giant mind or one shared valuation. It means that people coordinate their actions around expectations they know others also recognize.

The distinction is crucial:

- Valuation remains subjective.
- Monetary acceptance is intersubjectively coordinated.
- A price is a public settlement result.

Money therefore resembles language. The word *tree* works because speakers use it according to sufficiently shared conventions. That does not make the sound intrinsically tree-like, nor does it make every speaker feel the same way about trees. Likewise, a monetary unit works because enough participants recognize the ledger and its rules. That does not put value inside the token.

This is why a purely physical inspection cannot determine whether something is money. A gold coin on an uninhabited island has the same mass and chemistry as one in a busy port, but not the same monetary role. A perfectly valid Bitcoin key is not useful at a shop that will not accept Bitcoin. A banknote from a dead currency may retain paper, ink, and collector interest while losing its monetary network.

Money is not imaginary. Ledgers, laws, balances, signatures, mines, servers, and payment terminals are real. The mistake is to infer from the reality of the infrastructure that monetary value is an objective property of the object.

## 24.3 What problem does money solve?

Introductory textbooks often begin with a society of barter. A shoemaker wants bread; the baker wants a chair; the chairmaker wants shoes. Money supposedly appears to solve the “double coincidence of wants.”

The coordination problem is real. The origin story is not required.

People have long handled unfinished exchange through gifts, remembered obligations, credit, tribute, communal provision, and authority as well as direct swaps. The anthropological record does not support a universal historical stage in which isolated individuals first bartered and then invented money. Credit and accounting records are older than coinage.

The more general problem is this:

> Giving and receiving do not naturally coincide in person, object, amount, place, or time.

Money makes those events separable.

Without a transferable settlement ledger, Adam’s axe creates a specific relationship: Oz owes Adam. With the rope, Adam can later receive fish from Kai, even though Kai never received the axe. The ledger converts a bilateral history into a position that can circulate through the group.

This service has two parts:

1. **Transaction service.** It lowers search, bargaining, memory, verification, and settlement costs for exchanges people already want to make.
2. **Option service.** It lets people specialize and give now without deciding exactly what they will receive later. Holding a balance preserves future choice.

The ledger does not create one objective amount of “social value.” It expands the set of feasible next moves. Different people value those additional options differently.

This is also why money can be understood as a primitive form of memory. If everyone possessed complete, trustworthy knowledge of every relevant contribution and obligation, some monetary records could be unnecessary. Real people and institutions do not possess such memory. A ledger externalizes it.

## 24.4 What must a monetary ledger be able to do?

Textbooks often list desirable physical properties such as durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity, and scarcity. Those properties describe particular implementations. The ledger view reveals the requirements underneath them.

### 1. Sufficient resolution

The ledger must express the settlements people need to make.

This is broader than the number of decimal places. A system may technically divide a unit into a billion pieces yet still fail small transactions because fees, minimum balances, delays, or conversion costs exceed the payment. Divisibility means usable economic resolution after friction.

### 2. Conservation during transfer

An ordinary transfer must not secretly create an extra claim. If Bea gives Kai five units, Bea’s position must fall as Kai’s rises. In a physical bearer system, conservation may be enforced because the same coin cannot remain in Bea’s hand and arrive in Kai’s. In a digital system, the ledger prevents duplication or double-spending.

This does **not** mean the total money supply must be permanently fixed. Authorized issuance and destruction can occur under known rules. Conservation means that a transfer itself cannot mint.

### 3. Authentication

The system must determine who is authorized to move a position. Coins use physical possession and anti-counterfeiting features. Banks use identity systems, signatures, passwords, institutional controls, and legal recourse. Cryptographic ledgers use keys and consensus rules.

No mechanism is perfect. It must be sufficiently trustworthy relative to the value and risk of the transaction.

### 4. Symmetrically known supply

Participants must be able to know, with sufficient confidence, how claims are created and destroyed and who has that authority.

**Known is not the same as fixed.** A transparent supply that expands by an auditable rule can satisfy this requirement. A nominally fixed supply with hidden side claims, opaque custodians, or unverifiable reserves may not.

Scarcity is one possible way to make uncontrolled issuance difficult. It is not the underlying requirement. The underlying requirement is protection against privileged, hidden dilution.

### The surrounding world still matters

These four requirements concern the ledger substrate. A working monetary system also depends on conditions supplied by the surrounding society:

- **Acceptance:** will counterparties take it?
- **Availability:** are there actually goods, services, and markets into which a balance can clear?
- **Enforcement:** when the system uses credit, what makes promises, redemptions, and adverse judgments effective?

A technically flawless ledger with no users is not useful money. A widely used ledger with secret issuance privileges is vulnerable to extraction. A well-governed currency in an empty market cannot buy what is unavailable.

## 24.5 Gold, fiat, and Bitcoin: three governance technologies

The conventional division between **commodity money** and **fiat money** rests on “intrinsic value.” Commodity money supposedly possesses it; fiat money does not.

That taxonomy should be retired.

Gold has objective physical properties: mass, conductivity, malleability, and chemical stability. People may value those properties for electronics, ornament, ritual, or status. None of those uses creates intrinsic economic value. Economic value still depends on what people want, what alternatives they have, and what others will exchange.

The same distinction applies to every monetary system. The substrate and its rules can be objective. Its monetary standing is intersubjective.

A more useful comparison asks how each system governs the ledger and its supply:

| System | Ledger substrate | Supply-governance technology | Institutions that keep it working |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gold money** | Assayed bearer pieces and custodial records | Geology, mining technology, refining, weighing, and minting | Miners, assayers, mints, merchants, vaults, courts, and states |
| **Fiat money** | Public cash, central-bank reserves, and commercial-bank deposits | Law, bank charters, monetary policy, regulation, taxation, accounting, and payment rules | Legislatures, treasuries, central banks, commercial banks, courts, supervisors, and payment networks |
| **Bitcoin** | A distributed transaction ledger controlled by cryptographic keys | Protocol rules, proof-of-work, network consensus, and software governance | Developers, miners, node operators, exchanges, custodians, holders, and surrounding legal systems |

Gold outsources part of supply governance to mining technology. Bitcoin outsources part to cryptographic protocol and mining economics. Fiat administers supply and settlement primarily through law and legally constituted institutions.

All three remain institutional arrangements. None escapes governance:

- Gold must be assayed, standardized, secured, and adjudicated.
- Bitcoin’s code must be interpreted, maintained, adopted, and connected to real-world markets.
- Fiat’s law must be administered by institutions whose promises, balance sheets, and decisions remain credible.

### Why one money rather than another?

Two questions are often confused.

**What service does money provide?** It carries settlement across otherwise disconnected exchanges and preserves future options.

**Why does this particular money get selected?** People expect others to accept it, and they expect the ledger’s rules to remain sufficiently credible.

Money can therefore be **floorless but fruitful**. It needs no prior commodity value underneath it. Once accepted, however, the ledger provides a real service by enabling exchanges and options that would otherwise be too costly or impossible. That service gives each user a reason—valued differently by each—to participate. This is a coordination equilibrium with a payoff, not collective make-believe and not a single social value.

Law can help select a fiat currency by denominating taxes, public payments, contracts, and court judgments in it. A state decree is therefore not irrelevant. But “the government says so” is incomplete: currencies can circulate outside the issuing state, and legally designated currencies can fail when their institutions lose credibility.

Gold’s nonmonetary demand, Bitcoin speculation, state taxation, or a platform’s existing user base can help a network cross its cold start. These are bootstrap mechanisms. They are not objective value being injected into money from underneath.

## 24.6 The four “functions of money” are four different jobs

Most textbooks say money has three or four functions:

1. medium of exchange;
2. unit of account;
3. store of value; and
4. standard of deferred payment.

These are real jobs. It does not follow that one instrument should perform all of them.

| Job | What it needs | A suitable tool might be |
|---|---|---|
| **Settlement medium** | Broad acceptance, low friction, speed, security, sufficient resolution | Cash, deposits, or a transaction token |
| **Unit or index** | A transparent and relevant comparison rule | A currency unit, wage index, price basket, or personal cost index |
| **Store of wealth** | A chosen mix of liquidity, risk, expected return, and resilience | A diversified portfolio, productive assets, insurance, or safe claims |
| **Deferred contract standard** | Explicit timing, indexation, risk allocation, and enforcement | A contract tied to an agreed index and settled through a payment rail |

Bundling the jobs creates conflicts.

- A good payment rail should circulate easily; a scarce speculative asset rewards holding.
- A useful long-term contract index should track the parties’ relevant costs; a transaction currency may move for unrelated reasons.
- A household’s wealth should usually be diversified; a payment balance should be liquid.
- A thirty-year promise needs explicit rules for changing conditions; a spot payment needs finality now.

A household could receive wages and make payments in euros, index a long-term contract to a transparent cost basket, and hold long-term wealth in a diversified portfolio. The payment instrument need not carry thirty years of stability and investment performance on its back.

The traditional functions list is therefore best read as a description of today’s **bundle**, not as a definition of money’s essence.

### Digital systems make unbundling practical

Manually operating four instruments for every decision would be exhausting. Software can translate between them: compare prices across units, convert only when settlement is needed, maintain personal indices, and check contract rules.

Users do not need to perform every calculation any more than drivers need to calculate every turn on a long journey. They do need to choose the destination and the constraints.

For a financial agent, those constraints might include:

- never expose more than a chosen amount to currency risk;
- prefer the cheapest compliant settlement route;
- preserve a minimum liquidity buffer;
- use my household’s cost index when comparing long-term offers;
- require approval before borrowing, investing, or sharing data.

Agent ownership matters. An agent owned by a lender, marketplace, or advertising platform may optimize the platform’s revenue while appearing to optimize the user’s finances. That principal–agent problem exists in both conventional and unbundled finance. A trustworthy system therefore needs a user-controlled mandate, inspectable rules, data portability, and meaningful exit.

## 24.7 What counts as money?

Money is not identical to the device used to move it, and wealth is not identical to money.

| Item | Is it money? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| **Notes and coins** | Usually, within their acceptance network | They are portable bearer records recognized for settlement |
| **A checking-account deposit** | Yes | It is a bank liability the customer can transfer in payment |
| **A debit card or check** | No | It is an instruction for moving a deposit |
| **A credit card** | No | It is a payment instruction joined to a short-term credit facility |
| **A government bond** | Usually not transaction money | It is a financial claim that normally must be sold or pledged before spending |
| **A house or share portfolio** | No | It is wealth, but not ordinarily accepted in settlement |
| **Gold or Bitcoin** | Context-dependent | It functions as money where people actually use and accept it as a settlement medium; elsewhere it functions mainly as an asset |

### Liquidity is a spectrum

**Liquidity** is the ease with which a position can be used for settlement without delay, loss, or substantial transaction cost. Cash and transaction deposits are highly liquid. A house is not. A government bond may be liquid in a financial market but still unusable at a grocery checkout.

This is why central banks publish monetary aggregates such as **M1** and **M2**. These are statistical buckets organized largely by liquidity.

In the United States, M1 currently includes currency held outside banks and highly liquid deposit balances. M2 includes M1 plus certain small time deposits and retail money-market-fund balances. The exact definitions have changed and can change again as regulations and payment technology change.

That fact is instructive. M1 and M2 do not reveal the timeless nature of money. They are operational measures designed for particular analytical and policy purposes. Always ask:

- What instruments are included?
- Which issuers created them?
- How quickly can they settle a payment?
- What risks or conversion costs are hidden by adding them together?

## 24.8 Banks create deposit money

Banks do several jobs. They operate payment accounts, assess borrowers, make loans, transform maturities, manage liquidity, and settle with other banks. But the familiar description of a bank as a warehouse that collects savers’ money and then lends it out is incomplete.

Most money used by households and firms in modern economies consists of commercial-bank deposits. A bank normally creates such a deposit when it makes a loan.

Suppose a bank approves a €1,000 loan. At origination, the bank records:

| Commercial bank | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---:|---:|
| New borrower loan | +€1,000 |  |
| New borrower deposit |  | +€1,000 |

The borrower records the mirror image:

| Borrower | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---:|---:|
| New bank deposit | +€1,000 |  |
| New bank loan |  | +€1,000 |

The bank did not move €1,000 from a particular saver’s account into the borrower’s account. It created a new deposit liability and acquired a matching loan asset. Both balance sheets expanded.

If the borrower pays a seller who uses another bank, the deposit moves to the seller’s bank. The two banks then settle between themselves using central-bank reserves or other settlement arrangements. The lending bank must be able to obtain the funding and liquid assets required to meet those outflows.

When the borrower repays principal from a deposit account, the bank reduces both the deposit liability and the loan asset. Deposit money is destroyed. Interest and fees are transfers of income to the bank; they are not the same accounting operation as destroying principal.

### Banks cannot create without limit

Deposit creation is constrained by:

- willing and creditworthy borrowers;
- expected profitability and funding costs;
- credit and default risk;
- bank capital requirements;
- liquidity and settlement needs;
- regulation and supervision;
- the interest-rate and collateral environment set partly by the central bank;
- the willingness of customers and other banks to hold or accept the bank’s liabilities.

A bank can create a deposit entry, but it cannot force the rest of the system to hold its liabilities at par indefinitely.

### Why the simple money multiplier misleads

Older textbook models begin with a new reserve deposit, assume banks repeatedly lend a fixed fraction of deposits, and calculate a maximum expansion using:

\[
\text{deposit multiplier}=\frac{1}{\text{required reserve ratio}}
\]

This can be a useful arithmetic exercise for a deliberately simplified fractional-reserve model. It is not a good causal description of modern bank lending.

In practice, banks commonly make lending decisions first and obtain the reserves and funding needed for settlement afterward, subject to the constraints above. Central banks in ample-reserves systems implement policy mainly through administered interest rates and the terms on which reserves are supplied, not by fixing a small quantity of reserves and waiting for banks to multiply it. The United States has applied a zero reserve-requirement ratio since March 2020.

The modern sequence is better summarized as:

> A qualified lending decision creates a loan and a deposit; regulation, capital, funding, settlement, risk, demand, and monetary policy constrain the process.

### What the central bank does

A central bank usually:

- issues currency and reserve balances;
- provides the settlement asset used between banks;
- influences monetary and credit conditions through policy rates and other tools;
- supports liquidity in emergencies;
- supervises or helps supervise financial institutions;
- protects the monetary unit’s institutional continuity.

Commercial banks create most transaction deposits. The central bank governs the settlement hierarchy and influences the conditions under which deposit creation occurs. Legislatures, treasuries, regulators, courts, deposit insurers, and payment systems are also part of the monetary constitution.

Fiat money is therefore not merely “faith.” It is monetary coordination administered through law.

## 24.9 From object finance to relational finance

The conventional picture treats money as a thing that carries an amount of value. Once that picture is accepted, finance appears to be the objective manipulation of quantities already inside things.

The relational picture keeps the numbers but changes what they mean.

- A €100 balance is an objective ledger entry, but its significance depends on the holder, time, place, available goods, legal regime, and reference basket.
- A 5% return is an objective nominal ratio, but whether it improves someone’s position depends on inflation, taxes, risk, timing, and alternatives.
- A €200,000 debt is an objective contract balance, but its burden depends on income, indexation, maturity, enforcement, and the borrower’s circumstances.
- A market price is observable, but it records a settlement under a particular distribution of information and bargaining power.

Calling this “Einsteinian finance” is an analogy, not a claim that economics follows the equations of physics. The point is that a financial quantity is incomplete without its frame.

The objective layer does not disappear. Balances, transfers, contracts, rules, and recorded prices should be exact and auditable. What disappears is the unsupported leap from an exact number to an objective value.

This is also where a user-aligned financial agent can outperform conventional optimization. Instead of maximizing a nominal balance in one default unit, it can compare outcomes across the user’s actual frames: household costs, time horizon, jurisdiction, risk limits, fairness commitments, and future options.

## 24.10 Money is a constitution, not just a convenience

Once money is understood as a governed ledger, distribution and power enter the chapter automatically.

Every monetary system must answer:

1. Who may create and destroy claims?
2. By what rule?
3. Who receives newly created claims first?
4. Who earns fees, spreads, seigniorage, or interest?
5. Who bears losses from default, inflation, fraud, or institutional failure?
6. Which price index defines “stability” or adjusts long-term contracts?
7. Which transactions are visible, reversible, censorable, or private?
8. What appeal or exit exists when the ledger is wrong?

These are not questions added to money from politics. They are part of how the ledger works.

Some transfers are explicit taxes authorized through public law. Others are **tax-like extractions** whose economic incidence is real but whose amount is not shown as a tax: an opaque foreign-exchange spread, privileged access to new issuance, a compulsory negative real return, a payment-network toll, or an index that systematically understates one group’s costs.

Calling such transfers “implicit taxes” can make their incidence visible, but the legal distinction should remain clear. A bank fee is not literally a tax merely because it reduces disposable income. The useful question is comparative: who pays, who receives, under which rule, and could the amount have been seen in advance?

A single national average can hide these effects. The same inflation rate, fee schedule, or interest-rate change lands differently on renters and owners, borrowers and creditors, young and old, urban and rural households. Relational analysis therefore uses the relevant reference frame rather than pretending one aggregate number describes everyone’s experience.

## Key takeaways

- Value is not intrinsic to an object. A person values options relative to circumstances and alternatives.
- A price is a public settlement ratio, not a measurement of objective or shared value.
- Money is a sufficiently reliable ledger of who has given and not yet received.
- Monetary coordination is intersubjective: people accept a ledger partly because they expect others to accept it.
- The ledger needs sufficient resolution, conservation in transfer, authentication, and symmetrically known supply. Known supply need not be fixed supply.
- Gold, fiat, and Bitcoin all depend on institutions. They differ mainly in how mining technology, cryptographic technology, and legal technology govern supply and settlement.
- Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of wealth, and deferred-payment standard are different jobs that can be unbundled.
- Bank deposits are money; cards and checks are instructions for moving or borrowing it.
- Commercial-bank lending creates a matching loan and deposit. Repaying principal destroys deposit money.
- M1 and M2 are changing statistical conventions. The simple reserve multiplier is a stylized model, not the general operating mechanism of modern banking.
- Exact financial numbers remain possible, but their economic meaning is frame-dependent.
- Monetary design always distributes authority, risk, access, and extraction. Governance is part of monetary theory.

## Key terms

**Authentication** — The process by which a ledger verifies who may authorize a transfer.

**Bank deposit** — A liability of a commercial bank to its customer that can commonly be transferred in payment.

**Conservation** — The rule that an ordinary transfer cannot create an additional claim.

**Fiat money** — Money whose base unit is issued and governed through a legal monetary order rather than redeemable for a named commodity; not money made valuable by decree alone.

**Intersubjective coordination** — Mutually recognized expectations through which separate people align their actions without sharing one valuation.

**Liquidity** — The ease of using or converting a position for settlement without substantial delay, loss, or cost.

**Money** — A sufficiently reliable ledger of who has given and not yet received.

**Monetary aggregate** — A policy or statistical grouping of monetary instruments, usually organized by liquidity.

**Price** — The exchange ratio at which parties settle a particular transaction.

**Resolution** — The smallest economically usable settlement a ledger can express after fees and other friction.

**Settlement** — The discharge or transfer of a monetary position under the ledger’s rules.

**Symmetrically known supply** — A condition in which participants can inspect or reliably infer the amount of claims and the rules governing their creation and destruction.

**Value** — A subject’s context-dependent ranking of one option relative to another.

## Review questions

1. Lena would pay up to €70 for shoes, Rafael would accept at least €50, and they trade at €60. What does the €60 price reveal? What does it not reveal?
2. Why can a price be publicly observable without being an objective measure of value?
3. In what sense is money intersubjective? Why does that not imply one shared social valuation?
4. What does the tally rope accomplish that a collection of private IOUs does not?
5. Why is the double-coincidence problem logically useful but insufficient as a historical origin story?
6. Explain the difference between fixed supply and symmetrically known supply.
7. Compare gold, fiat currency, and Bitcoin by their supply-governance technologies and institutions.
8. Why is a debit card not money? Why is the deposit it moves money?
9. Give one example in which a good store of wealth would be a poor payment rail.
10. Show the balance-sheet entries created when a bank makes a €5,000 loan.
11. What happens to deposit money when €1,000 of loan principal is repaid?
12. Why does the reserve-multiplier model not provide a general causal account of modern bank lending?
13. What is gained by separating a transaction medium from a long-term contract index?
14. What decisions should remain with a user when an agent automates financial routing?
15. Identify one tax-like monetary extraction. Who pays it, who receives it, and what would make it visible?
