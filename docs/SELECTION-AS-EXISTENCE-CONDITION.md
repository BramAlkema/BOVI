# Selection Is an Existence Condition — the free-entry problem

*A research deep-dive on the theory-side argument against pure-expectation money: if a valueless object can be money, anyone can make one, and the models stop producing an answer. Parked for a later weave; **nothing here has been woven into the book or the canon yet**.*

---

## The question

The framework's load-bearing scene is two people and a tally. The rope is valueless; it is accepted because each party expects the next person to accept it. Value is *floorless but fruitful* — nothing underneath, real cargo inside.

The standard way to license that scene is to point at a model in which expectation alone suffices: Samuelson's overlapping-generations construction, Kiyotaki and Wright's search economy. Money circulates because everyone believes it will. No substance, no decree, no sink required.

This document is about a challenge to that licence which does not attack the evidence at all. It grants the models and asks a question they do not ask: **how many candidate objects are there?**

---

## The finding in one line

> **Under free entry of valueless candidates, pure-expectation models stop predicting — and in at least one setting stop having an equilibrium at all. Whatever breaks the tie is therefore not an explanation layered on top of money; it is the condition under which "money" is a determinate object. Selection is prior to value, not downstream of it.**

---

## The argument, as stated

Goldberg puts it in a single paragraph, and the mechanism is a free-entry argument (2005, p. 15 — ✅ read):

> "In Goldberg (2003a) I show in a search model that these models lose most of their predictive power once a large number *n* of potential 'fiat moneys' are allowed to exist, because then there are 2ⁿ stationary equilibria, all with different combinations of 'fiat moneys' circulating. Prescott and Rios-Rull (2000) go further by pointing that in an overlapping generations model no monetary equilibrium exists under such conditions. This is because if I pick up, say, a useless nutshell and try to use it as 'fiat money' to buy goods from you, then your optimal response is to pick up a different nutshell, or a fallen leave, and try to use it as a different 'fiat money' to buy goods from the next generation."

Two distinct results, and the second is much stronger than the first.

**Indeterminacy (2ⁿ).** With *n* candidates the model admits every combination of circulating and non-circulating objects. It has not been refuted; it has stopped saying anything. A theory that permits 2ⁿ outcomes predicts none.

**Non-existence.** Prescott and Rios-Rull's setting has no monetary equilibrium under free entry. The reason is that the qualifying condition is **non-excludable**: if what makes an object money is that it is worthless and expected to be accepted, then the supply of qualifying objects is unbounded, and my rational response to your nutshell is never to accept it — it is to issue mine.

The usual models suppress this by assuming exactly one candidate. That assumption is doing far more work than it looks like it is doing.

---

## Why this is different work from the rest of the paper

Goldberg's famous sections are an **evidence** argument: your exhibits are contaminated, the Yap stones had aesthetic and legal and religious value, the wampum was jewelry (see [the Rai case](rag/cases/rai-stones-yap.md) and [Yap in economics](rag/lineage/yap-in-economics.md)). Those are answerable in principle by producing a cleaner specimen.

This is a **theory** argument, and no specimen answers it. Even granting a perfectly valueless object with no legal status anywhere, the model still cannot say why *that* object and not the ten thousand others lying around. It is the harder half of the paper and the half that almost nobody cites.

---

## What it does to the framework

The framework already holds that common knowledge of acceptance **selects** which money wins and does not create the worth — the value/selection/termination split recorded in [the future-obligation lineage](FUTURE-OBLIGATIONS-LINEAGE.md), and the reason `Starr` exists as a capacity condition rather than a price.

This argument **promotes that from a useful distinction to a necessary one.**

The comfortable reading of selection is: expectation makes money possible, and selection explains which candidate happens to win. Free entry says expectation alone does not produce a determinate object to select *among*. So a sink, a legal status, a focal point, a prior common knowledge — these are not tie-breakers applied to a field of viable candidates. Without one of them there is no field.

Three things follow.

1. **The cold-start problem has a mirror, and one mechanism must answer both.** Cold start asks how anything becomes money. Free entry asks why everything does not. A framework that answers only the first is half a framework.
2. **`Starr` is load-bearing in a way its own header understates.** It currently reads as a capacity check on a claim someone else made. Under this argument the sink is doing existence work, not just sizing work — which is a stronger reading than the contract presently asserts, and should not be written into it until the sources below are retrieved.
3. **The strongest form of the framework's own thesis gets sharper.** *Money is not a substance and not a decree; it is the object a population has been made to converge on.* Convergence is the phenomenon. Expectation is only its medium.

---

## The reply the framework has and these models do not

The 2ⁿ result depends on the candidates being **symmetric**. Nothing in the setup distinguishes one nutshell from another, so every subset is an equilibrium.

That symmetry is exactly what the repo's own structural claim denies. From [extraction and the modes](EXTRACTION-AND-THE-MODES.md): money has value because it is *"a network with winner-take-most pull"* — the medium that lowers the most friction gets the steepest pull and crowds out rivals. Under increasing returns to adoption the candidates are not interchangeable, tiny differences in initial adoption amplify, and the equilibrium set collapses toward a small number of focal survivors.

So the indeterminacy may be an artifact of modelling candidates as interchangeable, and the framework already says why they cannot be.

**Calibration.** "Winner-take-most pull" is a structural assertion in this repository, not a theorem, and this document must not dress it as one. The honest claim is that the framework has a *mechanism* to offer where these models have an *assumption*, and that closing the gap would require the formal work — which is precisely the sort of thing [the specification language](SPECIFICATION-LANGUAGE.md) exists to make possible.

## The experiment nobody meant to run

The nutshell argument was published in 2000 and describes the altcoin problem before altcoins existed. Free entry of valueless candidates then happened at enormous scale.

The outcome was neither 2ⁿ indeterminacy nor non-existence. It was **extreme concentration on a few focal points with an enormous near-zero tail** — which is the shape winner-take-most predicts, and not the shape either theoretical result predicts.

Two cautions, and they are not small. Prescott and Rios-Rull's result is about a specific overlapping-generations construction with its own assumptions; an outcome in a different setting neither confirms nor refutes it, and calling this a test would be an overreach of exactly the kind this repository's calibration record warns about. And crypto's focal points are not costless objects — proof-of-work, brand, exchange listing and accumulated common knowledge are all asymmetries the nutshell case lacks. Which is arguably the point: **free entry of *candidates* is not free entry of *focal points*.** That may be the whole resolution, and it is testable in principle.

It also places Bitcoin precisely. Its selection devices are focal — cap, first-mover common knowledge, hashrate — and it has **no sink**: κ ≈ 0 in `Starr`'s terms, since no obligation is denominated in it at scale (see [the Bitcoin case](rag/cases/bitcoin.md)). So it sits in the configuration this literature says is most fragile, and has not been fragile in the predicted way. That is a live question for the framework rather than a settled point in its favour.

---

## The twist worth keeping

Goldberg reads as the anti-fiat economist, and this argument looks superficially like a case for commodity money.

Follow it through and it is not. Look at what actually restores existence in his own account of the escape routes: commodity value, yes — but also legal tender status, tax obligation, prescribed ceremonial use, religious necessity, and forcing on buyers or sellers. Six of his ten grounds are institutional rather than material.

**The nutshell problem is solved by relations, not by stuff.** That is the framework's position, reached by an author whose rhetoric points the other way — which is the most useful kind of support, because it cannot be attributed to shared priors.

## Pre-registered kill conditions

Staked before retrieval, so the sources cannot be read to fit. Same discipline that killed [the scale ladder](SCALE-LADDER.md).

- **K1.** If Prescott & Rios-Rull's non-existence result turns on a feature specific to overlapping generations with no bearing on search or ledger settings, then "selection is an existence condition" is **too strong** and must be demoted to "selection is required for determinacy" — the 2ⁿ result only.
- **K2.** If Goldberg (2003a)'s 2ⁿ result already assumes symmetric candidates *and says so*, the reply in this document is not a contribution — it is a restatement of a limitation its author had already noted, and the section must say so.
- **K3.** If either paper shows the multiplicity survives increasing returns to adoption, the winner-take-most reply **fails** and the framework needs a different answer to free entry.
- **K4.** If the escape routes in the literature turn out to be predominantly commodity-value rather than institutional, the "twist" section is wrong and should be cut rather than softened.
