# Selection Is a Determinacy Condition — the free-entry problem

*A research deep-dive on the theory-side limit of pure-expectation money: if a valueless object can be money, anyone can nominate one, and an existence proof still does not tell us which candidate a population will adopt. The cold-start mirror has now been woven into Chapter 6, `FOUNDATIONS.md`, and the RAG architecture note; this file retains the full source audit.*

---

## The question

The framework's load-bearing scene is two people and a tally. The rope is valueless; it is accepted because each party expects the next person to accept it. Value is *floorless but fruitful* — nothing underneath, real cargo inside.

The standard way to license that scene is to point at a model in which expectation alone suffices: Samuelson's overlapping-generations construction, Kiyotaki and Wright's search economy. Money circulates because everyone believes it will. No substance, no decree, no sink required.

This document is about a challenge to that licence which does not attack the evidence at all. It grants the models and asks a question they do not ask: **how many candidate objects are there?**

---

## The finding in one line

> **Under free entry of valueless candidates, an existence proof stops short of prediction. It can show that monetary equilibria are possible without explaining which candidate is selected, whether adoption crosses its threshold, or why most candidates disappear. Selection is therefore a determinacy and adoption problem, not a general existence condition.**

---

## The argument, as stated

Goldberg puts it in a single paragraph, and the mechanism is a free-entry argument (2005, p. 15 — ✅ read):

> "In Goldberg (2003a) I show in a search model that these models lose most of their predictive power once a large number *n* of potential 'fiat moneys' are allowed to exist, because then there are 2ⁿ stationary equilibria, all with different combinations of 'fiat moneys' circulating. Prescott and Rios-Rull (2000) go further by pointing that in an overlapping generations model no monetary equilibrium exists under such conditions. This is because if I pick up, say, a useless nutshell and try to use it as 'fiat money' to buy goods from you, then your optimal response is to pick up a different nutshell, or a fallen leave, and try to use it as a different 'fiat money' to buy goods from the next generation."

This paragraph reports two distinct claims. Only the first belongs to Goldberg's own model; the second is Goldberg's characterisation of Prescott and Rios-Rull, followed by Goldberg's nutshell gloss.

**Indeterminacy (2ⁿ).** With *n* candidates the model admits every combination of circulating and non-circulating objects. It has not been refuted; it has stopped saying anything. A theory that permits 2ⁿ outcomes predicts none.

**No-restarting, not non-existence.** Prescott and Rios-Rull do not model unrestricted entry by rival nutshell currencies and do not prove that monetary equilibrium disappears. Their standard OLG economy has a continuum of monetary equilibria. Those allocations fail the authors' additional **no-restarting** rationality condition: a later young generation in the same position as the founding generation could ostracise the old and start the intergenerational arrangement again on better terms. They replace the standard concept with an organizational equilibrium, which exists, is essentially unique, and improves on autarky. The paper permits a monetary interpretation under a *no-counterfeiting* condition, but Goldberg's free-issuance story is not its theorem.

The usual one-candidate models therefore leave a real question open, but it is narrower than Goldberg says: they establish consistency, not historical selection. The missing object is a transition mechanism that explains which candidate acquires an acceptance network.

## Why this is different work from the rest of the paper

Goldberg's famous sections are an **evidence** argument: your exhibits are contaminated, the Yap stones had aesthetic and legal and religious value, the wampum was jewelry (see [the Rai case](rag/cases/rai-stones-yap.md) and [Yap in economics](rag/lineage/yap-in-economics.md)). Those are answerable in principle by producing a cleaner specimen.

This is a **theory** argument, and no specimen alone answers it. Even granting a perfectly valueless object with no legal status anywhere, a stationary existence proof still cannot say why *that* object and not the ten thousand others lying around becomes focal. But the audit changes the indictment: this is an omitted selection dynamic, not proof that money cannot exist.

## Why everything does not go viral

Duncan Watts supplies the propagation shape, not the cold-start engine. His threshold-network model begins with a seed and asks whether it spreads. Most shocks die. A global cascade occurs only inside a cascade window and only when a seed reaches a sufficiently connected cluster of vulnerable agents. Sparse networks fail because vulnerable adopters are disconnected; dense networks can also resist because one adopter is too small a fraction of each neighbour's contacts. Small initial differences can therefore produce radically different outcomes—but the model does not explain who paid for a seed strong enough to challenge an installed monetary system.

William Luther identifies the hurdle that comes first. Historical acceptance supplies the incumbent focal point; network effects, switching costs and coordination costs bias adoption toward the installed money even when everyone agrees that an alternative is technically superior. The path dependency is deep: complementary infrastructure, contracts, units of account, regulation, habits and skills are installed around the incumbent. So are beliefs about legitimate origin, trustworthy administration, stable value and disciplined issuance. The challenger must outbid that whole system advantage for its first users, not merely offer better technical properties. Luther cites Prescott and Rios-Rull only for the cautious proposition that an unbacked, non-compulsory money may be prone to competition—and immediately notes Bitcoin's dominance over a large altcoin field. Free entry creates candidates. It neither funds their footholds nor creates equal networks.

One way challengers reduce that burden is **simulacrum rather than replacement**. Reserve claims, redemption promises, costly production, mining and fixed-supply imagery let a novel ledger resemble the inherited gold-origin story. The design borrows a familiar grammar of trust and issuance while its own acceptance record is short. This helps explain why monetary innovation repeatedly retains gold mimicry after material gold has ceased doing operational work. The mimicry is a bridge across epistemic path dependence, not evidence that substance supplies value—and it need not overwrite the earlier belief. Gold, fiat, dollars and local money can occupy different functional and symbolic positions in the same person's monetary world.

Nor is the candidate set exhausted by currencies. **Unit, ledger, instrument and rail are separate selection axes.** Phone money and card money may be rival interfaces to the same deposit or credit ledger; cash is a bearer instrument that can share their unit while clearing differently. A single encounter can partition by relationship: the restaurant receives card money for the meal while the worker receives cash for the tip. Casino chips, concert-hall tokens and coupons establish bounded acceptance networks that compete with general money at particular counters, even though redemption or denomination may tie them back to it. Competition is therefore local to a transaction graph: instruments can be complements across domains and rivals at the point where either could clear the same obligation.

**Calibration.** "Simulacrum bridge" and its placement inside the adoption premium are this framework's synthesis. Its components are borrowed: Maurer, Nelms and Swartz's *digital metallism*, Dodd's explicit gold-mimicry account, and Ferry's finding that gold's historical semiotic weight remains available for analogous claims by Bitcoin. The proposed causal mechanism remains to be tested: familiar origin and issuance cues should reduce early adoption resistance while holding the challenger's actual network utility constant. Shaw supplies the complementary warning from early Bitcoin: conceptual consensus did not arrive and was not required; practical affirmations of use displaced the argument. Beliefs can be borrowed, bypassed and held simultaneously rather than defeated.

The combined answer therefore has a hand-off: **candidate instrument -> bootstrap wedge (material plus epistemic) -> incumbent outbid for one transaction class -> critical mass in that domain -> possible cascade -> installed position -> network effects plus switching-cost defence**. Network effects are present throughout, but initially work *against* the challenger in the contested domain. Tax receivability, legal status, commodity demand, platform leverage and speculation can pay or compel the wedge; simulacrum can reduce its belief component. The result may be dominance, coexistence or functional unbundling. These are bootstrap mechanisms, not rival substances placed underneath value.

The hand-off also separates **priming from continuation**. The bootstrap wedge is the cost of removing the air from a siphon and forming the first continuous acceptance path. After installation, future trade, credit, cooperation and specialisation may create enough continuation surplus for users to maintain validity conventions, make correct change, challenge breaches, adjust future terms and repair the record without one issuer or regulator supplying every function. That maintenance claim is conditional: expected lost opportunity, adjusted for observation and response, must exceed defection and keeper costs. If it does not, the apparent self-sustaining network still rests on an ongoing subsidy, duty, office or coercive support.

Four cases keep the mechanism honest. **Fiat had to displace gold** and did so gradually, retaining convertibility, reserve and discipline imagery while institutions learned to operate with progressively thinner backing (Redish). **A dollarizing economy does not select dollars because US taxes reach it**; the dollar arrives as an already-installed foreign network and takes saving, pricing, contracting and sometimes payment from the local unit one function at a time. **Japanese Taiwan shows that local taxation is not enough**: a tax-only attempt to install Japanese money failed when the state's effective economic share was below the acceptance threshold. **The Iraqi Swiss dinar supplies the inverse case**: the old note continued in Kurdish Iraq after its issuer repudiated it, while the issuer's replacement Saddam dinar did not automatically displace it there. An issuer is neither a sufficient selector nor, for persistence, a necessary one. These cases do not yield one winner in one mind; they reveal a monetary ecology in which authority, network, function and belief can point to different instruments simultaneously.

## What it does to the framework

The framework already holds that common knowledge of acceptance **selects** which money occupies which use-domain and does not create the worth — the value/selection/termination split recorded in [the future-obligation lineage](FUTURE-OBLIGATIONS-LINEAGE.md), and the reason `Starr` exists as a capacity condition rather than a price.

This argument makes that distinction operational rather than merely taxonomic.

The comfortable reading of selection is: expectation makes money possible, and selection explains which candidate occupies which monetary function. Free entry exposes the missing transition between those statements. A sink, legal status, focal event, installed platform or speculative seed can distinguish candidates and alter adoption thresholds. None is logically necessary for a monetary equilibrium; some symmetry-breaking history is necessary to explain the observed allocation of functions, including both concentration and coexistence.

Three things follow.

1. **The cold-start problem has a mirror, and one mechanism must answer both.** Cold start asks how anything becomes money. Free entry asks why everything does not. A framework that answers only the first is half a framework.
2. **`Starr` stays where it was.** A tax sink can block finite-horizon unravelling and can select a currency subject to capacity conditions. Prescott and Rios-Rull provide no warrant for upgrading every sink into a general existence condition.
3. **The strongest defensible form of the framework's thesis gets sharper.** *Money is not a substance and not merely a decree; it is an object a population converges on for a use and keeps expecting others to accept in that use.* Convergence can be domain-specific rather than total. Expectation is its medium; network structure, institutions and history govern its spread.

## The reply the framework has and these models do not

Goldberg's reported 2ⁿ result appears to depend on candidates being **symmetric**. Until the 2003 paper is retrieved, that dependence remains an inference from the reported result rather than a verified assumption of the model.

That symmetry is exactly what the repo's own structural claim denies. From [extraction and the modes](EXTRACTION-AND-THE-MODES.md): money has value because it is *"a network with winner-take-most pull"* — within a use-domain, the medium that lowers the most friction gets the steepest pull and can crowd out rivals. Under increasing returns to adoption the candidates are not interchangeable for long: tiny differences in initial adoption can amplify, while most seeds fail before reaching a vulnerable cluster. The realized history can concentrate each domain on a small number of focal survivors even when the underlying equilibrium set remains large and other domains select differently.

So multiplicity need not describe observed market shares. It may instead be the static shadow of a dynamic selection problem that the equilibrium model has not represented.

**Calibration.** "Winner-take-most pull" is a structural assertion in this repository, not a theorem, and this document must not dress it as one. The honest claim is that the framework has a *mechanism* to offer where these models have an *assumption*, and that closing the gap would require the formal work — which is precisely the sort of thing [the specification language](SPECIFICATION-LANGUAGE.md) exists to make possible.

## The experiment nobody meant to run

Goldberg's nutshell gloss describes the altcoin problem before altcoins existed. Free entry of digitally reproducible candidates then happened at enormous scale.

The outcome was **extreme concentration on a few focal points with an enormous near-zero tail**. That does not refute equilibrium multiplicity: one realized history can select one member of a large equilibrium set. It does refute the loose intuition that unrestricted nomination makes sustained monetary adoption impossible.

Two cautions remain. Crypto is not Prescott and Rios-Rull's OLG environment, so it is not a test of their no-restarting refinement. And crypto's focal points are not costless objects—proof-of-work, brand, exchange listing and accumulated common knowledge are asymmetries the nutshell case lacks. Which is the point: **free entry of candidates is not free entry of focal points or acceptance networks.**

It also places Bitcoin precisely. Its selection devices are focal — cap, first-mover common knowledge, hashrate — and it has **no sink**: κ ≈ 0 in `Starr`'s terms, since no obligation is denominated in it at scale (see [the Bitcoin case](rag/cases/bitcoin.md)). It therefore separates two claims that Goldberg runs together: it lacks a formal terminus and remains fragile on that dimension, yet focal-network selection has plainly supported persistent adoption. That is a live question for the framework rather than a settled point in its favour.

---

## The twist worth keeping

Goldberg reads as the anti-fiat economist, and this argument looks superficially like a case for commodity money.

Follow it through and it is not. Look at what distinguishes candidates in his own account of the demand grounds: commodity value, yes — but also legal tender status, tax obligation, prescribed ceremonial use, religious necessity, and forcing on buyers or sellers. Six of his ten grounds are institutional rather than material.

**The nutshell problem is solved by relations and propagation, not by stuff alone.** That is the framework's position, reached by combining Goldberg's candidate problem with Watts's cascade mechanism and Luther's monetary incumbency model.

## Pre-registered kill conditions

Staked before retrieval, so the sources cannot be read to fit. Same discipline that killed [the scale ladder](SCALE-LADDER.md).

- **K1 — FIRED.** Prescott & Rios-Rull do not establish the non-existence result attributed to them. Their standard monetary equilibria fail a proposed no-restarting refinement, while an organizational equilibrium exists. "Selection is an existence condition" has therefore been demoted to **selection is required for determinacy and adoption explanation**.
- **K2.** If Goldberg (2003a)'s 2ⁿ result already assumes symmetric candidates *and says so*, the reply in this document is not a contribution — it is a restatement of a limitation its author had already noted, and the section must say so.
- **K3.** If Goldberg (2003a) shows the multiplicity survives within a use-domain under an explicit dynamic with increasing returns to adoption, the winner-take-most reply **fails** and the framework needs a different answer to free entry.
- **K4.** If the escape routes in the literature turn out to be predominantly commodity-value rather than institutional, the "twist" section is wrong and should be cut rather than softened.
