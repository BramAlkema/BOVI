# Money with a Return Date

*The Bank of England's own account of money creation, the 1844 law it vindicates, and what a future obligation can — and cannot — explain about the value of money.*

---

> "Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money."
> — McLeay, Radia & Thomas, Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin, 2014 Q1

## I. How money is made, according to the people who make it

In 2014 the Bank of England published a short article in its Quarterly Bulletin called "Money creation in the modern economy." It is nine pages long, written by three staff economists, and it states the mechanics of the monetary system in the issuer's own voice. Bank deposits make up "97% of the amount currently in circulation," and the principal way those deposits come into being is that a commercial bank makes a loan and writes the matching deposit into existence at the same moment — what Tobin (1963) called *fountain pen money*, "created at the stroke of bankers' pens."

The article's mirror sentence is quoted less often and matters more:

> "Just as taking out a new loan creates money, **the repayment of bank loans destroys money**."

The Bank's worked example is deliberately mundane: a consumer runs up a credit-card balance at the supermarket, and "if the consumer were to pay their credit card bill in full at the end of the month, its bank would reduce the amount of deposits in the consumer's account by the value of the credit card bill, thus destroying all of the newly created money." Creation on the loan, destruction on the repayment: "Banks making loans and consumers repaying them are the most significant ways in which bank deposits are created and destroyed in the modern economy."

The folk picture of fiat money — a sovereign decreeing value into paper and printing it into circulation — describes almost none of this. What the issuer describes instead is money that is *born matched to a dated obligation*: nearly every unit in circulation was created against someone's promise to hand it back, and is extinguished when the promise is kept. The word *fiat* — "let it be done" — names a decree. The mechanism the Bank of England describes is a contract with a schedule. Modern money is not spoken into existence; it is lent into existence, and it carries a return date from birth.

## II. The law of reflux, 1844

None of this is new. It is a restatement — nearly clause for clause — of the position that *lost* the great British currency debate of 1844.

John Fullarton, arguing against what became Peel's Bank Charter Act, put it this way in *On the Regulation of Currencies*:

> "Bank-notes … are never issued but on loan, and an equal amount of notes must be returned into the bank whenever the loan becomes due. Bank-notes never, therefore, can clog the market by their redundance … The banker has only to take care that they are lent on sufficient security, and **the reflux and the issue will, in the long run, always balance each other**."

He ranked this mechanism above gold convertibility itself: "It is not so much by convertibility into gold, as by the regularity of the reflux, that in the ordinary course of things any redundance of the bank-note issues is rendered impossible. … **It is the reflux, that is the great regulating principle of the internal currency.**" So long as the bank's notes are out on short-dated loans, "the channel of reflux must remain open, and the Bank is utterly impotent to add a superfluous note to the currency."

His ally Thomas Tooke supplied the discriminating case. Government paper spent into circulation is different in kind, precisely because it lacks the return path: "paper created and so paid away by the Government, **not being returnable to the issuer**, will constitute a fresh source of demand, and must be forced into and permeate all the channels of circulation." The line between the two papers is not metal versus no metal. It is *returnable versus not*.

The Banking School lost the legislative fight; the Currency School's quantity doctrine wrote the 1844 Act. But losing the policy argument and winning the mechanism argument are different events, and in this case they happened a hundred and seventy years apart: the endogenous-money literature (Moore 1988) recovered the operational point, and the 2014 Bulletin article is Fullarton's reflux stated as fact by the issuing institution itself. Two honesty notes belong here, because the reflux is often over-claimed on its author's behalf: Fullarton himself stated the law's boundary condition — cut off the short-dated discount channel and fresh issues "would then be so much permanently added to the circulation" — and the tidy list of "three reflux channels" recited in the secondary literature appears nowhere in his text as a list.

## III. One device, four substrates

Widen the lens and the reflux turns out to be one instance of a family that runs from Adam Smith to the modern fiscal theory of the price level. Each member explains the acceptance of an intrinsically valueless instrument by pointing at a **future obligation denominated in it**:

- **Future taxes.** Smith (1776), on the American colonies' paper: it "derived from this use some additional value," and — the neglected half — the value varied with the *ratio* of the paper issued to the tax take. His thought experiment needs no redemption at all: "A prince, who should enact that a certain proportion of his taxes should be paid in a paper money of a certain kind, might thereby give a certain value to this paper money, even though the term of its final discharge and redemption should depend altogether upon the will of the prince." Wicksteed (1910) added the propagation step in prose; A. Mitchell Innes (1914) stated it as a law — "the redemption of government debt by taxation is the basic law of coinage" — and located the micro bridge: the taxpayer "has to acquire his portion of the debt from some holder." Lerner (1947) completed the relay in one sentence: "all other people will be willing to accept these pieces of paper because they know that the taxpayers, etc., will accept them in turn."
- **Future loan repayments.** Fullarton and the Bank of England, above: the private, decentralised, sovereign-free member of the family.
- **Future primary surpluses.** The fiscal theory of the price level: the real value of government debt equals the present value of future surpluses. Cochrane (2023) is admirably plain about what kind of theory this is: "We may view the fiscal theory as a backing theory of money. … you have the right to pay your taxes with dollars. That right constitutes a backing. Dollars backed by gold can be soaked up by giving people the gold in return for dollars. Our dollars can be soaked up by taxes." His ancestry claim runs, correctly, straight back to the Smith passage above.
- **Future taxes, capitalised.** Barro (1974): households discount the future taxes that service today's bonds — the channel by which a *future* obligation appears in a *present* price, stated in expectations.

One object recurs in every row: **a dated, enforceable, unit-denominated claim on a named party.** Every such claim manufactures a guaranteed future acceptor — someone who, on a known date, must obtain the unit or bear a penalty, and from whom the token cannot be refused.

It is worth saying plainly whose ideas these are. The tax half is chartalist house doctrine under its own names — Mosler's *drain*, Wray's *redemption* ("taxes drive money"; the tax payment "simultaneously destroys currency as well as the taxpayer's liability"), Tymoigne's reflux-priced instruments. And the directional insight — that this whole family faces *forward*, where the classical answer faced *backward* — was stated by David Glasner in 2011: "The Regression Theorem, as its name attests, is backward-looking. But economic problems … are forward-looking." Mises terminates the regress of "accepted because accepted" in remembered prices, behind the money; the obligation family terminates it in a pre-committed acceptor, ahead of it. **A floor is behind you; a sink is ahead of you.** What appears not to have been said before is only the identification: that these are the *same* termination move, executed in opposite temporal directions — and that recognising the symmetry changes what the sink can be asked to do.

## IV. What the sink buys — and the fine print

It is tempting to conclude that the obligation is therefore *why money has value*. The formal literature, read closely, licenses something more precise and more interesting.

The problem the sink actually solves was stated by Patinkin (1949) and given its sharpest form by Ross Starr (1974): in a finite-horizon economy, money unravels from the end. "Near the terminal period the economy will be imbued with a *Weltuntergangsstimmung*," Starr writes, and worthlessness regresses backward to the first date; "if we say that money is accepted because it is accepted, then we must agree that if money were not accepted then it would not be accepted because it would not be accepted." Taxes payable in the token are his repair: they "create a demand for money independent of its usefulness as a medium of exchange."

But Starr's theorems carry fine print the slogans drop. If the aggregate tax take falls short of the money stock at every price, zero is the **only** equilibrium price of money — an undersized sink supports nothing at all (his Theorem 2). A positive price exists, and is pinned, exactly where the take crosses the stock (Theorem 3): Smith's ratio, proved. And the obligation itself contributes no nominal anchor: a dollar discharges a dollar of tax at *any* price level, so what the sink guarantees, at every date, is **acceptance** — never, by itself, a level. Every formal result that pins the price level imports a nominal rigidity from elsewhere, typically a predetermined nominal stock confronting obligations whose real bite does not shrink as prices rise.

The selection reading was stated from inside the Federal Reserve a year after "money is memory." Kocherlakota and Phelan (1999), explaining the fiscal theory: the theory "is equivalent to giving the government an ability to choose among equilibria"; "at its very core, a non-Ricardian policy is an equilibrium rejection device." On value they allow themselves one footnote, with the philosophy carried by punctuation: such a policy "eliminates the worthless-money equilibrium," and so can "'cause' money to have value" — scare quotes theirs. And they attach a warning that applies to every member of the family: whether a government would really refuse to balance its books along price paths that never occur is a claim about **unobserved, off-equilibrium behaviour**, and therefore "a religious, not a scientific, question."

So the honest ledger has three registers, and the obligation family occupies two of them. **Selection**: a tax statute is public, dated, uniform, and enforced — arguably the most efficient generator of common knowledge of acceptance a society has built. The search-theoretic literature formalises this as an operation on the set of equilibria, and adds its own fine print: in Li & Wright (1998), a government committed to accepting the money selects the monetary equilibrium — even one that would not exist without intervention — *if and only if* the government's share of economic activity exceeds a threshold; and the mechanism is guaranteed acceptance in ordinary trade, not taxation, which their model does not contain ("Although we do not have taxation explicitly in our model"). Aiyagari & Wallace (1997) derive existence from guaranteed acceptance alone. Size is the variable; the tax is one way for a state to be large at the point of acceptance, not a magic word. **Termination**: the dated obligation is the terminal-shaped node that blocks Starr's unraveling — where such a node exists. **Value-source**: no. The register the popular versions claim is the one the theorems do not deliver.

## V. Three experiments, run in three directions

The American Civil War ran the *addition* test. Two papers of the same government circulated side by side: Demand Notes, receivable for customs duties, and greenbacks, which were not. The tax-receivable paper held within about ten cents of gold par; the greenback fell to a forty-cent discount by March 1863 (Mitchell 1903; Koning 2017). Same sovereign, same war — the sink itself, priced in the wild.

Iraq in the 1990s ran the *deletion* test. When the Saddam government demonetized the old "Swiss dinar" notes in 1993, the notes went on circulating in the Kurdish north for a decade — in Mervyn King's words, "backed by no formal government, central bank, nor any law of legal tender": receivable nowhere, redeemable by no one, the supply frozen by the accident of unavailable printing plates. Value with the obligation machinery at zero. (King's own account adds the honest wrinkle: the note's late *appreciation* tracked the odds of future re-monetization — a merely-expected sink doing the work the legally pre-committed one was supposed to monopolize.)

And colonial Taiwan ran the *undersized-decree* test. Around 1895 the incoming Japanese administration required that taxes be paid in Japanese money rather than the copper coins in local use, precisely to install its currency as the medium of exchange. People deferred paying their taxes; revenue fell so far that the policy was abandoned (the case is reported by Li & Wright 1998, whose size-threshold theorems it illustrates). A tax sink was decreed — and it was too small a share of the economy to select anything.

Together the three experiments locate the sink exactly: priced when added, surviving when deleted, impotent when undersized. That is the signature of a stabiliser with a capacity condition — not a source.

## VI. Memory, plus a return date

The deepest way to say what the obligation family adds comes from the same economist who co-wrote the selection paper. Kocherlakota's "Money Is Memory" (1998) proved that money is technologically equivalent to a record of past actions — "an imaginary balance sheet"; "money is merely a physical way of maintaining this balance sheet." The word *tax* appears nowhere in it, and the mechanism needs no state: the theorem's whole friction is that nobody can commit to anything. In the working-paper version, one sentence — cut from the journal text — draws the line that joins the two literatures:

> "Money only provides memory, but **bonds provide both memory and commitment**."

That is the fusion. A future obligation is not more money; in Kocherlakota's own taxonomy it is a *bond* — a ledger entry plus the threat of external force. The obligation family, from Fullarton's reflux to Cochrane's surpluses, describes what happens when a monetary system is threaded with commitment: entries written in advance, for known dates, on parties who cannot refuse. Commitment is precisely what pure memory lacks, which is why pure memory-money unravels in Starr's finite world and a sink-equipped money does not.

Put the pieces in one sentence each. Money is a memory device; its value is the trade it unlocks, selected and sustained by the expectation of acceptance. A future obligation is a bond fused to that memory: it cannot be the value — the Swiss dinar circulated for ten years without one — but it recruits a guaranteed acceptor at a known date, and in doing so it makes the expectation common knowledge and the equilibrium robust.

Which returns us to the word we started with. *Fiat* is a double misnomer. The money is not made by decree — ninety-seven percent of it is made by contract, at the stroke of a pen, against a schedule of repayment. And its value is not decreed either — no decree has ever managed that; the state's real instruments are the acceptor it guarantees and the sink it prices. Money is made with a return date. The return date was never the value. It is the guarantee that, tomorrow, someone must still be standing at the door.
