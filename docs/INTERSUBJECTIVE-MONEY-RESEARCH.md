# Money as an Intersubjective Price Relation

## A research synthesis on acceptance, marginal surplus, and monetary power

### Abstract

This paper develops a strong intersubjectivist account of money. Money is not valuable because value is stored in its material substrate, nor because a state, a commodity, or a protocol injects value into it. Its monetary standing is a recursively sustained relation: each holder accepts it partly because they expect other participants to accept it in future transactions. That relation is not, however, equivalent to value itself. Acceptance selects the monetary instrument; the marginal net surplus generated by the trades and options that acceptance makes possible determines what holding it is worth.

The resulting position is **floorless but fruitful**. There is no intrinsic floor beneath monetary value, but the monetary relation can produce real transactional and specialisation surplus. A complete intersubjectivism therefore does not reduce money to collective belief. It treats price as a socially stabilised, materially constrained equilibrium of expected marginal benefits, costs, risks, and extraction. The same intersubjective infrastructure can coordinate, reciprocate, command, share, or extract. Price and welfare must therefore be analysed separately.

## 1. The question

“What gives money value?” usually compresses several distinct questions:

1. What is money’s mechanism?
2. Why does this instrument become accepted rather than another?
3. What is one additional unit worth to a holder?
4. Who receives the gains and who bears the costs?

Metallists, chartalists, credit theorists, New Monetarists, and intersubjectivist economists each illuminate part of this object. The recurring error is to promote one part into the explanation of the whole.

The central claim of this paper is:

> Money is intersubjective in its acceptance, but its price is the socially stabilised value of expected marginal cooperation, net of the costs, risks, and extraction imposed by the monetary system.

This is not a claim that everyone shares one valuation. It is a claim about mutually observed expectations among people whose valuations remain distinct.

## 2. What “completely intersubjective” means

A complete intersubjectivism rejects an intrinsic monetary value outside relations among users. The following are not metaphysical sources of value:

- gold or another commodity substrate;
- state decree considered in isolation;
- a cryptographic protocol considered in isolation;
- a token’s physical or digital substance;
- a single aggregate “social valuation.”

These can still matter greatly. They are technologies of supply governance, enforcement, credibility, or coordination. They help determine whether a monetary network forms and how robustly it survives. They do not put a quantity of value inside the token.

Intersubjectivity also does not mean that monetary value is whatever people happen to say it is. Expectations are constrained by production, scarcity, time, technology, legal force, institutional capacity, and human needs. These constraints are encountered through social relations, but they are not made irrelevant by belief.

The correct contrast is therefore not:

> objective value versus arbitrary opinion

but:

> value as a property of a thing versus value as a constrained relation among agents, institutions, and the world they must act in.

## 3. The scholarly lineage

### Simmel: value as relation

Georg Simmel provides the philosophical backbone. In *The Philosophy of Money*, value is not a substance residing in objects. It is produced through distance, sacrifice, desire, and exchange. Exchange makes private valuations socially comparable without turning them into one private or collective mind.

Money is therefore not an exception to subjectivism. It is the clearest case of value as relation because its usefulness consists largely in connecting one person’s present position to another person’s future willingness to transact.

### Samuelson and Tirole: the forward relay

Samuelson’s overlapping-generations model shows that a valueless token can sustain a positive monetary equilibrium. Tirole develops the rational-bubble treatment further. These models establish an important negative result: a prior commodity backing is not logically necessary for a monetary equilibrium.

They do not, by themselves, explain why a particular equilibrium is selected, and a pure bubble has no productive payoff. Their contribution is consistency, not a complete theory of value or origin.

### Kiyotaki–Wright and Lagos–Wright: liquidity under friction

New Monetarist models make the value of money operational. With bilateral trade, imperfect information, limited commitment, and the absence of a double coincidence of wants, a monetary instrument can facilitate trades that otherwise fail or become too costly. The liquidity service is not assumed to come from the token’s substance; it is derived from the frictional environment.

This supplies the economic meaning of “fruitful”: the monetary relation can generate real surplus at the margin.

### Ostroy and Kocherlakota: money as record and memory

The record-keeping and memory traditions identify the mechanism more minimally. Money preserves information about who has given, who has received, and what remains to be settled. A ledger can perform this function even when the substrate has negligible commodity value, and in the limiting case the record need not be a held token at all.

This is a theory of mechanism rather than a complete theory of value. A record explains what money does; the intersubjective account explains why that service is valued and how its value depends on continuing participation.

### Fullbrook, Orléan, Ingham, and intersubjective economics

Intersubjectivist and institutional approaches make the social relation explicit. Market valuations are not simply independent private numbers later added together. They are formed within structures of expectation, imitation, convention, authority, and monetary obligation. Money is consequently a social relation with institutional and political consequences, not merely a neutral object exchanged between isolated optimisers.

No single author in this lineage states the entire proposition developed here. The synthesis combines Simmel’s relational value, Samuelson’s forward equilibrium, New Monetarist liquidity services, the ledger/memory mechanism, and institutional analysis of authority and distribution.

## 4. The elephant and the blind men

The “blind men and the elephant” metaphor maps cleanly onto the monetary schools:

| School or tradition | What it sees correctly | What it risks mistaking for the whole |
|---|---|---|
| Metallism / Mises | impersonal supply, credible anti-dilution, resistance to issuer discretion | governance conditions for the source of value |
| Chartalism / Knapp / MMT | legal demand, fiscal capacity, elasticity, the issuer’s authority | an acceptance engine for the source of value |
| Credit and debt theory | obligation, ledger, memory, settlement | the residue of the relation for money’s entire essence |
| New Monetarism | liquidity, friction, bilateral exchange, welfare gains | direct transactional surplus for all monetary value |
| Simmel and intersubjective economics | relational valuation, convention, recursive expectation | social form for the entire price determination |
| BOVI synthesis | value, selection, mechanism, governance, and incidence as separate layers | no single layer is sufficient |

The schools disagree partly because they are measuring different fields. A metallist may count discretionary dilution as a cost. A chartalist may count elasticity under shock as a benefit. A New Monetarist may count trades that clear. A political economist may ask who receives the first claim and who bears the adjustment. These are not necessarily mutually exclusive observations; they use different counterfactuals, boundaries, and incidence rules.

## 5. A complete intersubjectivist account of price

If money is completely intersubjective, there is no absolute “price of money.” A price is always an exchange ratio against a basket, good, labour service, asset, or another monetary unit. The price of money is therefore the purchasing power that emerges from a network of expected future settlements.

For a holder (i), an intentionally schematic expression is:

\[
P^m_i \approx \operatorname{PV}\left[
MB^{direct}_i + MB^{network}_i + MB^{option}_i
 - MC^{transaction}_i - MC^{monetary}_i - Risk_i - Extraction_i
\right]
\]

where:

- (MB^{direct}) is the surplus from trades the unit directly helps settle;
- (MB^{network}) is the value of access to counterparties and specialisation;
- (MB^{option}) is the value of remaining able to transact under uncertainty;
- (MC^{transaction}) is the cost of using the monetary rail;
- (MC^{monetary}) is the cost introduced by the monetary layer itself, such as fees, verification, compliance, forensic legibility, privacy loss, and taxability;
- (Risk) includes default, devaluation, acceptance failure, and settlement risk;
- (Extraction) includes rents, asymmetric issuance, spreads, and uncompensated transfers where they affect the holder’s marginal position.

This is not a claim that people literally calculate the expression. It is a decomposition of the forces that a price must summarise. The relevant quantity is marginal and counterfactual: what does one more unit enable or spare relative to not holding it? It is not the total value of every trade in the economy.

The acceptance chain is therefore a condition of the calculation, not the calculation itself:

> Acceptance explains how money circulates. The surplus from the trades circulation unlocks explains what it is worth.

## 6. Public goods, externalities, and extraction

Many monetary benefits and costs do not belong to the direct acceptance chain. A payment network can produce public benefits—more specialisation, lower search costs, better coordination, and wider market access—that accrue to people who never hold the currency. Conversely, it can impose public harms—surveillance, exclusion, systemic fragility, ecological cost, or political dependence—that no individual transaction price fully records.

This creates an essential distinction:

\[
\text{private holder value} \neq \text{social net contribution}
\]

A public benefit affects the unit’s price only if it feeds back into acceptance, availability, credibility, supply expectations, or the marginal benefit of holding. It can improve social welfare without being capitalised into the monetary price. The reverse is also possible: an extractive privilege can support a currency’s price while reducing total welfare.

Transfers must be separated from real costs. A tax, fee, or seigniorage transfer may redistribute purchasing power without destroying resources. Verification work, congestion, privacy loss, foregone alternatives, and deadweight exclusion are resource or welfare costs. Incidence is a separate question from aggregate accounting:

> Who pays, who receives, who can refuse, and who has recourse?

The monetary layer is therefore neither simply a public good nor simply a cost. It is an institution whose benefits, burdens, and power relations must be measured separately.

## 7. What this implies for the schools

The metallist–chartalist dispute becomes clearer when treated as a dispute about governance rather than an exclusive theory of value.

- Metallism foregrounds impersonality and known supply. Its legitimate concern is betrayal through discretionary dilution.
- Chartalism foregrounds legal authority and elasticity. Its legitimate concern is the ability to answer demand shocks and mobilise real resources.
- Credit theory foregrounds enforceable obligation and settlement. Its legitimate concern is why claims are honoured.
- New Monetarism foregrounds friction and liquidity. Its legitimate concern is the real surplus produced by monetary exchange.
- Intersubjectivism foregrounds the recursive social relation. Its legitimate concern is how expectations become stabilised and how public conventions shape private valuations.

None of these governance properties is identical to monetary worth. Anchor type is a selection and credibility technology. It helps determine which monetary relation is adopted and how long it lasts. It is not a substance from which value is poured into the instrument.

## 8. The conclusion

Complete intersubjectivism does not say that money is “only belief.” It says that money is a social relation whose price is produced through mutually anticipated access to future cooperation. The relation is reflexive, but not unconstrained; social, but not immaterial; potentially productive, but also distributive and extractive.

The clean formulation is:

> Money has no intrinsic floor. Its price is the equilibrium purchasing power of expected marginal net monetary services, sustained by recursive acceptance and constrained by the material and institutional world.

Or more briefly:

> Intersubjectivity explains the loop. Marginal surplus explains the price. Power explains the distribution.
