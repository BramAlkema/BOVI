## The full pitch

Subject: **Video pitch: the Nobel-grade result about money that nobody teaches — and why**

Hi Joeri,

I want to pitch you a video with a genuinely strange hook:

> In 1958, Paul Samuelson published a model proving that money with no backing whatsoever — no gold, no promise, no intrinsic worth — can hold a positive value forever, as a self-fulfilling equilibrium. Tirole sharpened it in 1985. It's taught in graduate school under the name **rational bubble theory**. It is, quietly, the profession's own account of why fiat money works. And your Econ 101 textbook still opens with a story about barter and gold.

The video is about that gap: why the discipline proved something and then declined to teach it. Six beats.

**1. The result — with the actual math on screen.** There are two directions to point the chain of value, and the whole century-long argument is which way the arrow goes. Mises pointed it **backward**: money is valued at *t* because it was valued at *t−1*, and so on down until the chain stands on a commodity — value has to rest *on* something. Samuelson pointed it **forward**: money is valued at *t* because it will be accepted at *t+1*, which holds because of *t+2*, all the way up. A backward chain needs a floor; a forward chain needs a fixed point — and the fixed point exists. One displayed equation carries the video:

> *p*ₜ = (benefitₜ₊₁ + *p*ₜ₊₁) / (1 + r)  →  unrolled:  *p*ₜ = Σₖ benefitₜ₊ₖ / (1 + r)ᵏ

Money's value **is the discounted sum of all the future benefits it delivers** — the trades it clears that wouldn't otherwise have happened. It's the standard asset-pricing equation; money isn't even a special case. In Samuelson's pure bubble the benefit term is zero and the value floats free (that's the "arbitrary" part); put the real liquidity service in the numerator and the value is floorless *and* fruitful. And the price settles the boring way, the way everything is priced: you hold money up to the point where **marginal cost equals marginal benefit** — the last dollar of balances is worth exactly the interest you gave up to hold it. mc = mb. No special theory of value for money — *that's the punchline*. The mystery was manufactured by demanding money have its own foundation when the ordinary machinery prices it fine. (Honest caveat, worth one line in the video: the fixed-point theorem proves such an equilibrium is *consistent*, not why we land on it — but consistency alone kills the "it must be backed by something" intuition. The selection half is the climax — see below, because it's yours.)

**2. Why we flinched.** This isn't a story about ignorance — the people closest to the truth blinked at the last step. Menger subjectivized *all* value in 1871, then carved out one exception: money, which supposedly needed a commodity origin. Mises hardened that flinch into a theorem. And when Samuelson finally proved the no-hands result, the profession filed it under "curiosities" — partly because *bubble* is economics' word for pathology, and saying "the monetary system is a bubble" sounds like confessing a con. There's also a deeper fear worth naming on camera: that if people learned the truth, the thing would pop. It won't — the equilibrium runs on acceptance, not on folk beliefs about what's in the vault. The vault story was never load-bearing.

**3. Why we lack the pedagogy.** Negations teach terribly. "Money isn't backed by anything" headlines the myth, and myth-repetition research says the audience walks away remembering the myth. The barter-to-gold story survives in textbooks because it's *positive and wrong*, and positive-and-wrong beats negative-and-true in every classroom. The fix isn't debunking — it's demonstration: show a working money made of something with no value at all (a knotted rope tallying favors in a village), watch it coordinate cooperation, and the "what backs it?" question dissolves on its own. If the substance were the source, the rope couldn't work. It works.

**4. How little money actually needs.** The textbook hands money a job description: unit of account, store of value, stable, scarce, durable, portable, traceable — all that jazz. Strip it, on camera, one property at a time. Every item on that list is a bolt-on: a feature of *particular* monies, not a requirement of money. The minimal thing has exactly **two** requirements: the total supply must be **sufficiently symmetrically known** — no one can quietly mint, so everyone's estimate of how much exists is good enough that a mark means what it says — and it must be **sufficiently divisible** — fine-grained enough to record the trades people actually make. That's the entire spec for functioning as shared memory, and it's why the knotted rope clears trades: the village can see the whole rope (supply known to all), and one knot is small enough (divisible enough). It doesn't store value, it isn't scarce, it isn't stable, it isn't even a unit of account — and it works, because none of those were ever the mechanism. The segment plays as a strip-tease of the money concept: remove feature after feature, the trades keep clearing, and what's left standing at the end is a ledger.

**5. Wrong reasons don't mean bad outcomes.** Here's the part I've never seen a video do, and it's the emotional core. The equilibrium doesn't care *why* you accept money. Gold in a vault, "the government stands behind it," "it's just valuable" — all three beliefs produce the identical behavior, and the behavior is what sustains the fixed point. Folk essentialism about money is like folk physics: false, and it gets you across the street every single day. That's why the error survived for centuries — it's *free at the checkout counter*. Billions of people, wrong about money, clearing trades flawlessly. This is also the generous turn the video can take toward its own audience: you were never stupid for believing the vault story. It cost you nothing. Until —

**6. Why we should care.** The error is free when you *use* money and expensive when you *govern* with it. Act on essentialism and you get real policy wreckage: the gold standard transmitting the Depression, "sound money" deflation engines, Bitcoin's fixed supply marketed as a feature, hyperinflation panics that never arrive. And there's a real demand hiding under the essentialist label that deserves respect rather than mockery: people who say "back it with gold" are usually asking "how do I know you won't print my savings away?" — a governance question, and a good one. The answer is credible rules, not magic substances. More broadly: the same mental move — locating value in a substance instead of a relation — shows up in blood, flag, and ground. Money is the one domain where you can build a working counterexample in five minutes of video. That's what makes it the teaching wedge.

**Why Money & Macro, specifically.** I went through your catalog before writing this, and the video sits at the intersection of three things you've already built. *The Emergence of Credit Money in Europe* staged commodity-theory against credit-theory — the origin question — but left the value question open: what makes the credit worth anything? And your Economics Explained hyperinflation rebuttals established the channel's franchise — correcting folk monetary physics with the actual machinery — which is exactly what this is, aimed one level deeper. Your recent run has been geopolitics; this would be a return to the monetary-foundations territory the channel was founded on, with five years more audience.

**The climax only you can film.** The honest gap in the OLG result — consistency without selection — is precisely what an agent-based simulation demonstrates, and that's your dissertation toolkit. Seed heterogeneous agents with acceptance beliefs, let common knowledge spread or fail, and watch value condense onto an intrinsically worthless token — or watch it pop when the symmetric-knowledge condition breaks. The Marimon–McGrattan–Sargent / Duffy emergence-of-money literature has done this in papers; nobody has done it on camera. The video's third act is you *running the bubble live* in your own ABM — the theorem's missing half, supplied by simulation, by the one YouTube economist whose PhD was agent-based monetary economics.

## Appendix — source spine (for the beat-sheet, not the email)

- Samuelson (1958), Tirole (1985) — rational bubble / OLG: floorless value is a consistent equilibrium. Consistency, not selection; leans on the infinite horizon. Frame at exact strength.
- The equation: *p*ₜ = (dₜ₊₁ + *p*ₜ₊₁)/(1+r), unrolled to the discounted sum of future liquidity services. Pure bubble = zero-dividend limit; framework = positive service flow in the numerator (floorless **and** fruitful). Forward relay (t+1) vs Mises's regress (t−1) as the visual contrast.
- mc = mb pricing — Patinkin's marginal utility of holding real balances; Lagos–Wright derive the service from frictions. Money priced by the ordinary marginalist machinery; the demand for a special foundation *created* the mystery.
- Minimal money spec — two requirements only: total supply sufficiently **symmetrically known** (Integrity) and sufficiently **divisible** (Resolution). Unit-of-account, store-of-value, stability, scarcity, portability, traceability = bolt-ons of particular monies, not requirements of money. The rope passes the two-item spec and fails the whole textbook list — and works.
- Menger (1871) — the flinching forerunner; Mises (1912) — the regression theorem as hardened flinch.
- Simmel (1900) — all value is relational; money the purest case, not the exception. The floor-vs-bubble debate has a third position: floorless but fruitful.
- Patinkin (1956) — the anti-regression move; Lagos–Wright — the liquidity service derived from frictions.
- Kocherlakota (1998) — money is memory; the tally rope is this result made physical.
