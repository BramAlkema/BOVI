## Draft

She paid five euros for a coffee in Buenos Aires. The statement, three weeks later, said twenty-three.

We have met this number already, as a piece of friction — the sediment of ledgers that would not agree. Now we meet it as something sharper: a transaction forced, without her knowledge or consent, into a single mode. She thought she was buying a coffee — a small, warm, near-Immediate thing, one person handing another a cup. The system around her thought otherwise. To her bank, her card network, and the dynamic-currency-conversion engine that quietly intercepted the sale, she was not a guest being handed a coffee. She was a pricing opportunity. They ran the sale in pure **Value** mode — *what is the most this particular person, in this particular place, can be made to pay?* — and she learned the answer on her statement.

### The mode of strangers

Value mode is the one the textbooks know. *The price is the price.* A spot trade between equals who owe each other nothing before and nothing after — money for goods, cleanly, and then you walk away as unconnected as you arrived. Its tell, the question that reveals it, is always *is the price right?* Not *are we even,* not *who decided this,* not *are we okay* — just, is this a fair price for this thing, here, now.

And it is genuinely, enormously good — at the thing it is for. When you sell a jacket to a stranger on Vinted, hail an Uber, pay for Spotify, buy a coffee from a café you will never see again, Value mode is exactly right. Nobody wants to keep a relationship ledger with their music service; nobody wants their Uber driver to share the ride out of communal love. For anonymous exchange between people who are not in a relationship and do not wish to be, price is the most humane mechanism we have: fast, impersonal in the good sense, letting strangers cooperate without first having to become friends or subjects. We will not do the easy thing and call Value mode cold or wicked. For its proper domain it is a marvel.

### The trap is contagion

The danger of Value mode is not that it is bad. It is that it does not know its place. Of the four modes it is the most *imperial* — the most prone to spread into territory that belongs to the others and quietly convert it.

That is what happened to the tourist: a relationship that should have run near Immediate (a coffee, a small hospitality) was annexed by Value and priced to the maximum the system could extract. And the same annexation runs through modern life wherever the technology allows. Surge pricing turns an emergency — a ride home, now, in the rain — into a Value-mode auction at the moment you can least refuse. Dating apps have been caught charging older users more for the same subscription, because their willingness to pay is higher. Retailers vary a price by your postcode, your device, your browsing history. Each is Value mode doing what Value mode does — finding the price you will bear — applied to a domain where you did not agree to be priced, and often where you cannot tell it is happening.

Notice the link to the consent axis. Forced Value mode is *coerced* Value, and it carries the same illegitimacy as a coerced obligation. The tourist did not consent to a pricing event; it was imposed by a system that knew things about her she did not know it knew. We return to this — the deliberate, algorithmic forcing of Value mode onto unconsenting buyers — in its own chapter, because it is becoming one of the central conflicts of the digital economy.

### The defence

The defence against Value-mode contagion is small and surprisingly effective: name the basis, and refuse it when it does not belong. When a price appears where a price should not — when the workplace that calls itself a family suddenly invokes "market rate" to hold down your pay, when the "convenience fee" materialises, when the friend who borrowed your car wants to "settle up" in a way that feels wrong — the move is to say, out loud or to yourself, *this is being run as a Value-mode transaction, and I did not agree that it was one.* You cannot always win the argument. But you can refuse to be confused by it, and refusing to be confused is most of the battle, because Value mode does its worst work invisibly, by getting you to accept its frame before you have noticed there was one.

Value works for strangers. But strangers are not the only people we transact with, and for the people we are actually in a relationship with, a different mode keeps the books.
