## Draft

In 2011 the government of Argentina ordered McDonald's to sell the Big Mac at a loss.

Not for the customers' benefit. The Big Mac had a price problem — specifically, it had the *wrong* price, a price that told the truth. For decades *The Economist* had published its Big Mac Index, a wry global comparison of what the same burger costs in different countries, used by economists as a rough check on whether a currency is over- or under-valued. The Argentine Big Mac, priced honestly, was quietly testifying that the peso was worth far less than the official rate claimed. So the Secretary of Commerce instructed McDonald's to sell it at roughly half the price of the other burgers on the menu, dragging the index down to flatter the currency. McDonald's complied. And then, tellingly, the Big Mac simply *vanished* from the menu boards and the advertising — present if you asked, invisible if you did not — because a fake price is easier to hold when nobody is looking at it. The state had weaponised silence against a burger in a British magazine.

### Prices are the symptom, not the disease

We are taught a simple story about inflation: too much money chasing too few goods, so prices rise. It is not wrong, exactly, but it points at the wrong thing — it makes the rising prices the event. Watch the Big Mac war and the causation runs the other way. The prices were not the disease. They were a *readout.* What the Argentine state was actually fighting was not a number on a menu but the *common knowledge* the number carried — the shared, public, checkable fact of what the peso could buy. Inflation, at bottom, is not a price phenomenon. It is the cracking of common knowledge about a currency: the supply pillar from Chapter 4 — symmetrically-known supply, the integrity that lets everyone trust how much exists — gives way; the selection loop from Chapter 5 — the shared expectation that the money is good — frays behind it; and *then* prices climb, oscillate, and run, as the visible symptom of an invisible loss of trust. Keep the split from those chapters in view: the *worth* of money — the trades it could unlock — has not evaporated; what has broken is the *coordination* on which token still carries it.

This is why some inflations recover and others spiral into the abyss. If the broken common knowledge can be repaired quickly — credibility restored, the supply made legible again — the currency limps and heals. If the institutional trust is gone for a generation, as in Weimar or Zimbabwe, no amount of monetary tinkering brings it back, because the thing that broke was never really the quantity. It was the belief.

### Trust your own basket

Which is why the most honest inflation number you will ever have is not the government's, and not *The Economist's.* It is your own. Headline CPI is an aggregate — methodologically opaque, politically reachable, and, crucially, not specific to the things *you* buy; the official basket and your basket can drift apart for years. A personal index, by contrast — a notebook in which you write down what a coffee, a bus pass, a haircut, a week's groceries actually cost you, month after month — is more honest precisely because it is too small to be worth corrupting. The state can lean on McDonald's. It cannot lean on your notebook.

And the suppression, when it comes, is itself the cleanest signal you will get. When the Argentine government pressured the Big Mac off the menu, the *New York Times* and *The Economist* eventually exposed the manoeuvre — and within days the price leapt twenty-six per cent, the truth springing back the instant the thumb lifted. A government suppressing a measurement is telling you, more reliably than the measurement ever could, that the measurement was right. Watch what they hide; it is a better index than what they publish.

And the lesson runs both ways, which is the final twist. By the mid-2020s, under a very different government and a crawling-peg policy, Argentina had swung to the *opposite* distortion — its Big Mac now among the most expensive in the world, the peso held artificially *strong.* Same burger, same country, opposite lie. An honest price made artificially cheap and an honest price made artificially dear are both tells of a currency managed against the truth. The index is not telling you the burger changed. It is telling you the ruler did.

Sometimes the manipulation is enough to keep a currency limping along — distorted, but alive. Sometimes the anchor does not bend but breaks; the common knowledge does not crack but shatters; and a whole population has to find new money overnight. That is the next chapter, and its protagonist is someone we already know well.
