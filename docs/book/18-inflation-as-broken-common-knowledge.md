# Chapter 18: Inflation as Broken Common Knowledge

> *The inflation that breaks currencies is not a price phenomenon — it is a coordination failure. The supply pillar cracks, common knowledge collapses, prices rise as a symptom. The ordinary kind — real shocks inside a trusted unit — is weather; this chapter is about the instrument breaking.*

## Themes

- Measurement is political.
- An aggregate can be adequate while the distribution beneath it kills. Nobody eats a total.
- Honest indices are dangerous to dishonest regimes.
- Trust your eyes over headline statistics.
- Suppression of a measurement system is a more reliable signal than the measurement itself.

## Twists

- The reader expects "inflation makes prices go up." The chapter argues it is the other way around — prices rising are the *symptom*; the cause is broken common knowledge of supply.
- The claim is scoped, not universal: the rich world's early-2020s inflation was mostly weather — real shocks inside a trusted unit — and it ended the way weather ends. The chapter's subject is the other kind, and the tells (rate gaps, fleeing contracts, leaned-on measurements) distinguish them.
- The same currency, same country, can be artificially *cheap* (2011) or artificially *expensive* (now) — and both are tells of manipulation.

## Draft

In 2011 the government of Argentina ordered McDonald's to sell the Big Mac at a loss.

Not for the customers' benefit. The Big Mac had a price problem — specifically, it had the *wrong* price, a price that told the truth. For decades *The Economist* had published its Big Mac Index, a wry global comparison of what the same burger costs in different countries, used by economists as a rough check on whether a currency is over- or under-valued. The Argentine Big Mac, priced honestly, was quietly testifying that the peso was worth far less than the official rate claimed. So the Secretary of Commerce instructed McDonald's to sell it at roughly half the price of the other burgers on the menu, dragging the index down to flatter the currency. McDonald's complied. And then, tellingly, the Big Mac simply *vanished* from the menu boards and the advertising — present if you asked, invisible if you did not — because a fake price is easier to hold when nobody is looking at it. The state had weaponised silence against a burger in a British magazine.

### Prices are the symptom, not the disease

We are taught a simple story about inflation: too much money chasing too few goods, so prices rise. It is not wrong, exactly, but it points at the wrong thing — it makes the rising prices the event. Watch the Big Mac war and the causation runs the other way. The prices were not the disease. They were a *readout.* What the Argentine state was actually fighting was not a number on a menu but the *common knowledge* the number carried — the shared, public, checkable fact of what the peso could buy. Inflation — at least the kind that kills currencies, a distinction we will sharpen in a moment — is not, at bottom, a price phenomenon. It is the cracking of common knowledge about a currency: the supply pillar from Chapter 4 — symmetrically-known supply, the integrity that lets everyone trust how much exists — gives way; the selection loop from Chapter 5 — the shared expectation that the money is good — frays behind it; and *then* prices climb, oscillate, and run, as the visible symptom of an invisible loss of trust. Keep the split from those chapters in view: the *worth* of money — the trades it could unlock — has not evaporated; what has broken is the *coordination* on which token still carries it.

Now the promised distinction, because the word *inflation* names two things that deserve different names. There is the ordinary kind: a harvest fails, a war cuts the oil, a pandemic snarls the ports and a stimulus lands on the snarl — real things get scarce, flush money meets them, and prices rise *inside a unit everyone still trusts.* The ruler is fine; the readings moved, and everyone can see roughly why. The rich world's burst of the early 2020s was mostly this kind, and it ended the way weather ends. And there is the kind this chapter is about: when what cracks is not the supply of goods but the credibility of the unit itself — when people stop believing they know how much money exists, or what the ruler will mean next month. The first is weather. The second is the instrument breaking. And the tells are worth keeping in a pocket, because the two can look alike from inside a supermarket: weather-inflation is loudly and publicly explained, and self-limiting; instrument-inflation comes with a widening gap between official and street rates, with long contracts fleeing the unit — and, surest of all, with a government leaning on the measurements.

This is why some inflations recover and others spiral into the abyss. If the broken common knowledge can be repaired quickly — credibility restored, the supply made legible again — the currency limps and heals. If the institutional trust is gone for a generation, as in Weimar or Zimbabwe, no amount of monetary tinkering brings it back, because the thing that broke was never really the quantity. It was the belief.

### Trust your own basket

Which is why the most honest inflation number you will ever have is not the government's, and not *The Economist's.* It is your own. Headline CPI is an aggregate — methodologically opaque, politically reachable, and, crucially, not specific to the things *you* buy; the official basket and your basket can drift apart for years. A personal index, by contrast — a notebook in which you write down what a coffee, a bus pass, a haircut, a week's groceries actually cost you, month after month — is more honest precisely because it is too small to be worth corrupting. The state can lean on McDonald's. It cannot lean on your notebook.

And the suppression, when it comes, is itself the cleanest signal you will get. When the Argentine government pressured the Big Mac off the menu, the *New York Times* and *The Economist* eventually exposed the manoeuvre — and within days the price leapt twenty-six per cent, the truth springing back the instant the thumb lifted. A government suppressing a measurement is telling you, more reliably than the measurement ever could, that the measurement was right. Watch what they hide; it is a better index than what they publish.

And the lesson runs both ways, which is the final twist. By the mid-2020s, under a very different government and a crawling-peg policy, Argentina had swung to the *opposite* distortion — its Big Mac now among the most expensive in the world, the peso held artificially *strong.* Same burger, same country, opposite lie. An honest price made artificially cheap and an honest price made artificially dear are both tells of a currency managed against the truth. The index is not telling you the burger changed. It is telling you the ruler did.

### The famine with food in it

There is a harder version of this, and it is the reason the point matters well beyond a grocery bill.

In 1943 a nine-year-old boy in Bengal watched people walk into his schoolyard and die there. Decades later, as an economist, he went back to the question that had never left him — why they died — and found something that should not have been possible. There had been no exceptional shortage. Food in the province that year was not dramatically below years in which nobody starved at all. Two to three million people died with the food in the country.

What had failed was not the supply. It was the *claims* on it. A wartime boom pulled rice toward the cities and the war effort, and prices went with it; wages in the countryside did not follow. The people who died were identifiable by occupation — agricultural labourers, fishermen, barbers, transport workers — the ones whose earnings could not chase a price that had left them behind. Their claim on food broke while the food sat in the market, visible, counted, and adequate in the only number anyone was looking at. The total was survivable. The distribution of claims beneath it was not.

That is this book's argument about aggregates, stated at the highest stakes it will ever have to carry: **nobody eats a total.** Every gentler version we have run — the official basket that drifts from yours, the average that says your rent is fine — is the same shape at survivable scale. The aggregate was never merely unreliable. It was the wrong object to be looking at, and its adequacy was not evidence of anything at all about the people inside it.

The same economist made a second claim, and it is worth carrying at its narrow strength rather than its famous one: he held that no substantial famine has occurred in an independent, democratic country with a relatively free press. Read as a claim about ballots it is contested, and fairly — there are cases that sit awkwardly against it. Read as a claim about *information* it is harder to dislodge, and it is the version that concerns us: a distribution failure is legible to somebody long before it is fatal, and the question is only whether that somebody can be heard while being heard still helps. Which is a thing this book keeps arriving at from other directions, and will arrive at once more before the end.

Sometimes the manipulation is enough to keep a currency limping along — distorted, but alive. Sometimes the anchor does not bend but breaks; the common knowledge does not crack but shatters; and a whole population has to find new money overnight. That is the next chapter, and its protagonist is someone we already know well.
