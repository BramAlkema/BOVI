# Chapter 3: Consent as Orthogonal

> *Mode classification describes the shape of an exchange; consent provides legitimacy. They are separate axes.*

**Part I — The Anthropology of Exchange** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 2](02-four-modes.md) · Next: [Chapter 4](04-what-money-is.md)

## Opening scaffold

Two scenes side by side — a kink couple negotiating with safe words and a soldier reporting for compulsory service. Both are in Obligated mode. One is voluntary, one is not.

## Structural beats

1. The two scenes.
2. We draw out: same mode shape, opposite consent profiles.
3. The full table — voluntary and coerced versions of all four modes.
4. The framework refuses to flatten ethics by relocating it to a separate axis.
5. Why this matters — the eight-cell matrix beats the four-cell one for moral reasoning.
6. Most political fights live at the boundary between voluntary and coerced versions of the *same* mode, not between modes.

## Themes

- Ethics is not embedded in structure.
- Consent and exit conditions are the load-bearing legitimacy carriers.
- The framework expands rather than collapses moral analysis.

## Twists

- The reader expects "Obligated = coerced = bad." The chapter shows people actively choosing Obligated relationships (gym, dojo, religious order, BDSM, military enlistment) for reasons that are not pathological.
- Authority can be wanted.

- Establishes that the framework has *two* axes — mode and consent — which together produce an eight-cell moral matrix.

## Draft

Two rooms, the same evening.

In the first, a couple is negotiating. There are rules being set down with unusual care — what will happen, what will not, and a single word that either of them can say at any moment to make everything stop. The arrangement they are building is frankly hierarchical: for the next while, one will give orders and the other will follow them. It is, in the technical sense we now have, pure **Obligated** mode — an exchange organised entirely around rank.

In the second room, a nineteen-year-old is also entering an Obligated arrangement. He has received a letter. The letter informs him that the state requires two years of his life, that he will report on a stated date, that he will obey, and that the penalty for declining is prison. He, too, is about to spend a long while taking orders. His exchange is also organised entirely around rank.

By the only tool we have so far — the four modes — these two scenes are the *same*. Both are Obligated. Both are an authority, a subordinate, and a flow of compliance. And yet anyone can feel that something enormous separates them, something the four modes cannot name. The couple built their hierarchy together, out of trust, with an exit always within reach. The state built the young man's hierarchy without asking, and bolted the exit shut.

What separates them is not the *mode*. It is the *consent* — and it runs on a different axis entirely.

### Shape is not legitimacy

This is the correction the last chapter promised. The four modes tell us the *shape* of an exchange — whether it is a trade, a reciprocity, a ranking, or a sharing. They do not tell us whether that shape is all right. For that we need a second question, asked alongside the first: *did the parties consent, and can they leave?*

It would be tidier if ethics lived inside the modes — if we could say Value is cold, Immediate is warm, Obligated is sinister, and sort the world that way. The framework refuses this, and the refusal matters. Goodness is not a property of the shape. Each mode has a clean version and a coerced one, and the distance between them is the whole of the moral question.

| | Voluntary | Coerced |
|---|---|---|
| **Value** | shopping when you like | forced labour at any wage |
| **Balanced** | spotting a friend lunch | *"after everything I've done for you"* |
| **Obligated** | the dojo, the vows, the safe-word | the draft, the rent you cannot refuse |
| **Immediate** | a gift freely given | *"we're a family here"* — so work the weekend |

Read the table down the middle and you can feel that the modes are innocent. The same shape sits on both sides; only the consent has changed. A gift and an emotional extortion are both Immediate. A dojo and a conscription are both Obligated. What makes one humane and the other a violation is not the mode at all. It is whether you walked in, and whether you can walk out.

### Authority can be wanted

Most of us carry an unexamined equation — *Obligated equals coerced equals bad* — and it is worth dismantling on its own, because so much follows from it. People enter Obligated relationships eagerly, all the time, for reasons that are not pathologies. The gym member submits to a trainer's authority precisely so it will override her on the morning she would otherwise quit. The novice takes monastic vows; the apprentice binds himself to a master; the couple in the first room hand each other power; the volunteer enlists. In each case a person *chooses* to be ranked below another, because the ranking buys something — discipline, skill, devotion, intensity — that equality could not.

Authority, in other words, can be wanted. What we object to when we object to authority is almost never the rank itself. It is the absence of consent and the absence of exit. Get those two right and hierarchy becomes one of the most useful things humans do. Get them wrong and the gentlest-looking mode becomes a cage.

### Where the real fights are

Hold both axes and a great deal of political argument rearranges itself. We tend to imagine our fiercest disputes are *between modes* — market against state, the libertarian against the social democrat, Value against Obligated. Some are. But an astonishing number turn out to be fights along the consent axis *within a single mode* — two people who agree on the shape and disagree only about whether it was chosen.

Is a low-wage job a voluntary Value trade or a coerced one? Both sides agree it is Value; they are fighting about the exit — whether *take it or starve* counts as a choice. Is a tax an Obligated contribution we ratified at the ballot box, or an extraction we never agreed to? Everyone agrees it is Obligated; the fight is entirely about consent. Is a marriage a chosen Immediate bond or an imposed one? The mode is not in dispute. The exit is.

This is why the eight-cell picture — four modes, two consent states — does more moral work than the four-cell one ever could. It lets us stop accusing each other of being in the *wrong mode* and start asking the sharper, answerable question: *was this chosen, and can it be left?* We will lean on it in three places especially — when a state bootstraps a currency by coercion, when an Argentine back-room exchange turns out to be *freer* than the official market, and when a workplace flies the family flag to take unpaid weekends. Each is a consent story wearing a mode's clothes.

We now have both axes — shape and legitimacy — and together they are enough to turn loose on anything humans exchange: parking, sex, healthcare, weddings, work. But the most studied case of all, and the one with the strangest history, is money. Before we apply the framework to the world, we should look hard at what the world keeps mistaking money *for*. That is Part II — and it begins by refusing almost everything you were taught about what money is.
