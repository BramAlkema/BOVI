# Chapter 3: Consent as Orthogonal

> *Mode classification describes the shape of an exchange; consent provides legitimacy. They are separate axes.*

**Part I — The Anthropology of Exchange** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 2](02-four-modes.md) · Next: [Chapter 4](04-what-money-is.md)

## Opening scaffold

Two scenes side by side — a kink couple negotiating with safe words and a soldier reporting for compulsory service. Both are in Obligated mode. One is voluntary, one is not.

## Structural beats

1. The two scenes.
2. We draw out: same mode shape, opposite consent profiles.
3. The full table — voluntary and coerced versions of all four modes.
4. The framework refuses to flatten ethics by relocating it to a separate axis.
5. Why this matters — the eight-cell matrix beats the four-cell one for moral reasoning.
6. Most political fights live at the boundary between voluntary and coerced versions of the *same* mode, not between modes.

## Themes

- Ethics is not embedded in structure.
- Consent and exit conditions are the load-bearing legitimacy carriers.
- The framework expands rather than collapses moral analysis.

## Twists

- The reader expects "Obligated = coerced = bad." The chapter shows people actively choosing Obligated relationships (gym, dojo, religious order, BDSM, military enlistment) for reasons that are not pathological.
- Authority can be wanted.

## Subarcs

- Sets up Chapter 6 (state coercion as bootstrap), Chapter 13 (the cueva inverting state-vs-informal coercion), and Chapter 16 (workplace authority).
- Establishes that the framework has *two* axes — mode and consent — which together produce an eight-cell moral matrix.

## Closing scaffold

We now have both axes. We can apply them to anything humans exchange. But the most studied case — money — has its own peculiar history we should examine first.
