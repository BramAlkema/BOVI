## Draft

One last time, the bridal party — and this time we are not diagnosing the fight. We are asking what would have prevented it.

By now the answer is almost obvious. The fight happened because six people brought four modes to one weekend and had no shared way to hold them apart. They reached, as people do, for the tool they had — a Splitwise spreadsheet — and it made things worse, because Splitwise knows only one mode. It turns the gift the mother offered, the favour one friend did, the thing another simply wanted to treat everyone to, all into the same flat column of who-owes-whom. The tool did not resolve the mode collision; it imposed a mode and called it fairness. What the bridal party needed was a tool that knew there were four.

### The rope, updated

We have met the right primitive already, ten thousand years ago, lying in the middle of a village. A tally — a shared, public record of who has given and not yet received, that everyone can inspect and no one can quietly forge. The village rope did for four traders exactly what six friends need now: it let unfinished exchange be held in the open, netted, and settled, without forcing every trade to square immediately or harden into a personal debt. Only the materials have changed. We do not need fibre and pegs; we have screens and a network. But the design is the rope's, and it is worth stating plainly because almost no modern money tool has it: a single shared ledger, visible to everyone in the group, that nets circular obligations automatically and remembers what memory cannot.

The one thing we would add to the rope — the thing the village did not need, because everyone could see the whole rope at once — is *modes.* Call it the **Tally.** Picture the same shared ledger, but every entry carries a tag, chosen when it is made: **O**, **V**, **B**, or **I**. This dinner was a Value split — pay for what you had. This was Balanced — we'll even it out over the month. This rent payment was Obligated — due, not up for negotiation. And this one was Immediate — I wanted to treat you; *do not* enter it as a debt; mark it untouchable, a gift the ledger records only so no one mistakes it for a loan. The maths behind each tag differs — Value settles now, Balanced nets over time, Obligated carries a deadline and a penalty, Immediate never settles at all, because settling it would be the insult. One canvas, everyone's net position visible, circular debts dissolving on their own, and — the crucial part — the *mode* of every exchange preserved instead of flattened.

Say it the way Chapter 4 would. The four buttons are the four clearing disciplines, restored. Money, we saw there, is what happened when one of the four — clear-it-now, between strangers — got its own infrastructure, and the other three stayed behind in memory, rank, and love. The Tally is the rest of the ledger coming home: one surface that can hold all four disciplines at once, without forcing any entry into the wrong one.

### A tool that respects relationships

This is more than a feature list, because of what the modes carry. A tool that strips mode information does not merely lose data; it strip-mines relationships, quietly converting gifts into debts and friendships into accounts — exactly the damage Splitwise does, exactly the damage the bridal party suffered. A tool that *keeps* mode information does the opposite: it lets a group be honest about what kind of exchange each thing was, and so lets the friendship survive the bookkeeping. To enter a Tally with someone — a flatmate, a friend group, a partner, a festival squad, a band splitting its takings — is therefore not a small act. It is a small relationship-defining commitment: an agreement to keep a shared, mode-aware ledger, and to be honest, together, about which mode each thing belonged to.

It is worth being exact about what kind of visibility this is, because a shared ledger is not automatically a kindness. The Tally works because everyone on it is on it the same way: each of them can see the whole rope, each of them writes to it, and any of them can leave the group without losing a home or a wage. Visibility among equals who chose each other, and can walk, is a tool. The identical surface operated by someone who is *not* in the group — a landlord, an employer, a platform, anyone whose position lets them watch without being watched — is not the same object at all, whatever the screen looks like. It has become an instrument. So the question to ask of any ledger somebody offers you is not *how much does it show*, but **who can see whom, and which of us is free to leave.**

And — because we have not forgotten the hardest problem in money — the Tally does not pretend to escape the cold-start. A new shared ledger with no one on it is worth nothing, the same trap that killed Hayek's perfect currency. So the Tally does not try to be a network of its own. It inherits one. It lives on top of the chat apps where the groups already are — the WhatsApp threads, the iMessage groups, the Signal chats — so the bridal party can start a Tally inside the conversation they are already having. The bootstrap is borrowed, honestly, from the platforms that already solved it. That is the whole trick, and we named it chapters ago.

There is one more thing the Tally carries, and it may be the most subversive. Open any banking app on earth and look at what it shows you: a single number, presented like the level of grain in a silo — a stock of stuff you *have*. That interface is a tiny catechism. Glanced at ten times a day by billions of people, it teaches the push picture more effectively than any textbook ever taught anything: money as a substance, sitting somewhere, holding worth the way a tank holds fuel. This is a large part of why the folk physics feels so natural — the most-checked screen in human history confirms it at every glance. No mainstream money tool has ever shown its user the other picture. The Tally does, structurally: what it displays is not a stock but a *position* — where you stand in a small web of unfinished exchange, what has flowed, what leans, what must clear and what never will — worth exactly what it lets the next exchange be. A user who lives on that surface for a season absorbs the pull picture the way this book's reader absorbed the modes: without being lectured. Products carry frameworks. The existing products carry the wrong one.

We allow ourselves this one piece of design because it completes a loop the book has been drawing from the start. The framework is not only a way of *seeing*; it can be built *into* the tools we use, so the seeing becomes the using. The rope was the right idea before there were screens. The Tally is the rope, with the modes made explicit and the network borrowed — the framework, finally, as a thing you could hold.

You, defended alone, with a notebook. You, defended with others, by a mode-aware ledger. What remains is to gather up everything the book has quietly handed you, and put it where you can carry it out the door.
