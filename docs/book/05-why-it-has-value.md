# Chapter 5: Why It Has Value

> *Money's value is floorless but fruitful: the trades it unlocks, pulled from the future — not pushed from any substance in the past. Common knowledge of acceptance selects which money wins; it does not create the worth.*

**Part II — Money as Coordination Infrastructure** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 4](04-what-money-is.md) · Next: [Chapter 6](06-cold-start-problem.md)

## Opening scaffold

Mateo, on a rooftop in Buenos Aires, unprompted, explaining why Bitcoin works and why the dollar works for the same reason. He is right that they share a mechanism — but the mechanism he names is only half the answer.

## Structural beats

1. The rooftop monologue: *"anchor plus network — the dollar's anchor is the Fed, Bitcoin's is the math; both work."*
2. **The split the chapter turns on.** Two questions hide inside "why does money have value," and almost everyone answers the wrong one. *What is it worth?* — the value-source. *Why this money and not another?* — selection. The textbook, the chartalist, and the crypto-evangelist all answer the second and believe they have answered the first.
3. **Value-source — floorless but fruitful.** Money is worth the **trades it unlocks**: the cooperation that only happens once it is accepted, that would not have happened otherwise. No floor beneath it — no backing, no intrinsic worth, no metal. Real cargo inside it — the surplus is real. Its worth is *pulled from the future* (what holding it lets you do next), never *pushed from the past* (what stands behind it). A valueless tally carries real cargo; that is the whole point. Intermezzo 8A lives this out in the village.
3b. **The direction, tested — the intuitive kill-shot, deckchair-runnable.** Three tests: (i) the backing dial (1971 both directions — turned to zero, nothing; turned to max among non-acceptors, nothing; the next-trade dial is the wired one); (ii) where the past went (the Zimbabwe hundred-trillion note: past fully intact, worth fully gone — only the *next trade* is missing); (iii) the order a currency dies (savings → contracts → bread: value drains from the far future toward the present; the longest pull snaps first — forward-ref Ch 19). Then the vocabulary installed: **push** (folk physics, worth as cargo loaded from behind) vs **pull** (worth drawn from the margin by the next trade). This is the book's hill; everything else follows from the direction.
4. **Selection — common knowledge of acceptance.** Why *this* token becomes the one is a separate, self-fulfilling fact: I accept it because I expect you will, and you because the next person will. Three inputs feed that expectation — anchored supply, network depth, issuer credibility. Three anchor types — legal, natural, mathematical — equally valid; the *type* does not matter, only that the expectation holds.
5. **The second leg.** Acceptance — *is the money good?* — is one leg. Availability — *is there a market to clear into?* — is the other. They fail separately: good money in a thin market buys nothing; a liquid market in collapsing money clears into nothing.
6. Why the dollar works abroad where no one owes US tax: a *selection* fact (global network, credible anchor) and a *value* fact (the trades it unlocks there). The killer counterexample to naïve chartalism — but notice it refutes a claim about *selection*, not about worth.
7. Why bad currencies fail: the acceptance loop breaks, selection collapses, the trades stop — and only then does the "value" everyone located in the token visibly evaporate, because it was never in the token.

## Themes

- Value is not in the token — and not in the common knowledge either. It is in the **trades the common knowledge unlocks.**
- The anchor type does not matter; that is a fact about selection.
- Chartalism and commodity theory are both real answers — to *which money*, mistaken for *what it's worth*.
- Concede the demand, refuse the label: *"don't let an issuer silently debase the supply"* is a fair governance demand; *"therefore the value is in the metal"* is the essentialist error bolted on top.

## Twists

- The reader expects to be told value comes from acceptance, or the state, or the metal — and discovers those are all answers to *which money wins*, not *what money is worth.*
- The worth was floorless the whole time. The thing that felt most solid about money — that it must be *backed* by something — is the one thing it never needed.

## Subarcs

- Floorless-but-fruitful pays off in Intermezzo 8A (the rope is worth the trades it unlocks, *"not what it's made of"*).
- Sets up the cold-start problem (Ch 6): if selection is self-fulfilling, it still has to *start* somewhere, with no evidence at t=0.
- Mateo first appears here — recurring witness, not protagonist.
- The value/selection split returns in Chapter 18: inflation is the *selection* loop breaking, read as a price event.

## Closing scaffold

Value is the trades; selection is common knowledge; and common knowledge has to start somewhere. Chapter 6 is about why starting it — bootstrapping the expectation from nothing — is the hardest engineering problem in money.

## Draft

Mateo is twenty-two and explaining money to us on a rooftop in Buenos Aires, unprompted, the way only the very young and the very sure can. Below him the city is doing its evening business in three currencies at once.

"Bitcoin isn't magic," he says. "It's a ledger anyone can audit, with a supply the protocol fixes. That's the whole trick — everyone knows how much there is, and no government can change it. And people ask me why it has *value*." He shrugs. "Same reason the dollar does. Anchor plus network. The dollar's anchor is the Fed; Bitcoin's is the maths. Both work."

He is right — more right than most economists — and he has still answered only half the question. There are two questions hiding inside *why does money have value*, and almost everyone, Mateo included, answers the second while believing they have answered the first.

The first is: *what is it worth?* The second is: *why this money and not some other?* Keep them apart and a great deal of confusion lifts at once.

### What it's worth: floorless, but fruitful

Start with worth, because that is the one the textbook fumbles. What is a dollar — or a peso, or a rope-mark, or a Bitcoin — actually *worth*?

Not what it is made of. The polymer of the note is worth nothing; the entry in the ledger is worth nothing; a rope-mark is a smudge of charcoal on fibre. The worth is not *in* the token, and it is not hiding behind the token in a vault either. The worth of money is **the trades it unlocks** — the cooperation that happens only because the thing is accepted, that would not have happened otherwise. The night out you could not have arranged by barter. The surplus from specialising that you would not have dared without a way to clear it. Money is worth exactly the gains from cooperation it lets people reach. (One scope note, now that the modes are in the room: the gains we mean are the *priced* kind — the ones the clear-it-now books carry. The gift was never money's to unlock, and we make no claim on it.)

This is a strange kind of value, and worth naming precisely, because two famous camps get it half-right and stop. There is no *floor* under it — nothing it is grounded in, no prior substance, no backing; the goldbug's search for something solid beneath the note is a search for a thing that was never there. And yet it is not *arbitrary*, not a pure bubble that could just as well be zero, because the trades are real and the surplus is real. **Floorless, but fruitful.** No seabed beneath it; a full hold of genuine cargo inside it.

And notice the direction the worth comes from. It is not *pushed* into the money from the past — from yesterday's gold, yesterday's labour, yesterday's decree, and something behind that, turtles all the way down. It is *pulled* from the future: you want the thing for what accepting it lets you do *next*. The village rope made this almost tangible — a mark is worth nothing for what went into it and everything for the next awkward trade it lets you close. History matters, but only as evidence: yesterday's successful trade makes tomorrow's acceptance more rational. The worth itself faces forward.

### The direction, tested

That direction claim is the most heretical sentence in this book — nearly everyone alive believes the opposite, and no bank, no app, no textbook will tell you otherwise. So do not take it on our word. It makes predictions, and you can test them from a deckchair.

**Turn the backing dial.** In 1971 the United States cut the wire between the dollar and gold — over a weekend, completely. If worth is loaded into money from what stands behind it, the dollar's worth should have drained out. It did not: five decades of working dollar since, inflating at times, collapsing never, and running the world's trade the entire while. Now turn the same dial the other way: fill a vault with gold and issue notes against it among people who will not accept them, and the notes buy nothing — history has run this experiment too, every time a "fully backed" currency died anyway. A dial that does nothing when turned to zero and nothing when turned to maximum is not connected to the machine. Then turn the other dial — the next-trade dial. Wherever the next trade is there, value holds, backed or not: the unbacked dollar, the rope, the M-Pesa balance. Wherever the next trade goes, value goes, backed or not. *That* dial is wired straight in.

**Ask where the past went.** Hold a hundred-trillion-dollar note from Zimbabwe. Everything that ever stood behind it is still there, fully intact: the decrees that ordered it accepted, the labour of the economy that once traded in it, the state that issued it still exists. Nothing behind the note has been removed — and the value is entirely gone. What is missing is not behind it but *in front* of it: there is no next trade. A worth pushed from the past cannot explain a note whose past is complete and whose worth is zero. A worth pulled from the future explains it instantly. (Collectors now pay real money for that note — but notice what they are buying: a souvenir, ornament-value, the diamond kind of worth. The only value the note retains is the kind that was never monetary, and it arrived only after the money in it was completely dead. The push picture's favourite anchor turns out to be what is left over when money is gone.)

**Watch the order in which a currency dies.** Money never dies all at once; it dies in a sequence, and the sequence is the fingerprint. Savings flee first — nobody will hold the currency for next year. Long contracts flee second — nobody will write next year's rent in it. The corner shop accepts it last — it still buys bread *today* even while it can no longer carry a promise to Tuesday. Value drains from the far future toward the present, furthest promises first, the immediate trade last. If worth were pushed from the past it would have no reason to die in that order. A worth pulled from the future dies in *exactly* that order — the longest pull snaps first. We will watch this in slow motion, in a later chapter, in the hands of someone who survived it.

Three tests, one verdict, and now the two pictures deserve their names, because they are the deepest vocabulary this book will hand you. The **push** picture: worth is loaded into money from behind — by the metal, the labour, the decree — and carried along inside it like cargo in a hold. It is the folk physics of money, as natural-feeling and as wrong as the sun circling the earth. The **pull** picture: worth is drawn into money from ahead, at the margin, by the next trade — the one exchange that happens only because this thing is accepted, and would not have happened otherwise. Every test above returns the same answer. The ledger definition, the bundle, the bootstrap — everything else in this book follows from getting this one direction right.

### Which money: the part Mateo got right

*That* is worth. Now the second question, the one Mateo actually answered: of all the things that could carry this floorless-but-fruitful value, why does *this* one win?

Because of **common knowledge of acceptance.** You take the peso because you expect the next person to take it, and they because they expect the person after. It is a self-fulfilling loop, and three things feed the expectation: an anchored supply (you can trust how much exists), a deep network (many others already use it), and a credible issuer (who will keep the rule, not abandon it when keeping it costs). Mateo's "anchor plus network" is this, exactly. And he is right that the *type* of anchor — a central bank's promise, a metal's rarity, an algorithm's rule — does not matter to the holder; all that matters is that the expectation holds. This is the question chartalism answers ("the state makes us"), and the one commodity theory answers ("the metal is wanted"), and the one the crypto-evangelist answers ("the maths is fixed"). They are all real answers — to *which money wins.* None is an answer to *what money is worth*, though each believes it is.

There is a second leg to selection, easy to miss because in a working economy it is always present. Acceptance asks *is the money good?* Availability asks *is there a market to spend it in?* They fail apart: a perfectly trusted coin in a village with nothing to buy clears into nothing; a bustling market in a collapsing currency is full of sellers who will not hold what you pay them. You need both — the money good, and the market there.

This is why the dollar works in a Lima market where no one owes a cent of US tax: a global network and a credible anchor (selection), carrying real trades (worth). It is the clean refutation of the naïve story that money is valuable only because a state demands it for tax — but notice it refutes a claim about *which money*, not about *worth.* And it is why bad currencies die in a particular order: the anchor cracks, the expectation frays, the loop unwinds, people stop accepting — and only *then* does the "value" everyone had located in the banknote visibly evaporate. It evaporates because it was never in the banknote. The trades stopped; the worth was always the trades.

### The thing it never needed

So when someone insists money must be *backed* by something — that beneath the dollar there had better be gold, or beneath the token a reserve, or the whole thing is a confidence trick — we can answer precisely. They are right to want a credible, knowable supply; that is a real demand, and an issuer who debases in secret has betrayed them. *Concede the demand.* But they have bolted onto that fair demand a false conclusion — that the worth therefore lives in the backing, in the metal, in the stuff. *Refuse the label.* The worth was floorless the whole time. A valueless tally, accepted, carries real cargo; a vault of gold no one will trade for carries none. The one thing about money that felt most solid — that it must rest on something — is the one thing it never needed.

Value is the trades; selection is common knowledge; and common knowledge, self-fulfilling as it is, still has to *start* somewhere — from nothing, with no evidence at all that anyone else will join. That turns out to be the hardest engineering problem in money, and it is the next chapter.
