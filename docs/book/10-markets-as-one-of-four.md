# Chapter 10: Markets as One of Four Mechanisms

> *Allocation can happen through Value (price), Balanced (reciprocity), Obligated (command), or Immediate (sharing). Standard economics covers only the first.*

**Part III — Trade, Coordination, Production** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 9](09-transaction-costs.md) · Next: [Chapter 11](11-value-mode.md)

## Opening scaffold

A 12-person crew on a 47-year voyage to another star. They cannot run their economy on prices alone. We watch them discover, by accident, that food allocation requires four different mechanisms running at once.

## Structural beats

1. The voyage scene.
2. Markets allocate via Value (works for anonymous spot trade).
3. Bureaucracy allocates via Obligated (works for what cannot be opted out of).
4. Reciprocity networks allocate via Balanced (works among peers with memory).
5. Communities allocate via Immediate (works for relationships valued above their transactions).
6. Every domain uses some mix.
7. The political question is *which* mode dominates *which* domain — a choice, not a discovery.

## Themes

- Allocation is plural.
- Standard economics is parochial.
- Mechanism choice is political and revealing.

## Twists

- The reader expects "markets vs. command economy" — the dichotomy of late-20th-century debate.
- The chapter shows there are *four* mechanisms, not two, and the binary debate was hiding two whole modes (Balanced and Immediate) that quietly do most of the work.

## Subarcs

- Sets up Part IV (each mode in detail).

## Draft

Twelve people are forty-seven years from anywhere, and they have to decide who eats.

The ship is a generation vessel, crawling between stars; the crew of twelve will live their whole adult lives aboard it, raising the children who will arrive. Early on, someone proposes the obvious thing: run the ship's economy on a market. Price the food, the water, the air-filter maintenance, the medical hours; let people trade; let prices allocate. It works, for a while, for some things. And then it starts producing absurdities, and the crew discovers — not by theory but by friction — that a market alone cannot run a ship, because allocation is not one problem. It is four.

### Four ways to decide who gets what

Some things on the ship really do want a market. When two crew members swap personal rations or trade a turn at the good telescope, **Value** mode is perfect — a clean spot trade between equals, priced, square afterward. Nobody wants the captain rationing telescope time.

But the air filters are not like that. You cannot let someone outbid the rest for a working life-support system, or decline to pay and suffocate. Life support is allocated by **Obligated** mode — an authority ranks above the market and rules that everyone is kept breathing, price be damned. Every society reserves *some* goods this way, the ones too important to leave to willingness-to-pay: a country's courts, its conscription, its clean water. The starship only makes the necessity vivid.

The day-to-day cooperation among the four engineers — covering each other's shifts, lending a hand, remembering who helped whom — runs on neither price nor command. It runs on **Balanced** mode, reciprocity with a memory: *I took your shift last month; you'll take mine.* No money changes hands; a ledger of favours sits in everyone's head, and it works precisely because the four of them will be together for forty years and cannot afford to cheat.

And when a crew member falls seriously ill, something else takes over entirely. Nobody invoices the sick. The crew pools its care, shares its hours, carries the load, because the relationship is worth infinitely more than any transaction inside it. That is **Immediate** mode — sharing without counting — and a ship that could not switch into it for its own wounded would not be a society. It would be a vending machine with people inside.

Four mechanisms. Markets price; bureaucracies command; reciprocity networks remember; communities share. They are, notice, the four clearing disciplines from Chapter 4 run at societal scale: the market clears at once, the bureaucracy clears on the authority's schedule, the reciprocity network nets over years, and the community never settles at all. The crew did not invent them in sequence, one failing into the next. All four were present from the first day — the way they are present in every human group that has ever existed — and the crew's only real task was to notice which good belonged to which, and stop forcing all four through the single channel of price.

### The argument that hid half the economy

Step off the ship and this rearranges a century of politics. The great economic argument of the twentieth century was framed as a binary: *markets versus the state.* Capitalism or communism, price or plan, the invisible hand or the central committee. We chose sides and fought about it for seventy years.

But look at what the binary left out. It saw Value mode (the market) and Obligated mode (the state) and called those the only two options — and in doing so it rendered *invisible* the two modes that, between them, do most of the actual coordinating in any real society. Balanced mode — the vast informal economy of favours, reciprocity, mutual aid, rotating savings, the help between neighbours and the lending between friends — never appeared on the ballot. Immediate mode — the entire economy of family and care and gift, which is where most human beings spend most of their lives and receive most of what they need — was not even counted as economics. Two whole ways of coordinating human life, quietly carrying the species, written out of the debate because the debate had only two boxes.

Standard economics is, in this precise sense, the study of one of the four modes. It is Value-mode economics: very good at the corner of life that is anonymous spot trade between strangers, and largely blind to the other three-quarters. That is not a small omission. It is most of how people actually live.

So the political question is not *markets or the state.* It is the richer, four-cornered one the starship crew had to answer good by good: *which mode should govern which domain?* And that — as the next part of the book will show, mode by mode and then fight by fight — is a *choice*, made and remade by every society, not a fact handed down by nature. To make the choice well we need each mode up close: its own logic, its native institutions, and the particular way each one rots. That is where we go next.
