## Draft

Go back to Doña Elena one last time in this analytical mode, and read her Sunday not as sophistication, and not as warmth, but as *field notes from a forty-year emergency.*

Every move she makes we have admired in turn — the timed pesos, the dollars into stablecoin, the loan, the cueva rate in her head. Now read them the way a casualty reads a battlefield. She does not hold pesos a day longer than the law forces her to, because a peso held is a peso bleeding value; she keeps her savings in USDT because the peso cannot be trusted to store anything; she knows the blue rate because the official one is a fiction she cannot afford to believe. None of this is a strategy she chose. It is a set of scars, each one the healed wound of a specific betrayal by a currency that failed the people who depended on it. The competence we have praised for nineteen chapters is, seen plainly, the competence of a survivor.

### The same collapse, over and over

What happened to Argentina has happened, in its essentials, again and again — Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, Lebanon, Venezuela, a long grim roll. And it always moves in the same order, the order our framework predicts. First the **anchor** breaks: the supply becomes untrustworthy, the common knowledge cracks — the thing from the last chapter. Then the **network** frays: as people lose faith, they begin refusing the currency for the things that matter most, and each refusal makes the next more rational. And then the population **migrates** — not by decree, not by ideology, but by survival, toward whatever anchor they can still reach.

That migration has a name economists use too narrowly: *dollarisation.* But watch what it actually is, and you will recognise it, because it is the whole thesis of this book happening under duress. As the home currency fails, its bundled functions come apart and each migrates to wherever it still works. The medium of exchange limps on for small daily trades. The store of value flees into dollars, or USDT, or gold, or property, or cattle — whatever holds. The unit of account quietly switches: people begin *thinking* in dollars even while spending in pesos. Long-term contracts get written in a foreign unit or an index. The only function the dying currency reliably keeps is the one an authority can compel — taxes, payable in the local money because the state still ranks above the market and says so. That tax demand is the Obligated floor beneath the whole collapsing structure, the single thread keeping the home currency in use at all. And notice the order of the flight — savings first, contracts second, the daily bread last, the tax floor beneath it all. This is the direction test from Chapter 5, read as an autopsy: value drains from the far future toward the present, longest promises first, because it was always hanging from the future. Nothing "behind" the peso changed on the day the savings fled. What changed was ahead of it.

This is **spontaneous unbundling** — the unbundled stack we have spent the book describing, assembling itself overnight, out of necessity, in the hands of people who have never read a word of monetary theory.

It also puts a famous law the right way up. Gresham told us bad money drives out good — people spend the debased coin and hoard the sound one — and that holds *precisely where an authority forces the two to trade at the same official value.* The compulsion is not a wrinkle in the law. It is the law's precondition. Take it away, let people choose, and the thing runs backwards: the good money wins, and the bad is spent as fast as it can be got rid of, a hot potato nobody holds an hour longer than they must. That second case has a name of its own, Thiers' law, and it is what collapse looks like from inside. In Argentina both run at once, on the same afternoon — the peso *compelled* for tax, the dollar *chosen* for everything else. Bad money keeps the till; good money keeps the store. Unbundling again, written in the language of distress.

And there is a second migration underneath the first, easier to miss because no statistic tracks it. When the clear-it-now channel breaks, exchanges do not only move between instruments; they move between *modes*. Trades that yesterday cleared at a price retreat into the disciplines that need no currency: favours remembered, kin credit extended, care pooled — the Balanced and Immediate books, kept in memory and love, absorbing what the Value rail can no longer carry. Doña Elena's Sunday table has never stopped carrying part of the load. And where those older books cannot stretch far enough, people do something even more telling: they weave new ropes. Argentina's collapse in 2001 produced barter networks counted in the millions of members, complete with their own scrip and their own weekly markets — a population re-inventing the minimal ledger from scratch, in real time, because the pull that first created money does not stop pulling when a particular money dies. The modes are not a taxonomy laid over the crisis. They are the shock absorbers a society falls back through — and a people rich in the other three disciplines survives a currency collapse that would break a society that had let them atrophy.

### The mirror: when the anchor holds too well

One more case belongs here, and it looks like the opposite of everything above until you notice what it shares.

A woman in her thirties in Andalusia has never seen a currency fail. The money in her pocket is among the most credible on earth — its supply knowable, its issuer impersonal, its minutes published, its inflation boring for as long as she can remember. Nothing about it has broken. And unemployment among people her age, in her region, has spent most of her adult life at levels no rich country would tolerate as a national figure. Three of her four siblings work somewhere else.

Her region's unemployment was high before the euro existed, and any serious account of why runs through a labour market split between the permanently employed and the permanently temporary, a construction boom that broke, and a good deal more. The currency did not make it. What the currency did was narrower and survives all of that: when the shock arrived, the channel that would ordinarily have taken some of it was gone, and what a currency cannot absorb gets absorbed by people.

Doña Elena's anchor was too loose, and *value* fled. Hers is, for the place she happens to live, too rigid — and so *people* flee instead. When a region inside a shared currency falls behind the average, it cannot become cheaper by having its money become cheaper; there is no longer a money of its own to move. So the adjustment comes out of wages, then jobs, then the Sunday bus to somewhere hiring. Economists have a name for the condition — a currency area is only comfortable when the regions inside it can absorb different shocks, by moving people, by moving money between regions, or by wages that fall as easily as they rise. Where none of those is available, the shock is absorbed the last way left.

It is the same variable failing in opposite directions. Doña Elena's money could not hold still; the Andalusian's cannot move. Both are elasticity; in both cases what the money could not absorb was absorbed by people instead. The difference is that only one of them was reported as a crisis. A collapse announces itself. A rigidity just quietly empties a province over thirty years, and is filed under regional policy.

### What the survivor knows

Here is the reframing the chapter has been moving toward, and it closes the inversion the whole book is built on. We opened by placing Doña Elena against the Berlin barista and asking you to notice that the barista's invisible, effortless money is the historical exception and Doña Elena's juggling is the human norm. Now we can say why her competence matters so much: *she can see the thing the barista cannot.* The person living inside a broken currency knows, in her body, that money was never one thing — because she has watched it come apart into its component jobs and had to hold each one separately to survive. The person living inside a working currency has been spared that knowledge, and the sparing is a real mercy, and it is also a kind of blindness. She mistakes the bundle for the nature of money, because her bundle has never failed.

The framework in this book was not, in any honest sense, built by economists. It was built by the millions of people like Doña Elena who were forced to learn it the hard way, and who have been running sophisticated unbundled monetary architectures on instinct and necessity for as long as currencies have failed — which is to say, always. We are not teaching them anything. We are reading their practice back to ourselves, slowly, and giving it the words it never needed.

Doña Elena is not a remarkable exception. There are, on any honest count, billions of people closer to her condition than to the barista's. The next chapter steps back from her at last, to survey the whole vast unbundled world the comfortable economies have spent a century mistaking for a backwater — and that turns out to be the actual present, and quite possibly the future, of money.
