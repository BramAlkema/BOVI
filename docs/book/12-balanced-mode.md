## Draft

The bridal party is back, and this time we can see exactly what is wrong.

Three weeks before the wedding, six women are at war over a spreadsheet — the same fight we watched in the second chapter, when we had no words for it. Now we do. The fight is a **Balanced** mode dispute, and more precisely a Balanced-mode *pathology*: a group trying to keep a ledger of evenness and discovering the ledger has turned on them.

### The mode of even

Balanced mode is reciprocity with a memory. *Are we even?* — that is its tell, its single anxious question. It governs the exchanges between peers in an ongoing relationship who keep a running, usually unspoken, account of give and take. You buy this round; I'll buy the next. You watered my plants; I'll feed your cat. You spotted me twenty; here it is back. No authority sets the terms, as in Obligated; no price clears the trade, as in Value; and crucially, unlike Immediate, *we do count* — gently, over time, with the understanding that the books should roughly balance in the end. It is the mode of friendship, of flatmates, of colleagues who are not quite friends, of the long warm web of favours that holds ordinary life together.

When it works, it is nearly invisible, which is its charm. Two friends can trade hundreds of small kindnesses over decades and never once feel they are transacting, because the ledger is loose, forgiving, and mutual. Nobody keeps receipts. The evenness is felt, not calculated.

### Two ways it rots

It rots in two characteristic ways, and the bridal party manages both at once.

The first is the **weaponised ledger** — when one party stops keeping the books loosely and starts keeping them precisely, as leverage. *I did X for you, so you owe me Y.* The moment a favour is named as a debt and presented for collection, the warmth drains out of Balanced mode and what remains is a cold accounting with a grievance attached. The friend who reminds you, pointedly, of every ride she ever gave you is not being fair. She is converting a relationship of loose reciprocity into a precise ledger she intends to win.

The second is **equal-split-as-false-balance** — the bridesmaids' real problem. An even division *looks* like Balanced fairness; it has the shape of evenness. But splitting a luxury weekend six ways is only even if the six have equal capacity, and they do not: two are lawyers, one is a teacher counting the weekend in rent. A split that ignores capacity is not balance. It is balance's costume worn over an outcome one party experiences as extraction. The teacher is not bad at maths. She is doing Balanced-mode fairness correctly — *are we really even, if this costs you a coffee and me a week?* — and finding her friends have stopped at the arithmetic.

### When the tool picks the mode

There is a deeper culprit in the fight, and it is sitting on all their phones. Splitwise — the friendly little app that tracks who owes whom — does something quietly radical: it runs *everything* through Balanced mode. Every shared dinner, every gift, every round becomes a line item, a debt, a number to be squared. And in doing so it silently converts the other modes into Balanced ones. A gift (Immediate — *I wanted to treat you*) becomes a debt you now owe. A moment of communal generosity becomes an entry to be settled. The friction the app removes — the awkwardness of money among friends — turns out to be the friction of *what kind of relationship the friends are having.* By making everything even, it makes everything counted, and some things are damaged by being counted.

This is the trap of the tool that does not know about modes. Splitwise is not malicious; it is mode-blind, and a mode-blind tool imposes one mode by default. In the terms of Chapter 4: it keeps only one kind of books, and forces every entry to clear on the ledger's schedule — now, or nagged until squared — including the entries that were never supposed to clear at all. We will come back to this in the final part of the book, because it points straight at what a *better* tool would do — one that knew a gift from a debt, and let the Immediate things stay uncounted. (We will call it the Tally, and this bridal party is its reason for existing.)

### The mode that banks half the world

It would be easy, after a chapter of pathologies, to file Balanced mode as the awkward one — the mode of resentment and unspoken scores. So it is worth seeing it doing serious work, at a scale that dwarfs anything in this chapter so far.

Across most of the world there is an arrangement with a different name in every language and the same shape everywhere. A *tanda* in Mexico. A *chama* in Kenya. A *susu* in West Africa and the Caribbean. A *committee* in Pakistan, a *hui* in China, a *gam'iya* in Egypt. A fixed group of people — neighbours, market traders, colleagues, women who have known each other for years — each puts in the same amount on the same day, and the whole pot goes to one member. Next round, the next member. Round it goes until everyone has taken the pot once, and then, very often, it begins again.

Look at what is not there. No interest. No collateral. No institution, no application, no credit score. Nobody is in debt, exactly; the books simply lean, and are trusted to right themselves by the end of the cycle — which is the definition of Balanced mode, running as financial infrastructure for hundreds of millions of people, and older by centuries than any central bank. The enforcement is not legal. It is the standing you would lose. Take your pot in the second round and vanish, and you have not defaulted on a loan; you have stopped being someone these people will do anything with, in a town where that matters more than a credit file.

Which is the same mechanism as the bridal party, working. Everything that broke in the spreadsheet fight is present here and intact: the group agrees what mode this is, agrees the ledger openly, and knows what happens if the evenness is not honoured. The pathology is not Balanced mode. It is Balanced mode without an agreement. We will meet these clubs again when we survey the world's unbundled stacks, where they sit alongside mobile money and foreign currency as ordinary furniture.

### The defence

The defence in Balanced mode is to say the ledger out loud. Most Balanced-mode pain comes from *secret* scores — accounts one person is keeping and the other does not know about, or a split presented as obvious that one party experiences as unfair. Naming it dissolves it: *are we keeping track here — and if so, are we tracking the right thing: money, or capacity, or care?* The bridal party fight cannot be solved by arguing the amount. It can only be solved by naming the mode, asking what kind of weekend it is supposed to be, and deciding the ledger together rather than discovering it apart.

Balanced runs on memory and mutual consent. The next mode runs on neither. It runs on authority.
