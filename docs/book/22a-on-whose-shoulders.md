# Intermezzo 22A: On Whose Shoulders

## Themes

- Borrowed is the strength, not the apology. A teaching tool should carry what the giants settled, not a pet theory.
- The honest contribution is the *assembly*, not the invention — and assembly is a real thing.

## Draft

By now you have a way of seeing. You can look at a banknote and see a claim, look at a price and see one public settlement ratio among many, look at a *"we're a family here"* and see Value mode wearing Immediate's clothes. It is tempting — for you, and for us — to feel that this seeing is ours, handed over like a thing we made.

It is not. We made none of it. Every load-bearing idea in this book belongs to someone else, usually someone who said it more carefully, a century earlier, in a language we have only translated. Before the last chapter hands you the vocabulary to keep, this one names the debt. And the naming is not a modesty ritual — it is the evidence. A way of seeing money that *no one had ever thought* would be a reason for suspicion, not trust. That this one is assembled from people who spent their lives on it is exactly why you should believe it.

The strange part is not that economics lacked the correction. It is that economics knew it in pieces. Value theory removed value from objects. Monetary theory produced fiat equilibria. Banking theory found the balance sheets. Institutional economics put law and power inside the market. Anthropology buried the barter age. Each specialist spent a career unteaching one piece of the inherited picture, while the introductory story kept reinstalling the whole thing for the next class. No conspiracy was required. No professor had to believe the complete ontology. The discrepancy lived between chapters, where nobody was assigned to look.

So: whose shoulders.

**The ones who told us what value is.** Georg Simmel, in 1900, wrote a *Philosophy of Money* most economists never read, and put the whole thing in a sentence: value is not a property of things; it is the distance between us and what we want, and the sacrifice of crossing it. Money's worth was never *in* the coin. It was the distance the coin lets us cross. Don Patinkin, half a century later, made the point respectable to economists — value money by the *holding* of it, he said, and stop hunting for the substance underneath. There is no substance, and you do not need one.

**The ones who told us what money is.** Alfred Mitchell-Innes was a British diplomat, not an economist, which is probably why he could say it plainly: money and credit, he wrote in 1913, are the same thing seen from two sides — there is no coin that is not, underneath, a record of a debt. Narayana Kocherlakota gave it the cold modern proof: *money is memory.* Perry Mehrling has spent a career teaching that all of banking is a swap of IOUs, balance sheets all the way up. The rope in our village was theirs before it was ours.

**The one whose two lessons the textbook tore apart.** Carl Menger saw, in 1871, that value is subjective and that money is the most *saleable* good — chosen because it is the easiest thing to pass on again. Those claims need not conflict. Subjective value tells us where value comes from; saleability helps explain why one candidate is selected as money. But the textbook tradition kept his commodity-emergence story and quietly suspended his value theory when the commodity became money. A thing wanted for prior uses became a thing with "intrinsic value"; a route through the cold start became a floor beneath monetary worth. His student's student, Ludwig von Mises, hardened prior non-monetary demand into a regression requirement. We keep the serious question inside that theorem — how today's expectation acquires yesterday's evidence — and refuse its terminus in material substance. The ledger service can supply the first use. The rope does not have to be woven from gold.

**The one who wanted money to rust.** Silvio Gesell was a merchant in Buenos Aires — of all the cities — before he was a monetary heretic. He noticed that a good *medium* and a good *store* pull against each other, and proposed the unthinkable fix: money that loses a little if you sit on it, so that it keeps moving. Keynes, who agreed with almost no one, said the future would learn more from Gesell than from Marx. Bernard Lietaer, who worked at Belgium's central bank on the ECU — the euro's precursor unit — and then spent his later years building that currency's opposites, turned the heresy into working community currencies.

**The one whose perfect argument no one used.** Friedrich Hayek wrote, in 1976, an airtight case for competing private money. Nobody adopted it — not because it was wrong, but because no one wanted to be the first to hold a currency no one else held yet. Hayek's beautiful failure is still the clearest reminder in the literature that good design is not adoption — and the designs since that nobody could have stopped have failed the same way, which is how we know it was never only the law. The cold start is real, and it humbles everyone.

**The ones who gave us the modes — and this is the part of the book that feels most ours, which is exactly why it is most theirs.** Alan Fiske went to Burkina Faso to study something else and came home with four ways every human being, everywhere, structures a relationship: sharing, ranking, matching, pricing. Even the observation this book leans on hardest — that his four patterns are the four classic scales of measurement, from not-counting to counting-in-ratios, which is what let us call the modes four disciplines of one ledger — is Fiske's own; we only carried it to the ledger. Paul Bohannan, among the Tiv of Nigeria, watched general-purpose money arrive and *collapse* the separate spheres a society had carefully kept apart — and watched the moral disquiet that followed, which is the same disquiet a bride feels when the wedding becomes a spreadsheet. Viviana Zelizer showed that we never did treat money as the cold solvent the textbooks describe: we *earmark* it, we keep the funeral money apart from the winnings, we make special monies for special bonds. Behind them stand the elders — Marcel Mauss on the gift, Karl Polanyi on the forms of exchange a market society forgot it had, Marshall Sahlins on how reciprocity bends with kinship distance. The four modes are not our discovery. They are anthropology's. We have only carried them into the room where the money is kept.

**The one who buried the barter myth.** David Graeber spent five thousand years of history establishing what anthropologists had quietly known for a century: there was never a barter age that money came along to rescue. Credit came first. We have leaned on him every time we refused to open a story with two strangers swapping a chicken for an axe.

**And the one who counted the pins.** Adam Smith — yes, that one — actually counted: a lone worker could scarce make one pin in a day, and certainly not twenty; ten specialising workers made upward of forty-eight thousand. The entire prize of money, orders of magnitude of it, was sitting in his pin factory in 1776, waiting for someone to notice it was a *coordination* win and not a *production* one.

There are more, and we have mostly kept them in the footnotes where they will not slow you down. Richard Cantillon, who noticed three centuries ago that whoever receives new money *first* quietly robs whoever receives it last. Ronald Coase and Douglass North, who showed that the real economy is the cost of coordinating it. Barry Eichengreen and Albert Hirschman, who between them explain why the holder of a weaponised currency has no *voice* and a long, expensive road to *exit*. And the living company we are honoured to keep on the same shelf — Felix Martin, Geoffrey Ingham, Graeber again, Mehrling — who have been telling the relational story to ordinary readers while we were still finding our words.

So what, in the end, did we add? One honest thing: the arrangement. We put these people in a room together and turned on a light, and saw that Simmel's *distance* and Innes's *credit* and Gesell's *rust* and Fiske's *four modes* were all describing one object from different doorways. That is not nothing — Mendeleev rearranged elements no one disputed, and the arrangement *was* the discovery. But it is not a new element. We have proved no theorem these people did not already prove.

So when the next chapter hands you your sight and calls it a gift, hold it the way you would hold any gift that cost the giver a whole life: gratefully, and knowing it was never the wrapper's. The seeing is a debt. We are only paying it forward.
