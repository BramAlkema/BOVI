## Draft

One last time, the €16 coffee — and now we care neither about the friction nor the definition. We care that it was *done to her.* (The state's fictional exchange rate did the heaviest lifting, as we saw — but the engines that stacked their skims on top of it chose her, specifically.)

She did not choose to enter a Value-mode pricing event. She thought she was buying a coffee. A system she could not see — her bank, the card network, a dynamic-currency-conversion engine that knew her home country, her card type, her ignorance of the local rate — decided, in milliseconds, to run the transaction in the mode most profitable to itself, and to do so at the precise moment she was least able to object. This is not a confused friend assuming the wrong mode at dinner. This is an adversary, choosing a mode against you, on purpose, with better information than you have. It has a name: **adversarial mode forcing.** In ledger terms, it is being forced onto the counterparty's clearing discipline — priced now, on their information, at the moment you can least refuse.

### The arms race over your willingness to pay

The engine underneath it is the oldest dynamic in commerce, newly supercharged. A seller would always love to know the most you would pay and charge exactly that; a buyer would always love to know the real market price and pay no more. For most of history the buyer had a fighting chance — prices were posted, markets were public, the baker did not know your salary. The digital economy has quietly demolished that symmetry. The seller now knows your postcode, your device, your browsing history, the hour of night you are shopping, whether your battery is low, how many times you have looked at this item. You know almost nothing in return. The price is no longer a public fact; it is a private estimate of your weakness.

This is why the personal price-tracking we will recommend later is not a productivity tip but a *defensive* one — and why it provokes the other side to escalate. As buyers acquire tools to discover the real price, sellers acquire tools to obscure it: the unique price shown only to you, the discount that evaporates when you clear your cookies, the fare that rises because you searched the route twice. It is an arms race, and at the moment the buyers are losing it badly, because the asymmetry of information runs entirely the seller's way.

And it wears two disguises especially worth knowing. The first sounds like a kindness: *personalised pricing.* A decade of helpful recommendations has trained the word "personalised" to sound like a gift. In pricing it means the opposite — a price computed *against* you, aimed at your specific willingness to pay: weaponised Value mode in the language of service. The second sounds like warmth: *we're a family here* — Value-mode extraction (work the weekend, take the lower salary, give the extra mile) flown under the Immediate banner, because Immediate mode's defencelessness, its refusal to count, is exactly what the employer is counting on. And a third, blunter one simply tries to end the conversation: *it's just market rate* — three words deployed to collapse a negotiation that had other modes available, insisting the exchange was always and only a Value transaction so no other basis can be raised.

### The defence, and the uncomfortable twist

The defences are the ones we have built all along, now aimed at an adversary rather than a friend. *Name the basis:* this is being priced; who decided it should be priced, and on what information? *Refuse the frame:* "it's just market rate" is a move, not a fact; you may decline to accept that this was a Value-mode exchange. *Invoke a different mode, out loud:* sometimes simply saying "I thought we were friends here, not counterparties" changes what is possible. None of these wins every time. All of them break the spell of the imposed frame, which is most of what the imposition relies on.

There is an uncomfortable twist here for the comfortable reader, and it closes a loop we opened in the first chapter. We have spent the book treating Doña Elena, juggling her currencies in a broken system, as the exposed one, and the Berlin barista, tapping her card without a thought, as the protected one. On *this* axis the order reverses. The barista, precisely because her environment is so smooth, so digitised, so frictionless, is *more* exposed to adversarial mode forcing, not less. Every frictionless tap is a data point; every invisible payment is a price she did not check; the very convenience that hides the machinery from her also hands the machinery to whoever runs it. Doña Elena, forced by a hostile currency to inspect every transaction, has defences the barista has been lulled out of. Smooth money is comfortable. Comfort is exactly the condition adversarial pricing needs.

At the scale of one coffee, mode-forcing is a private friction — annoying, recoverable, yours to fight or shrug off. But the same move made at the scale of a whole society — deciding which mode shall govern healthcare, or housing, or the care of the old — stops being friction and becomes the central question of politics. That is the next chapter.
