## Draft

A child breaks an arm. What happens next depends entirely on which country the arm is in — and the differences are not medical. They are about which mode a society has decided should govern its medicine.

In the United States the arm is largely a **Value** transaction: a bill arrives, itemised, and the family pays it, or fights it, or is bankrupted by it. In Britain the same arm is **Obligated**: the state ranks above the market and rules that you are treated, free at the point of use, funded by a tax you did not choose line by line. Across much of sub-Saharan Africa it is **Immediate**: there is no system to bill, so the family simply finds the money, because you are theirs and that is what family is. And across much of northern Europe it is **Balanced**: a social-insurance pool into which everyone pays and from which everyone draws, even over a lifetime — a great mutualised ledger of contributions and claims. One injury. Four modes. Nobody imagines these are four different bones.

### The defaults have names — and so do the parties

Here is the move that makes a great deal of political noise suddenly legible. Each of those national arrangements is a *mode default* — a society's standing decision about which fairness logic governs a given domain. And our political tribes turn out to be, in large part, organised around which default they prefer.

The libertarian is at heart a **Value**-defaulter: let the market govern wherever it possibly can; price is the fairest and freest mechanism; reach for the others only when price truly cannot work. The social democrat is an **Obligated**-defaulter for the things that matter most: some goods are too important for willingness-to-pay, and a legitimate authority should guarantee them. The communitarian is an **Immediate**-defaulter: the good life is lived in thick webs of mutual care, which both the market and the state corrode. The mutualist and the cooperator are **Balanced**-defaulters: not the cold market, not the distant state, but us, pooling and reciprocating among ourselves. Healthcare, housing, education, childcare, pensions, water — every great political fight of our age is, underneath, an argument about which mode should be the default for that domain.

This is why political opponents so reliably talk past each other. They are not, mostly, disagreeing about facts. They are defaulting to different modes and each experiencing the others' defaults as not merely wrong but *unfair* — because a thing that is fair in one mode is often viscerally unfair in another. To the Value-defaulter, the obligated provision of a good looks like theft (you took my money for someone else's care). To the Obligated-defaulter, the Value provision of the same good looks like abandonment (you let the market decide who lives). Neither is being stupid. Each is applying a coherent fairness logic and finding the other's intolerable. Without the vocabulary, this registers as the other side being evil. With it, it registers as a mode-default disagreement — still a real disagreement, but a *negotiable* one, about which logic should govern which corner of life.

### The largest default, and nobody voted for it

Every case so far is a domain a country argues about in the open. Here is one it stopped arguing about — and the stopping was the point.

Within living memory, unemployment was a *monetary* question and a political one. Britain's 1944 White Paper on employment and America's Employment Act of 1946 wrote full employment into law as something governments were for. Devaluation was an ordinary instrument, reached for the way a chancellor reaches for a tax rate. And by the end of the 1950s there appeared to be a menu: a steady trade between how much inflation a country would tolerate and how many of its people would be out of work. If that menu is real, choosing a point on it is precisely what elections are for — and for about a generation, that is partly what they were for.

Then the menu closed. Friedman and Phelps argued at the end of the 1960s that any such trade is temporary: people notice, adjust what they expect, and the unemployment returns with the inflation still attached. The 1970s appeared to prove it in public. A decade later came a further argument, more constitutional than economic — a government that *can* pull the lever will be expected to pull it, and being expected to pull it is itself inflationary, so the only way to be believed is to put the lever out of reach. Rules, not discretion. Take the pen off the politician.

What followed was one of the century's more successful institutional projects. Central banks were made independent, given published targets and printed minutes, and asked to be boring. Inflation across most of the rich world came down and stayed down for a generation — until the burst of the early 2020s, which we look at properly in the next part, and which behaved like weather rather than like an instrument breaking. Then, for one group of countries, it went further than independence: a shared currency removes the lever not from the politician but from the *country*, permanently and by treaty.

Notice first that this is our own recommendation. Everything this book says about who holds the pen — bind them to a rule, publish what they do, make the writer watchable — describes central bank independence rather exactly. We will not pretend otherwise, and we would not want the 1970s back in order to score a point.

But notice what was traded, because we have the words for it. Chapter 4 argued that a good money's supply must be *known*, not *fixed* — that rigidity is a crude and expensive way of buying knowability. The same holds for an adjustment channel. Independence buys credibility by making the response predictable; a currency union buys it by removing the response. And a shock does not stop arriving because the thing that used to absorb it is gone. It gets absorbed somewhere else. When a region inside a shared currency falls behind the average, it can no longer become cheaper by having its money become cheaper — there is no longer a money of its own to move. So it becomes cheaper the other way: wages, then jobs, then the young people leaving. The adjustment happens in bodies instead of prices, and we will watch it happen in a later chapter, because it is the exact mirror of the collapses this book has spent its time describing.

Which brings the mode reading, and it is an uncomfortable one. A default *was* chosen here. Somebody decided which discipline would carry the shock — that it would fall on the labour market rather than the price level, on the region rather than the union, on whoever was least able to move rather than on everyone a little. That is a distributional decision of the first order. And it was made, deliberately, in a form designed to sit beyond the reach of the next election, because sitting beyond the next election was the entire source of its credibility.

Run our fifth question over it. *Who decided that, and could I refuse?* The honest answer is that the decision was excellent by its own lights, that a young person in Andalusia cannot refuse it, and that the second fact is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.

The pen was taken from someone accountable and handed to someone credible. Both are real virtues. They are not the same virtue, and the swap was very rarely described to anyone as a swap.

It was not done identically everywhere, either, and the difference matters. America's central bank still carries employment in its statutory mandate alongside prices; the political question was constrained rather than removed. Europe's has price stability as its primary mandate, with employment secondary. Two answers to one question — and a great deal of what a young person in southern Spain lives through follows from which of them they were born under.

### The book refuses to tell you the answer

You may, at this point, expect us to tell you which mode is right for healthcare, for housing, for the rest. We will not — and the refusal is not coyness, it is the whole design of the framework. It is the difference between a vocabulary and an ideology.

The refusal costs something here, and it should. Most readers hold no strong view about which logic ought to govern education; almost everyone has a view about central bankers. We are not going to tell you whether the trade was worth it. The framework has no opinion on whether credibility is worth more than accountability — that is a moral and political judgement belonging to the people who live with the answer, and most of all to the ones in the regions where the adjustment lands. What the framework insists on is narrower and harder to dismiss: that it *was* a trade, that it was distributional, and that describing it as a technical improvement rather than a choice about who absorbs shocks is the part that should not pass unnoticed.

The framework's job is to make the question *visible*, not to answer it. *Which mode should govern this domain* is a political and moral question, and it belongs to citizens, not to a book about money. What the framework offers is not a programme but a clarity: the ability to see that a fight over healthcare funding is a fight about mode defaults, to name which default each side is reaching for, and to argue about *that* — explicitly, in shared words — instead of shouting past each other in the dark. A libertarian and a socialist can both use this book. They will still disagree, possibly violently, about which mode belongs where. But they will be having the *same* argument, in the same language, which is already an enormous gain over the mutual incomprehension that passes for political debate now.

Descriptive, not prescriptive. The framework hands you the question, sharpened, and steps back. That self-restraint is the point — and we will perform it once more, deliberately, in the very last chapter.

When a society's mode defaults hold, the system is, for most people, invisible — it simply works, and they never have to think about which logic governs what. When the defaults *break* — when the currency everyone trusted stops being trustworthy, when the institutions meant to guarantee a thing fail — the invisibility ends, the modes come apart, and people discover, often brutally, what their money was actually made of. That breakage is the subject of Part VI.
