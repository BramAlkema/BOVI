# Chapter 17: The Politics of Mode Defaults

> *Most political fights are over which mode should dominate which domain.*

**Part V — When Modes Collide** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 16](16-adversarial-mode-forcing.md) · Next: [Chapter 18](18-inflation-as-broken-common-knowledge.md)

## Opening scaffold

Healthcare across countries, side by side. Same need, four different mode defaults — US (Value), UK (Obligated), large parts of Sub-Saharan Africa (Immediate, family-based), parts of Northern Europe (Balanced, social-insurance-mutualist).

## Structural beats

1. The healthcare table.
2. Libertarian = Value-default; social democrat = Obligated-default.
3. Communitarian = Immediate-default; mutualist = Balanced-default.
4. The framework does not resolve these fights; it makes them legible.
5. Why political opponents talk past each other (arguing about defaults without naming them).
6. Housing, education, family policy, retirement — all mode-default fights.
7. Once you can mode-tag a political position, you can have a different conversation.

## Themes

- Most political incoherence is mode-confusion.
- The framework is non-partisan precisely because it does not pick a side.
- Vocabulary creates the possibility of debate where shouting was happening.

## Twists

- The reader expects to be told which mode the book endorses for healthcare/education/etc.
- The book refuses. The framework's job is to make the question visible, not to answer it.

## Subarcs

- The "framework as descriptive vocabulary, not ideology" subarc reaches its peak here.
- Sets up Part VI's macro chapters, where mode-default *failures* (currency collapse, banking crisis) get analysed.
- Final chapter before the macro turn.

## Closing scaffold

When mode defaults break, the system breaks. Part VI examines what breakage looks like.
