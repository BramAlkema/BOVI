## Draft

Let us run the bridal party fight one more time, but slowly, with the sound off, naming each move as it happens.

The bride's sister posts the spreadsheet — six-way split, tidy total. *Balanced mode:* we are peers, we are even. The teacher reads it and feels her stomach drop. She is also in Balanced mode, but checking a different ledger — capacity, not headcount — and by her ledger this is not even at all. The bride's mother, watching the temperature climb, offers to pay for the villa. *Immediate mode:* this is family, we do not count. Her offer lands like a slap, because to accept it is to agree the weekend is a gift, and half the group is not willing to be given to. One friend suggests phoning the resort to negotiate. *Value mode:* it's a transaction, get the price down. And underneath it all sits the non-refundable deposit with someone's name on it — *Obligated mode*, an authority that does not care how anyone feels and is counting down to a deadline.

Six women, four modes, one weekend, and not a single villain. Watch what the slow replay reveals: nobody in this fight is arguing about the *amount.* They cannot be, because they have not agreed what *kind of thing* the amount is for. You cannot settle a price until you know whether you are pricing, sharing, squaring up, or complying. The fight feels like it is about money and is actually about mode — which is why it cannot be won by anyone clever with a calculator.

### Different bases, guaranteed collision

This is the general shape of most everyday money trouble, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. When two people bring different modes to the same exchange, a fight is guaranteed *regardless of the numbers* — because each is computing fairness on a different basis, and there is no amount that is simultaneously fair-as-a-price, fair-as-a-ledger, and fair-as-a-gift. (If you want one image for it, and only one: it is as though the exchange sits in a superposition of all four modes until someone forces it to collapse into a single one — and the fight is over which one it collapses into. We will not push the metaphor past this sentence; it earns its keep and then becomes a liability.)

The everyday cases are endless once you have the eye. The end of a group dinner, when the table divides into those who want to split evenly (Balanced), those who want to pay for exactly what they ordered (Value), and the one who quietly wants to get the whole thing (Immediate) — and someone always leaves faintly annoyed. The housemate silently tallying whose turn it was to buy the washing-up liquid (a Balanced ledger nobody agreed to keep). The moment a parent hands you money and you cannot tell, and dare not ask, whether it is a gift (Immediate), a loan (Balanced), or a lever (Obligated) — and the not-knowing is the whole discomfort. None of these is about the sum. Each is a mode one person assumed and another did not.

### The cure is the naming

Here is the quietly remarkable thing, and the reason this chapter sits where it does. The cure for a mode collision is almost never to argue the amount harder. It is to name the mode out loud. *Is this dinner a split-evenly thing or a pay-for-what-you-had thing?* — asked at the start, that question prevents the entire fight. *Mum, is this a gift or a loan? I just want to know which.* — asked plainly, it dissolves the dread. The naming works because the conflict was never in the numbers; it was in the unspoken, mismatched assumption about what kind of exchange this was. Surface the assumption and the parties can negotiate it. Leave it buried and they will fight about everything except it.

And now notice something about yourself. We never sat you down and taught you to do this. We told you four scenes, gave you four words, and let the words work in the background. But you can feel the difference: a group-dinner standoff, a passive-aggressive housemate note, an awkward transfer from a relative — you can now mode-tag them almost without trying. You have been trained without being taught, which is the only kind of training that survives the book being closed. The defensive vocabulary the final chapter will hand you formally is one you have, in fact, already half-built.

But there is a difference between the collisions in this chapter and those in the next. Everything here was between consenting adults muddling toward an answer — a teacher and her friends, a child and a parent, housemates and a bottle of soap. Nobody was *forcing* a mode on anyone. In the next chapter the collision is no longer accidental and no longer symmetric. It is imposed, deliberately, by systems built to make you accept a mode you did not choose — and at that point the small private confusion grows a politics.
