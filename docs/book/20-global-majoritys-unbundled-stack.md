## Draft insert — Stable Money as Privileged Compression

By now it is tempting to say that Doña Elena has an unusually complicated financial life. She has pesos for taxes and groceries, dollars for rent, USDT on her phone, a loan to a friend's furniture business, real estate, family obligations, a cueva rate in her head, and Sunday lunch that refuses to become an account. A Berlin reader can look at this stack and see inconvenience. Too many instruments. Too many conversions. Too much attention spent on money.

But that reaction gives away the privilege.

Stable money is an achievement. That matters. People who live without it know exactly how much labour, anxiety, and social damage unstable money creates. But an achievement is not an ontology, and it is not costless. Stable money should receive respect as infrastructure. It should not receive reverence as truth. It is not the natural form of money, and it is not evidence that money is one thing. It is money good enough to make its own machinery disappear. When one currency works well enough for daily transactions, short-term saving, price memory, contracts, salaries, taxes, and subscriptions, we stop seeing the separate jobs being performed. The bundle compresses reality for us. We learn that coffee costs about four, rent about twelve hundred, a phone bill about twenty-five a month. We keep a cash buffer because it prevents failed payments and saves attention. We sign monthly contracts because the unit feels stable enough to carry promises forward. We use the current account as a dashboard for whether life is affordable this week.

None of this is foolish. It is an efficient compression system. Stable money lowers cognitive cost. It lets ordinary people rote-learn prices, hold transaction inventory, and move through the day without running a private treasury desk between breakfast and lunch.

The problem is that every comfort has a bill attached.

Stable money charges in attention that no longer gets paid. It charges in habits that become invisible. It charges in institutions we stop inspecting because they work most of the time. It charges in long-term contracts denominated in units we no longer question. It charges in subscriptions that turn future claims into small monthly numbers. It charges in official indices that replace our own baskets. It charges in banking defaults, fees, spreads, renewals, and cancellation friction that hide inside smooth payment rails. And, increasingly, it charges in legibility: the more convenient the system becomes, the easier it is for banks, platforms, states, and merchants to see and route us.

This is how the cost arrives at user scale. The price map becomes so easy to memorise that we mistake nominal memory for real value. The current-account balance becomes an affordability oracle. A subscription stops looking like a future obligation and starts looking like a tiny monthly price. Inflation becomes something announced by an index rather than measured in our own basket. The bundle works well enough that we forget it is a bundle.

That forgetting is not evenly distributed across the world. It is a stable-currency privilege.

Where money is volatile, managed, or politically manipulated, people learn the stack because survival requires it. The local currency may still work for bread, buses, and taxes. It may not work for rent, savings, or long-term promises. So the functions separate in daily life. Transaction money, savings assets, contract units, price discovery, reciprocal credit, and family care move into different instruments. This is painful. It is not romantic. It costs attention, dignity, and time. But it also reveals something that stable money conceals: money was never one thing.

Doña Elena does not need a theory of unbundling to know this. She knows it because a peso held too long teaches the lesson. A landlord asking for dollars teaches the lesson. A shopkeeper quoting one price for cash and another for card teaches the lesson. A state pretending the official exchange rate is the real one teaches the lesson. The stack is visible because the local bundle will not hold.

The Berlin barista, by contrast, lives inside a monetary environment that still compresses these distinctions for her. That is a real material advantage. It is also an epistemic disadvantage. She can mistake convenience for truth. She can believe that people juggling currencies, assets, obligations, and informal networks are outside the financial system, when they may simply be seeing the system more clearly than she does.

This is why the phrase "underbanked" misleads. It describes people by their distance from our preferred institutions, not by the coherence of their own arrangements. A Kenyan with M-Pesa, cattle, a rotating savings club, and family credit is not necessarily missing a bank account in the way the textbook imagines. An Argentinian with pesos for tax, dollars for rent, USDT for savings, and a cueva for price discovery is not waiting to be taught what money is. They are already operating a decomposed stack.

This is also where the goldbug critique has to be handled carefully. Goldbugs often see the first half correctly. They see that fiat stability is political, not natural. They see that official units can conceal debasement. They see that long-term contracts need an anchor, and that price memory can become a kind of propaganda when the unit changes underneath it. They are right to refuse reverence for stable fiat.

But then they give the reverence to something else.

Gold is not monetary truth. It is another compression technology. It compresses trust into a natural anchor: hard to mine, hard to fake, hard for a finance minister to print before an election. That constraint is real, and it has value. It is also not free. Hard money can charge through deflation, austerity, credit contraction, colonial extraction, and the political violence required to defend convertibility when social needs exceed the metal constraint. The gold standard did not remove politics from money. It relocated politics into the defence of the anchor.

So the error is symmetrical. Stable-fiat users mistake convenience for truth. Goldbugs mistake constraint for truth. One worships smoothness; the other worships hardness. Both are still worshipping a bundle.

The question is not whether bundling is good or bad. Bundling is compression. In stable systems, compression saves attention. In volatile systems, the bundle decomposes and users must separate functions manually. In platform systems, firms often rebundle future obligations into small nominal prices, exploiting the habits that stable money created.

The real question is who controls the bundle, what it hides, and whether we can decompose it when we need to.

That is the privilege check at the centre of this chapter. Stable money gives us comfort. It also trains us to confuse that comfort with the nature of money itself. The global majority's unbundled stack is not a backward version of our system. It is the thing our system lets us forget. Stable money is an achievement. It is not an ontology.
