## Draft

Three weeks before the wedding, six women are at war over a spreadsheet.

The bride's sister made it — a tidy grid of the bachelorette weekend, names down the side, costs across the top, a formula in the corner totalling what each one owes. By any reasonable standard it is fair. It splits the villa, the dinners, the spa morning, the bottomless brunch, straight down the middle, six ways. And it has detonated the group chat.

One friend thinks the equal split is obviously right: everyone came, everyone shares, that is what fair *means*. Another is quietly furious, because she is a teacher and two of the others are lawyers, and an equal split is not equal at all when one of you is counting the weekend in rent. The bride's mother has offered, twice, to simply pay for the villa herself — and cannot understand why this generous offer keeps making things *worse*. One friend wants to phone the resort and haggle the package down, which strikes half the group as mortifying and the other half as the only sane move in the thread. And the bride, who started all this, now privately wishes she had eloped, because the one thing she wanted — a nice weekend with the people she loves — has become an accountancy dispute with feelings attached.

Sit with this fight a moment before we explain it, because the confusion is the point. Everyone in it is reasoning correctly. Nobody is being stupid, or selfish, or passive-aggressive — though all of them will be accused of all three before the weekend is out. What looks like a fight about *money* is not about money at all. The numbers are a proxy. What they are actually fighting about is *what kind of thing this weekend is* — and they are fighting because each of them has, without noticing, answered that question differently.

This is the moment the book has been waiting for, because we are about to be handed the words we have been missing. Once we have them, we will not be able to watch this fight — or a hundred others like it — the same way again.

### Four answers, all at once

In the early 1990s an anthropologist named Alan Fiske published the conclusion of years of fieldwork — much of it in a village in Burkina Faso, watching how people there structured their obligations to one another — with a claim large enough to be suspicious. *Every* human relationship, in every society anyone had studied, is built out of just four elementary patterns. Four ways of being fair. Not four cultures, or four personality types — four *grammars*, available to everyone, switched between constantly, often several times in a single conversation.

He gave them careful academic names. We will use plainer ones, and a one-line tell for each — the question the mode is silently asking.

| Mode | The question it asks | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Value** | *"Is the price right?"* | A spot trade between equals who owe each other nothing afterwards. The market. |
| **Balanced** | *"Are we even?"* | Reciprocity, kept track of over time. Favours, turns, debts that should square up. |
| **Obligated** | *"Who decides — and is that legitimate?"* | Authority. One party ranks above another and the exchange follows the rank. |
| **Immediate** | *"Are we okay?"* | Sharing without counting. Gift, care, the pooled life of people who do not keep score. |

One regularity is worth flagging before we go on, because it will quietly turn out to be the hinge of the whole book. The four modes *count* in four different ways. Immediate does not count at all. Obligated counts rank — who stands above whom. Balanced counts one-for-one — turns and favours that should match. Value counts in ratios — prices, proportions, anything divided by anything. Fiske noticed this himself: his four patterns are, in order, the four classic scales of measurement — nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. Four modes, four kinds of arithmetic. Two chapters from now, that observation will join the modes to money at the root.

Now go back to the spreadsheet, and the fight decodes instantly.

The friend who thinks the equal split is obviously fair is in **Balanced** mode: *we are peers, we are even, six ways down the middle is what evenness looks like.* The furious teacher is *also* in Balanced mode — she just thinks the ledger is being kept wrong, because a split that ignores capacity is not balance, it is balance's costume. The bride's mother, offering to pay, is in **Immediate** mode: *this is family, this is love, we do not count among people we love* — which is exactly why her offer keeps making things worse, because to accept it is to agree that the weekend is the kind of thing a mother provides, and some of the women are not ready to be provided for. The friend who wants to haggle is in **Value** mode: *it's a transaction; get the price right.* And hanging over all of it is **Obligated** mode — the non-refundable deposit, the deadline, the booking with someone's name on it: the authority of a contract that does not care how anyone feels.

Four modes, asserted at once, by people who love each other, about a single weekend. No villains. Just four right answers to a question nobody realised they were asking: *what kind of exchange is this?*

### Not money — everything

Here is the part that should unsettle you a little. We opened with a money fight because money is where this is easiest to see and where the literature is deepest. But the four modes are not a theory of money. They are a theory of *exchange*, and exchange is most of social life. Once you have the four words, you will start finding them in places that have nothing to do with a spreadsheet.

Take parking. A metered space downtown is pure **Value** — you pay the posted rate, the city owes you nothing but the time. A disabled bay is **Obligated** — an authority has ruled the space reserved, and your fitness to use it is not a matter of price. The unspoken law of the street, where you do not take the spot your neighbour dug out of the snow, is **Balanced** — reciprocity with a long memory and no contract. And the friend who says *just put it in my driveway, don't be silly* is in **Immediate** — the space is shared, the offer is not invoiced. One mundane act; four modes; the difference is entirely *whose* space it is and what you are to them.

Or healthcare, which we will come back to when the politics turn serious, but which already shows all four. The same broken arm is a **Value** transaction in one country (a bill, itemised), an **Obligated** entitlement in another (the state ranks above the market and rules that you are treated), a **Balanced** mutual-insurance pool in a third (we all pay in, we all draw out, even over a lifetime), and, across much of the world, an **Immediate** matter for your family, who simply find the money because you are theirs. Nobody imagines these are four different *injuries*. They are four different *modes* laid over one need.

Even intimacy — to be brief and careful — runs on all four, and most of the unhappiness around it comes from two people quietly choosing different modes and not saying so: one in Immediate, one keeping a Balanced ledger. And work, where *"we're a family here"* is an Immediate-mode banner flown over what is, on payday, unmistakably a Value-mode arrangement — a tension we will spend a whole later chapter taking apart, because it is one of the most lucrative confusions of our age.

The pattern holds wherever we look. The modes are not a feature of money. Money is just the corner of human life where someone finally bothered to write them down.

### Why this is not a chapter about irrationality

Standard economics has a word for the bridesmaid who will not accept the equal split: *irrational*. It has the same word for the mother whose generous offer backfires, and a whole flourishing subfield cataloguing the ways real humans depart from the cool calculating agent the models assume.

We want to propose something gentler, and we think more accurate. None of these people is malfunctioning. Each is running a perfectly coherent mode; the only error — shared, with no author — is that they have not noticed they are running *different* ones. What economics files under "irrational" is very often just a mode the model cannot see, because the model has eyes for only one of the four. The teacher is not bad at maths. She is doing Balanced-mode fairness correctly and discovering that her friends are not. That is not a cognitive failure. It is a coordination failure, and it has a cure, and the cure is a vocabulary.

Which, in the end, is all this chapter has handed you: four words, and the suggestion that most exchange is one of them. It does not feel like much. But watch what it does. Before the words, the bridal party fight is a swamp of hurt feelings and unfair maths. After the words, it is legible — you can point at each speaker, name her mode, and ask the one question that actually dissolves the fight: *which mode is this weekend supposed to be?* That question has an answer the group can negotiate. *Who's being unreasonable?* does not.

We will meet this bridal party again — three times more, each with sharper tools — and by the last time we will know exactly what would have helped them. For now it is enough to have watched them fight, and to hold the four words that explain why.

We have the modes. But the modes describe the *shape* of an exchange, not whether it is *right*. A gift and an extortion can wear the same mode; an authority can be one you chose or one you cannot leave. Shape is not legitimacy. The next chapter adds the second axis — the one that tells a wanted obligation from an imposed one — and with it the framework is complete enough to turn loose on the world.
