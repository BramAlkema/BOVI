## Draft

In 1776 Adam Smith walked into a pin factory and counted.

A single workman, he reported — untrained, doing every step himself, drawing the wire, straightening it, cutting it, pointing it, grinding the head — could make perhaps one pin in a day. Certainly not twenty. But he had seen a small factory of ten people, each performing only one of the eighteen distinct operations, and those ten made upward of forty-eight thousand pins in a day. Four thousand eight hundred each.

Sit with the size of that number, because it is the number this whole part of the book is about. The same ten people, with the same hands, the same hours, the same iron, became something like two hundred times more productive — not by working harder, not by discovering a machine, but by *arranging their work differently.* The gain was not inside any pin. It was in the pattern.

This is the engine of nearly all prosperity, and it is almost embarrassingly under-celebrated, because it does not feel like wealth. It feels like organisation. But specialisation — each person doing the thing their hour is worth most at, and trading for the rest — is where the overwhelming majority of human living standards actually come from. Not from working longer, and not, mostly, from better tools, but from the deepening division of labour that better coordination allows.

### Specialisation needs an extent

Smith named the limit in the same breath he named the gain: *the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market.* You cannot specialise into making only pins unless there is a market large enough and reliable enough to sell forty-eight thousand pins into and buy your dinner back. A pin-maker in a village of twelve starves. A pin-maker in a city of a million grows rich. The deeper you wish to specialise, the larger and more frictionless the web of exchange you need beneath you — which is exactly what money infrastructure provides. Money does not make the pins. It makes the *market* into which a pin-specialist can dare to exist.

And the size of that effect is enormous — large enough to read off the map. Across cultures and centuries, the density of monetised exchange tracks the density of people: cities monetise, thin populations stay nearer subsistence, because specialisation needs a crowd to sell into. The cleanest natural experiment we were ever handed is the Korean peninsula — one people, one language, one history, split in 1945 and handed two different coordination systems. Seventy years later the South's output per person is something like twenty or thirty times the North's. Same hands. Same daylight. The difference is almost entirely in how the work is allowed to be arranged and exchanged.

Run the gap the other way and you can put a number on what breaks when money breaks. A hyperinflation does not merely raise prices; it dissolves the extent of the market — contracts cannot be written, specialists cannot trust they will be paid, people retreat toward baking their own bad bread — and output falls by tens of per cent in a single year. We will watch this in detail later. For now, hold the order of magnitude: the distance between a society with no usable monetary coordination and one with good monetary coordination is something like two orders of magnitude in productivity. A hundredfold. That is the prize.

### The prize is coordination, not production

Here is the twist the chapter has been walking toward, and it reframes what economics is even about. We are trained to picture wealth as *stuff produced* — factories, harvests, output. But the pin factory tells a different story. The ten workers did not produce more pin-metal. They coordinated their existing labour better. North and South Korea do not differ in how hard anyone works. They differ in how the work is allowed to be arranged.

Most of the gap between rich and poor societies, in other words, is not a production gap. It is a *coordination* gap — and money is the master technology of coordination. Which means the real subject of economics is not the optimisation of supply and demand around a price. It is the management of the cost of getting people to work together at all. Before we put a name and a number to that cost, though, we should feel it from the inside — in a village, ten thousand years ago, with four people and a length of rope. That is the intermezzo that comes next.
