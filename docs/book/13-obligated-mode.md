# Chapter 13: Obligated Mode

> *"Because they said so." The mode of authority, hierarchy, and rule-imposed exchange — voluntary or coerced.*

**Part IV — The Four Modes Up Close** · [Index](README.md) · Prev: [Chapter 12](12-balanced-mode.md) · Next: [Chapter 14](14-immediate-mode.md)

## Opening scaffold

A *cueva* above a Buenos Aires clothing store. A nondescript door, a back room, a man with a cash drawer. Everyone changes their dollars at the rate the state pretends does not exist.

## Structural beats

1. The cueva scene.
2. Definition of Obligated mode.
3. Voluntary versions (BDSM, monastic vows, gym discipline, military enlistment, apprenticeships).
4. Coerced versions (tax, draft, rent under broken markets, arranged marriage).
4b. **The hut tax — Obligated money at the scale of a region.** A tax levied per dwelling, payable only in the coloniser's currency, and that currency obtainable in practice only by selling your labour to a mine or a settler farm. Nobody had to be marched anywhere; the obligation to clear did the marching. The result was a migrant-labour system whose settlement patterns outlived the tax that produced them. Two things to draw and no more: it is the book's own thesis about demand for a record — *the obligation to clear creates the demand for the unit, not anything in the unit itself* — running at the scale of a social formation; and it is the clearest Obligated money the book will handle, because the authority was explicit and the exit was not there. **Care:** standard scholarship, not a provocation. Write it as mechanism; the mechanism is damning without help. And state the honest limit — the tax worked *alongside* land alienation and movement controls, and historians weigh those differently; the claim here is about the mechanism's shape, not a sole cause.
5. The tell: *"is the authority legitimate?"*
6. The trap — penalties dressed as prices (overdraft fees, late fees, cancellation fees, "convenience charges").
7. Invented authority (subscription dark patterns, fake-family workplaces).
8. Defence — *"who decided this and on what authority?"*

## Themes

- Authority can be wanted.
- Legitimacy is not in the structure but in the consent and exit conditions.
- "Real" markets often run on hidden Obligated impositions — the cueva is a freer market than the official one.

## Twists

- Big one: the Argentine state's *official* market is the more coercive one.
- The cueva, which a Western reader might initially see as illicit, is the *less* coerced exchange.
- The state's "legality" and the cueva's "illegality" do not map onto consent.

## Draft

Above a clothing shop in Buenos Aires there is a nondescript door, and behind it a small room with a man and a cash drawer. This is a *cueva* — a cave — and it is where the city actually changes its money. You hand over dollars; he hands back pesos at the *blue* rate, the real market rate, the one the state insists does not exist. Everyone uses the cueva. By the official law it is illegal. It is also, as we are about to see, the *freer* of the two markets available to you — and that fact is going to teach us something uncomfortable about authority.

### The mode of rank

Obligated mode is exchange organised around authority. One party ranks above another, and the flow follows the rank: the superior decides, the subordinate complies. Its tell is not *is the price right* or *are we even* but *who decided this, and is their authority legitimate?* It is the mode of the state and the citizen, the boss and the worker, the officer and the recruit, the parent and the child — every exchange where someone has the standing to impose terms and someone else is expected to accept them.

The reflex is to file Obligated mode under *coercion*, and therefore *bad.* Resist it. Chapter 3 made that case at length and it holds here: people choose to be ranked below another constantly — the novice, the apprentice, the gym member on the weak morning — because the ranking buys something equality cannot. What turns the mode sinister is never the rank. It is the loss of the two things on the consent axis: did you choose it, and can you leave? The dojo you can walk out of and the conscription you cannot are the *same mode*, and everything moral about the difference lives outside the hierarchy.

### Penalties dressed as prices

The characteristic abuse of Obligated mode is disguise — the smuggling of an authoritarian imposition into the clothes of a Value-mode price. Watch for the word *fee.* An overdraft fee, a late fee, a cancellation fee, a "convenience" fee — these present themselves as prices, as though you were buying something. You are not. You are being *fined.* A penalty is an exercise of authority: the bank, the platform, the airline has ranked itself above you and is punishing a behaviour. Calling it a price disguises the authority and removes the question you would otherwise ask — *who gave you the standing to fine me, and did I agree to it?* Subscriptions that bury their cancellation in a maze do the same thing: manufacturing an authority over your time and money you never knowingly granted. So does the workplace that calls itself a family and then issues family-shaped commands. Invented authority, everywhere, hiding behind the friendlier modes.

### The cueva's lesson

Which brings us back to the cave, and to the inversion promised at the top. The state's *official* market enforces a rate nobody believes, by an authority nobody consented to, with no exit — pure coerced Obligated mode wearing the costume of law. The cueva's rate is simply what the real market clears at, agreed between two parties who both chose to be there and can both walk away. By the two tests we have — consent and exit — the *illegal* exchange is the freer one. The state's "legality" and the cueva's "illegality" do not map onto coercion at all. Legality is a claim about authority; it tells you who is permitted, not who consented. The cueva is where Argentines go to escape an authority they never agreed to, into an exchange they did.

One caution, and it is the kind this book should always add to a sentence that pleasing. The cueva is freer *for whoever can reach it* — which is to say, for whoever already has dollars. The person holding only pesos, with none coming in and no cousin abroad, gets the official rate and the queue. Consent and exit are the right tests and they are answered person by person, never market by market. The freer exchange for Doña Elena is not the same claim as a freer economy, and we are not making the second.

### And when there is no cave

The cueva is a door. That is its whole lesson — one exists, and the people who need it know where. Now hold everything else the same, the authority and the obligation denominated in somebody else's unit and the absence of anyone's consent, and take the door away. Across colonial southern and eastern Africa, administrations levied a tax on each dwelling — a hut tax, a poll tax, a head tax — payable not in grain, not in cattle, not in work on the land a family already farmed, but in the coloniser's currency. And that currency was, for most rural households, obtainable in essentially one way: by sending a man to earn wages at a mine or on a settler farm. Nobody had to be marched anywhere. The obligation to clear did the marching.

A tax denominated in a unit you can obtain only by selling your labour to a particular buyer is not a revenue measure that happens to have a labour effect; the labour *is* the mechanism. What followed was a migrant-labour system, households split across long distances for most of the year, and settlement patterns that outlived by generations the taxes that produced them. It did not act alone — land alienation and movement controls did much of the work, and historians weigh them differently — but the shape is unmistakable, and it is the clearest instance this book will meet. Chapter 6 named state coercion as one of the four engines that solve a money's cold start. This is that engine seen from underneath, by the people it was run on: the demand for the record came from the obligation to clear and from nothing inside the unit itself. The consent axis reads the rest instantly. The mode was Obligated, the authority was real, and there was no exit.

Nor is the mechanism a relic. Any obligation denominated in a unit obtainable from only one counterparty has this structure, which is why the company town and the company store belong in the same paragraph as the hut tax — and why the question to ask of any such arrangement is the one the cueva has already taught us. Not whether it is legal. Whether there is another door.

### The defence

The defence in Obligated mode is a single question, asked plainly: *who decided this, and on what authority?* Most illegitimate obligation survives by not being asked that — by passing itself off as a price, a norm, a law, a family expectation, anything but what it is. Naming the authority does not always let you escape it. But it lets you see it, and distinguish the authority you chose (the trainer, the vows, the cueva) from the one merely imposed (the fine, the official rate, the dark pattern) — which is the whole of the moral question.

Obligated mode has an authority that decides and a subordinate who complies. The fourth and final mode has the opposite of all of it: no authority, no ledger, no price, no count.
