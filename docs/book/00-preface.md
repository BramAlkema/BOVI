# Preface

**Part 0 — Front matter** · [Index](README.md) · Next: [Chapter 1](01-two-coffees.md)

Value is not a property of a thing. It is a relation between a person and a possibility — what this, to me, now, is worth against what else I might do instead. Every economics textbook I have read says so, plainly and early, in the chapter about demand. Two people can want the same object differently, and neither is making an error.

Then the money chapter arrives and the grammar quietly changes. Value becomes something an object holds, that money measures, that a vault stores, that backing or law puts into a note. Nobody announces the change, because nobody is defending it — it is not a doctrine anyone would sign; it is a habit of speech that has survived inside a discipline whose own theory contradicts it. And the moment you look for the evidence that would settle it, the habit fails: a tally of marks on a rope, worth nothing at all as an object, does the work of money for a village that never mined anything. The thing has no value in it. The arrangement does everything.

That mismatch never sat well with me. For a long time I assumed I had misread something. The more interesting possibility — the one this book grew out of — is that the teaching and the theory have come apart at one specific joint, and that the joint is money.

## Held at its actual strength

That possibility can be tested rather than asserted, and testing it is one of the projects standing behind this book. The claim has levels. The weakest is lexical: introductory texts use object-like phrasing about value — easy to demonstrate, and it proves little on its own, since language is loose everywhere. The middle level is pedagogical: money chapters offer students unqualified object-like propositions about value, not as asides but as the account. The strongest and most interesting version is the *switch* — that a single book teaches preference-dependent valuation in its demand chapter and object-like value in its money chapter, without ever marking the transition.

As I write, the middle level is supported by a small pilot and the strongest one is not yet established. I say so here because a book that will repeatedly ask you to hold claims at their real strength should be seen doing it first, on its own foundational claim, on page one.

## The question is practical

The question, then, is not *is the textbook wrong.* It is: **can the introductory account of money be taught without the switch?**

That is a harder demand than criticism, and a fairer one. A replacement has to do everything the original does, for someone who has not yet been given a reason to care: explain what money is, why it is worth anything, why *this* money and not another, where new money comes from, why it sometimes fails, and what a person can do when it does. An account that is philosophically tidier and pedagogically useless has not won anything. So the standard I have tried to hold is efficacy, not novelty.

Which brings me to the least fashionable thing I can say about this book. Almost nothing in it is new. The pieces were assembled by people who are named throughout and thanked properly at the end; the value theory, the anthropology, the ledger, the modes, the mechanisms all belong to somebody else. "This has been done before" is not an objection I need to survive — it is the strongest thing going for the account, because a picture of money assembled entirely from load-bearing work that the field already accepts is much harder to dismiss than one that needs a new theorem. The contribution I claim is the assembly and the teaching. That is all, and it is enough to be worth a book.

## Two kinds of rigour, and they are not the same

Something has changed recently that makes this more than an argument about wording, and it is worth being exact about what.

The first change is **constructive**. It has become practical to write an economic mechanism as an executable contract, and doing so imposes a discipline that prose cannot: you cannot implement a step you are able only to gesture at. Every trusted noun has to become a named act — who may write, who decides a dispute, who says what the world is, who counts as a member. Build the whole account that way and the parts that *refuse* to become acts are left standing in plain view. That is not a decorative result. It is where the most honest section of this book came from: not designed, but discovered as the residue, once everything that could be made mechanical had been.

Economics reached this ground about a decade ago, from three directions and in that order. In **2016** Catalini and Gans reduced the whole technology to two costs — the cost of verification and the cost of networking — and made it something economics could hold in its hand. In **2018** Holden and Malani set it against Hart and Moore's theory of incomplete contracts and asked the sharper question: that theory rests on the premise that contingencies cannot be cheaply specified and verified, so what survives if a technology relaxes it? (Hart was unconvinced. The question stands anyway.) And from **2020**, with Roughgarden's analysis of Ethereum's fee mechanism, mechanism design acquired something it had almost never had — a mechanism running live, with real money in it, whose behaviour obliged the theorists to write new definitions of incentive compatibility, because the inherited ones did not fit the case.

I should be exact about what that licenses. Economists have been *studying* these systems, and in places have found them putting weight on an assumption the field had been quietly resting on. They have not adopted executable contracts as a general method for making their own premises explicit, and this book is not reporting a practice the profession has settled into. The claim here is narrower: the tool exists, the discipline it imposes is real, and turning it on an account of money is informative — most of all where it fails.

The second change is **empirical**, and it is a different thing entirely. Whether textbooks make the switch is a question about a corpus, and can be coded. Whether a person's beliefs about what money *is* change how they behave, and what those beliefs cost them, is a question about people, and can be run. Both are underway; neither is finished.

What a contract is *not* is a test of a theory of value. It cannot tell you that money is a relation rather than a substance. It can tell you that an account of money as a relation is complete enough to execute, and that the rival account, asked to do the same, has to keep borrowing what it claims to have supplied. That is a constructive existence proof and a completeness audit. It is genuinely useful and it is not the same as evidence, and running the two together is the first mistake I want to avoid making in front of you.

## Where it actually starts

None of the above is where the book begins. Arguments about ontology persuade almost nobody, and they are a poor way in.

It begins instead with two cups of coffee — one bought in Berlin without a thought, one bought in Buenos Aires by a woman for whom nothing about money is thoughtless — and with the observation that the second is not the broken case. From here on the voice is *we*, because from here on we are looking at the same thing.
